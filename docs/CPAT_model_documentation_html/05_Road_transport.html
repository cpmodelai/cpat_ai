5  Road transport

Road transport is the largest user of oil products in most countries, and the place where carbon pricing meets a slow-moving capital stock: cars, trucks and motorcycles last ten or twenty years, and the choice between a petrol car and an electric one is made once, at purchase. The model offers two ways to project road fuel use.

  • The elasticity model (the default). Road gasoline, diesel, LPG and natural gas follow the same constant-elasticity fuel-use equation as every other sector and fuel (M3.1); it is described in Chapter 3.
  • The engineer road model (switched on per scenario with TrModel). It projects the vehicle fleet itself: how many vehicles people own, how many they buy and scrap, whether they buy electric, how efficient the new vehicles are and how far they are driven. Fuel use follows from vehicles, distance and fuel economy.

This chapter describes the engineer model: what it is (Section 5.1), its data (Section 5.3), how it computes one year (Section 5.4 to Section 5.14), its caveats (Section 5.15), and how it compares with legacy CPAT (Section 5.16). The implementation in Excel-AI and CPAT Python closes the chapter (Section 5.17).

The CPAT methodology describes the elasticity approach in section 3.3.3; it has no chapter on the engineer road model, whose source is the legacy CPAT workbook and its methodology note (Transportation_methodology.docx). The methodology’s road transport module (chapter 7) is related but different: it projects vehicle-kilometres, congestion, accidents and road damage from fuel prices with estimated elasticities (section 7.3.1, section 7.3.8), for the co-benefits of the welfare results (Chapter 7). The engineer model sends its road fuel use to that module (Section 5.14).

The switch. TrModel on ScenarioDefinition (one value per scenario, default Elasticity). The flag

\[ sw^{T} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{TrModel starts with "Engineer"} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.1}\]

decides, in every year, which fuel use the rest of the model reads (M3.2):

\[ F_{rod,f,t} = \begin{cases} F^{T}_{f,t} & sw^T = 1 \text{ and } f \in \{\text{gasoline, diesel, LPG, natural gas, biofuels}\} \\ F^{e}_{rod,f,t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.2}\]

where

  • \(F^{T}_{f,t}\) is road fuel use of the engineer model (ktoe, Equation 5.41 and Equation 5.44), trkt, trbio;
  • \(F^{e}_{rod,f,t}\) is road fuel use of the elasticity model (ktoe), enel (biofuels: trbe);
  • \(F_{rod,f,t}\) is the road fuel use used everywhere else, ener.

Road electricity enters power demand (M4.1, Chapter 4) the same way:

\[ E_{rod,t} = \begin{cases} E^{T}_{t} & sw^T = 1,\ t > t_0 \\ E_{rod,t-1}\, m_{tra,t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.3}\]

where \(E^{T}_{t}\) is road electricity of the engineer model (ktoe, trkte, Equation 5.42), \(m_{tra,t}\) the growth multiple of the transport demand group in the power model, and \(t_0\) the base year (2022). In the base year road electricity is the data under both models.

With the engineer model on, CO2, methane, local air pollutants, revenues and all totals follow the engineer road fuel use, because they are computed from \(F_{rod,f,t}\). With the default, every result is exactly the elasticity model’s.

What the engineer model covers. Road gasoline, diesel, LPG, natural gas (CNG and LNG) and electricity, and the road biofuels, which follow gasoline and diesel (Equation 5.44). Coal, kerosene and other oil products in road, and rail, aviation and navigation, stay on the elasticity model whatever the switch says.

No circular reference. The engineer model reads last year’s power price, last year’s GDP growth and this year’s GDP level after the GDP feedback (which uses last year’s effect); fuel prices do not depend on fuel use. So switching it on adds no loop to the year.

Data years. A year is a data year when Transport1 has it (2022-2025). In data years the model takes the data (ownership, stocks, EV shares, fuel economy, distance) wherever legacy has IF(ISNUMBER(data), data, model); from 2026 it projects. A blank Transport1 cell counts as no data.

Base-year split of road fuel. Transport1 gives, for each fuel, the share used by each vehicle type in the first data year. Light vehicles take the rest (A3.5):

\[ Q_{k,f} = sh_{k,f}\, F^{bal}_{f,t_0}, \qquad L_{k,f} = Q_{k,f}\, \lambda_f / 10^{9}, \qquad sh_{lgt,f} = 1 - \sum_{k \ne lgt} sh_{k,f} \tag{5.4}\]

where

  • \(sh_{k,f}\) is the share of fuel \(f\) used by vehicle type \(k\) (passenger, 2-3 wheeler, bus, freight), trshf;
  • \(F^{bal}_{f,t_0}\) is base-year road use of the fuel in the energy balance (ktoe), trp.eb;
  • \(\lambda_f\) is liters per ktoe (natural gas counted in liters of gasoline equivalent, as legacy), trp.lpk;
  • \(Q_{k,f}\) and \(L_{k,f}\) are the base-year fuel use of the type in ktoe and billion liters, trp.bsh.

Purchase taxes from the data. VAT on vehicles is the general VAT rate of the customs data year (VAT_WEO, from the domestic price data); freight vehicles pay no VAT, as legacy. Import duties on ICEVs and BEVs are those of the customs data year (2024), import shares those of the import data year (2022); 2-3 wheelers use the passenger import share. Bus electricity in the base year is the passenger EV stock share of 2024 times base-year bus fuel.

5.3.3 Data-year quantities

In the data years the model turns the Transport1 data into the quantities it needs. Passenger cars and light vehicles are blended by the passenger share of their ownership,

\[ \sigma = \frac{O^{data}_{pas}}{O^{data}_{pas} + O^{data}_{lgt}}, \qquad x_{mix} = \sigma\, x_{pas} + (1 - \sigma)\, x_{lgt} \tag{5.6}\]

which gives the blended fuel economy (LPG and CNG light vehicles scaled by \(fe_{pas,f}/fe_{pas,gso}\)), BEV fuel economy, distance, and EV shares of the stock and of purchases (trsps, trfem, trfem2, trfebm, trvkm, trevom, trevpm). From them:

  • PHEV distance share \(\phi = fe_{gso}/fe_{phv}\), the share of PHEV distance on gasoline; PHEV share of distance = (EV − BEV stock share) × \(\phi\) × \(\sigma\).
  • Distance by fuel = fuel economy × base-year fuel (billion liters, Equation 5.4), held from the base year; the vehicle shares by fuel are this distance split × (1 − EV stock share). The base-year split is used in every data year.
  • BEV distance: ICEV distance × \(\min\!\big(cap, (c^{BEV}/c^{ICE})^{\epsilon_U}\big)\), the cost-per-km ratio of a BEV to a gasoline car (prices before new policies; last year’s residential power price) raised to the usage elasticity, capped (trbvu).
  • Freight distance per vehicle in the base year = diesel freight distance / (diesel vehicle share × freight ownership × population); in later data years it moves with the passenger distance ratio (trvkfd).
  • Stocks by class and powertrain = ownership × (1 − BEV stock share) for ICEVs, the rest for BEVs (trstd).
  • Ownership years of a passenger vehicle \(= u\,L_{pas} + (1-u)\,n\) (trowny), with \(u\) the used share (data, then held).

The freight share of ownership is freight ownership over passenger and light ownership in data years; after the last data year it falls with income towards its target, capped at the last-data-year value \(\nu\):

\[ \phi^{fre}_t = \min\!\left(\nu,\ \max\!\left(\phi^{*},\ \nu - (\nu - \phi^{*})\,\frac{y_t}{y^{*}}\right)\right) \tag{5.7}\]

where \(\phi^{*} = 0.0236\) is the target share and \(y^{*} = 87{,}398\) USD the income at which it is reached.

5.4 Vehicle ownership and its calibration

Ownership is the backbone of the model: how many vehicles per thousand people a country wants at its income. It follows an S-shaped Gompertz curve in GDP per capita, scaled so that it passes through the observed ownership, and the fleet moves only part of the way towards it each year (M5.1, A3.6).

The Gompertz target for passenger and light vehicles:

\[ O^{*}_{pas,t} = \overline{S}\,(1 - \phi^{fre}_t)\; e^{\,a\, e^{\,b\, y_t/1000}}\; J_{pas} \tag{5.8}\]

where

  • \(\overline{S}\) is the ownership saturation point (vehicles per 1,000 people, Transport1, held after the data years), trsat;
  • \(\phi^{fre}_t\) is the freight share of ownership (Equation 5.7);
  • \(a = -5.897\) and \(b = -0.23\) per 1,000 USD are the Gompertz constants;
  • \(J_{pas}\) is the calibration factor (Equation 5.9), trjjp.

Freight uses the same curve with \(\overline{S}\,\phi^{fre}_t\) in place of \(\overline{S}\,(1-\phi^{fre}_t)\) and its own \(J_{fre}\).

Calibration to observed ownership. In each data year the model compares actual ownership with the curve; the factor of the last data year is held for all later years:

\[ J_{pas} = \frac{O^{data}_{pas,T}}{\overline{S}\,(1 - \phi^{fre}_{T})\, e^{\,a\, e^{\,b\, y_{T}/1000}}}, \qquad T = \text{last data year (2025)} \tag{5.9}\]

Freight’s \(J_{fre}\) is the same ratio with the freight curve. The factor works both ways: a country below the curve stays below it. For Egypt, ownership was 55 per 1,000 in 2025 against a Gompertz level of 152, so \(J = 0.36\).

Partial adjustment and limits. Desired ownership moves a fraction \(\mu\) of the way to the target, and actual ownership may grow or fall only within limits:

\[ \tilde{O}_{k,t} = \mu\, O^{*}_{k,t} + (1 - \mu)\, O_{k,t-1} \tag{5.10}\]

\[ O_{k,t} = \begin{cases} S^{data}_{k,ICE,t} + S^{data}_{k,BEV,t} & \text{data year} \\ \max\!\Big(O_{k,t-1}(1 - d_k),\ \min\big(O_{k,t-1}(1 + g_k),\ \tilde{O}_{k,t}\big)\Big) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.11}\]

where \(\mu = 0.09\) (own_adj), \(g_k = 7.5\%\) the maximum annual growth and \(d_k = 10\%\) the maximum annual decline (TrOwnDecline for passenger and freight, own_max_decline for 2-3 wheelers). This is the only partial-adjustment equation of the model.

2-3 wheelers follow a hump in income instead of a Gompertz curve, without partial adjustment (\(\mu = 1\)). With \(H = 7{,}000\) USD the income of the peak and \(\kappa = 0.737\) a scale factor:

\[ J^{cyc} = O^{data}_{cyc,T}\left(1 + \kappa\Big(1 - \frac{y_T}{H}\Big)\right), \qquad O^{*}_{cyc,t} = \frac{2\, J^{cyc}}{y_t/H + H/y_t} = \frac{2\,J^{cyc}\,H\,y_t}{y_t^2 + H^2} \tag{5.12}\]

\(J^{cyc}\) is the peak level, set from the last data year \(T\) and held. The target then enters Equation 5.11 with the 2-3 wheeler limits.

5.5 The stock: retirements, purchases and the closing stock

Each year some vehicles are scrapped and new ones are bought to reach the ownership of Equation 5.11. The stock is followed separately for new and used passenger vehicles, freight vehicles and 2-3 wheelers, each split into ICEVs and BEVs (A3.6).

Opening stock. In a data year it is the data (passenger: × the new or used share); otherwise last year’s closing stock:

\[ S^{o}_{k,v,t} = \begin{cases} S^{data}_{k,v,t}\, w_k & \text{data year} \\ S^{cl}_{k,v,t-1} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.13}\]

where \(v\) is ICE or BEV and \(w_{pas} = 1 - u_t\), \(w_{pau} = u_t\), \(w_{fre} = w_{cyc} = 1\).

Retirement is the average opening stock of the last years over the life, at most the stock itself:

\[ R_{k,v,t} = \min\!\left(\frac{1}{L_k}\cdot\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1} S^{o}_{k,v,t-j},\ \ S^{o}_{k,v,t}\right), \qquad m = \min(L_k,\ t - t_0 + 1) \tag{5.14}\]

with lives \(L_{pas} = n = 7\) (a new passenger vehicle is resold after seven years), \(L_{pau} = 20 - 7 = 13\), \(L_{fre} = 10\) and \(L_{cyc} = 7\). The stock after retirement is \(S^{a} = S^{o} - R\).

Purchases fill the gap between ownership and the stock that remains; passenger purchases are split between new and used vehicles by the used share:

\[ P_{k,t} = O_{k,t} - \sum_{v} S^{a}_{k,v,t}, \qquad P_{pas,t}^{new} = (1 - u_t)\, P_{pas,t}, \qquad P_{pau,t} = u_t\, P_{pas,t} \tag{5.15}\]

The purchases of each powertrain are the class purchases × its share from the choice of Section 5.10.

Closing stock. In data years a balancing item makes the closing stock equal the data; afterwards it is zero:

\[ S^{cl}_{k,v,t} = S^{a}_{k,v,t} + s_{k,v,t}\, P_{k,t} + \Delta_{k,v,t}, \qquad \Delta_{k,v,t} = \begin{cases} S^{data}_{k,v,t}\, w_k - S^{a}_{k,v,t} - s_{k,v,t}\, P_{k,t} & \text{data year} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.16}\]

where \(s_{k,v,t}\) is the purchase share of the powertrain. The BEV stock in million vehicles, \(\sum_k S^{o}_{k,BEV}\,N/1000\), drives the charging stations of Section 5.6; the EV share of the opening passenger stock drives the charging mark-up.

5.6 Charging, the charging mark-up and range anxiety

Electric vehicles face two costs that petrol cars do not: a mark-up for public charging, and range anxiety, the money-equivalent of the fear of running out of charge. Both fall as electric vehicles spread and charging stations are built.

Charging-station subsidy (policy scenarios only): zero before its start year, linear to the target level, then constant:

\[ \varsigma_t = \pi_t \cdot \begin{cases} 0 & t < t^{cs}_{0} \\ \varsigma_0 + (\varsigma_1 - \varsigma_0)\min\!\left(1,\ \dfrac{t - t^{cs}_0}{\max(1,\ t^{cs}_1 - t^{cs}_0)}\right) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.17}\]

where \(\pi_t\) is 1 in a policy scenario (scenario number above 1) and 0 in the baseline, \(t^{cs}_0, \varsigma_0\) the start year and level, \(t^{cs}_1, \varsigma_1\) the target year and level (TrCsStartYear, TrCsStartLevel, TrCsTargetYear, TrCsTargetLevel). The cost per charging port after the subsidy is \(C_t = C^{port}_t(1-\varsigma_t)\).

Charging stations are the data in data years, then grow with the BEV stock and fall with their cost:

\[ H_t = H_{t-1}\left(\frac{V^{BEV}_t}{V^{BEV}_{t-1}}\right)^{0.3}\left(\frac{C_t}{C_{t-1}}\right)^{-0.67} \tag{5.18}\]

where \(V^{BEV}\) is the BEV stock (million vehicles).

Charging mark-up on the electricity price falls with the EV share of the passenger stock \(e_t\):

\[ mku_t = \max\!\left(0.02,\ 0.3\,\frac{0.5 - e_t}{1 - 0.5}\right)\times 0.2 \tag{5.19}\]

(minimum and maximum mark-up in USD per kWh, the EV share where the minimum is reached, the share of public charging). The EV electricity price is last year’s end-user price plus the mark-up, \(p^{EV}_{u,t} = p^{T}_{u,t-1} + mku_t\), for residential (\(u = res\): passenger vehicles, 2-3 wheelers, PHEVs) and industrial users (\(u = ind\): freight). Using last year’s price avoids a circular reference with the power model.

Range anxiety (USD per vehicle, by class) keeps its start value for the first three years, then falls with the growth of charging stations:

\[ RA_{k,t} = \begin{cases} RA^{0}_k & t - t_0 + 1 \le 3 \\ RA_{k,t-1} & RA_{k,t-1} < 0 \\ \max\!\left(0,\ RA_{k,t-1}\,(1 - \delta_k)\left(\dfrac{H_t}{H_{t-1}}\right)^{-0.5}\right) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.20}\]

where \(\delta_k\) is an annual decline (default 0) and \(RA^0_k\) the start value from Transport3.

The start value is fitted. \(RA^0_k\) is the value that makes the model’s EV share of purchases in the fit year (2025, scenario 1) equal the observed share:

\[ r_k(RA^0_k) = s^{EV}_{k}(2025;\ RA^0_k) - s^{EV,data}_{k}(2025) = 0 \tag{5.21}\]

The root is found offline by a goal seek on this model and stored by country (negative values allowed, issue I145); countries without a fitted value use the legacy Transport3 value, and countries without either the Transport3 average. The fit is reported in the rows “EV sales share calculated − actual” of the data years (Section 5.14). For the data edition Public01 the fitted values (Egypt, the only country fitted so far) are 64,064 USD (passenger), 469,574 USD (freight) and 1,027 USD (2-3 wheeler). Legacy also allowed a Dashboard source of 10,000 USD for every class (ra_source, ra_dash); the model uses the per-country values.

5.7 Fuel economy of new vehicles and the feebate

New vehicles become more efficient over time, and faster when fuel is dear. A vehicle feebate (a fee on high-emission vehicles and a rebate on low-emission ones) acts as if it raised the fuel price (A3.8). The methodology describes this efficiency margin of the price response in section 3.3.5.3.

The feebate rate (USD per tCO2) is the scenario’s vehicle feebate from its introduction year, plus the road regulation shadow price; it applies in policy scenarios only:

\[ \beta_{k,t} = \pi_t\left(\mathbb{1}[t \ge t^{fb}_k]\,\beta^{0}_k + reg_{rod,t}\right) \tag{5.22}\]

where \(\beta^0_k\) is TrFbRate<k>, \(t^{fb}_k\) is TrFbYear<k> and \(reg_{rod,t}\) the road regulation shadow price (Chapter 1). The carbon price is not added: it is in the fuel price already.

Feebate per liter is the rate × the fossil CO2 per liter of the fuel:

\[ fb_{k,f,t} = \beta_{k,t}\, \varepsilon_f\, \theta_f \tag{5.23}\]

where \(\varepsilon_f\) is the CO2 emission factor (tCO2 per liter) and \(\theta_f\) the fossil share of road gasoline and diesel in the base year (base-year fossil use / (fossil + bioethanol or biodiesel); 1 for LPG and gas).

New fuel economy is the data × (1 + gap) in data years, then responds to the price incl. the feebate and gains an autonomous efficiency improvement:

\[ fe^{new}_{k,f,t} = \begin{cases} fe^{data}_{k,f,t}\,(1 + \gamma_k) & \text{data year} \\ fe^{new}_{k,f,t-1}\left(\dfrac{p_{f,t} + fb_{k,f,t}}{p_{f,t-1} + fb_{k,f,t-1}}\right)^{|\epsilon_{F}|}(1 + \alpha) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{5.24}\]

where

  • \(\gamma_k = 0.05\) is how much better new vehicles are than the stock average (fe_gap);
  • \(\epsilon_F\) is the road efficiency elasticity (passenger and 2-3 wheeler: gasoline’s for every fuel; freight: diesel’s);
  • \(\alpha\) is the autonomous efficiency gain of road oil;
  • \(p_{f,t}\) is the fuel price (freight: net of VAT; PHEV: the PHEV price, its ratio taken on the average passenger price).

The average new ICEV fuel economy and CO2 intensity weight the fuels by last year’s stock shares (data years: the vehicle shares); CO2 intensity of a fuel is \(\varepsilon_f \cdot 10^6 / fe\) (gCO2 per km). Used vehicles carry the past: their fuel economy is the average new fuel economy of the last \(\min(n, t - t_0 + 1)\) years, their CO2 intensity and their BEV fuel economy those of new vehicles \(n - 1\) years earlier.

5.8 Distance for the purchase decision

A buyer values a vehicle by the distance they expect to drive it. This distance per new vehicle is the data in data years, then follows income and, if switched on, the cost per km:

\[ v^{new}_{k,f,t} = v^{new}_{k,f,t-1}\left(\frac{c^{km}_{k,f,t}}{c^{km}_{k,f,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_U \cdot \chi}(1 + g_{t-1})^{\eta_k}, \qquad c^{km}_{k,f,t} = \frac{p_{f,t}}{fe^{new}_{k,f,t}} + \tau^{km}_{k,t} \tag{5.25}\]

where

  • \(c^{km}\) is the cost per km of a new vehicle (BEV: EV electricity price / new BEV fuel economy), USD per km;
  • \(\tau^{km}_{k,t}\) is the distance tax (Equation 5.26), USD per km;
  • \(\epsilon_U\) is the road usage elasticity (gasoline for passenger and 2-3 wheelers, diesel for freight);
  • \(\chi\) is 1 if the TCO distance responds to prices (vkt_new_price), 0 by default (legacy Dashboard Q551);
  • \(g_{t-1}\) is last year’s GDP growth and \(\eta_k\) the income elasticity of distance.

Freight distance is kept between 50,000 and 120,000 km. Used vehicles drive in proportion to their relative fuel economy, \(v^{used} = \bar{v}^{new}\,(\bar{fe}^{new}/fe^{used})^{\epsilon_U}\) (BEVs likewise with BEV fuel economy).

The distance tax (policy scenarios only) starts at its first-year amount and rises linearly to its target, then stays there:

\[ \tau^{km}_{k,t} = \pi_t\, \mathbb{1}[t \ge t^{dt}_0] \cdot \begin{cases} \tau_0 & t = t^{dt}_0 \\ \min\!\left(\tau_1,\ \tau^{km}_{k,t-1} + \dfrac{\tau_1 - \tau_0}{t^{dt}_1 - t^{dt}_0}\right) & t > t^{dt}_0 \end{cases} \tag{5.26}\]

with TrDtYear<k>, TrDtStart<k>, TrDtTargetYear<k>, TrDtTarget<k>.

5.9 Total cost of ownership

The choice between an ICEV and a BEV is made on the total cost of ownership per km: what the vehicle costs to buy, insure, maintain and fuel over the years it is owned, discounted, divided by the discounted distance it is driven (A3.7).

5.9.1 Discounted distance and purchase price

Discounted distance over the ownership period is the present value of the yearly distance:

\[ K_{k,v,t} = v_{k,v,t}\,\frac{(1 + r_t)^{N_k} - 1}{r_t\,(1 + r_t)^{N_k}} \tag{5.27}\]

with \(N_{pas} = 7\), \(N_{pau} = 13\), \(N_{fre} = 10\), \(N_{cyc} = 7\) and \(r_t\) the consumer discount rate (Equation 5.5).

Purchase price after policies. For an ICEV and a BEV of class \(k\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P^{ICE}_{k,t} &= P^{0,ICE}_{k,t}\,(1 + du^{ICE}_k\, m_k)(1 + adv^{ICE}_{k,t})(1 + vat_k) + FB^{ICE}_{k,t} \\ P^{BEV}_{k,t} &= P^{0,BEV}_{k,t}\,(1 + du^{BEV}_{k,t}\, m_k)(1 + vat^{BEV}_{k,t})(1 + adv^{BEV}_{k,t}) + FB^{BEV}_{k,t} + sub_{k,t} \end{aligned} \tag{5.28}\]

where

  • \(P^{0}\) is the vehicle price before taxes (Transport2), USD;
  • \(du_k\) the import duty and \(m_k\) the share of imports (customs and import data years);
  • \(adv\) the ad-valorem purchase tax: the baseline rate, or the policy rate from TrAdvYear<k> in policy scenarios (TrAdvIceBase, TrAdvBevBase, TrAdvIcePol, TrAdvBevPol);
  • \(vat_k\) the VAT on vehicles (0 for freight); for BEVs the policy rate (baseline × TrVatPolFactor) applies from TrVatYear<k>, and the policy BEV duty (baseline × TrCustPolFactor) from TrCustYear<k>;
  • \(FB\) the vehicle feebate (Equation 5.29);
  • \(sub_{k,t}\) the EV purchase subsidy (negative), Equation 5.30.

The vehicle feebate charges the CO2 intensity above a pivot, at the feebate rate, over the discounted distance:

\[ FB^{v}_{k,t} = \left(I^{v}_{k,t} - I^{piv}_{k,t}\right)\frac{\beta_{k,t}}{10^6}\, K_{k,v,t} \tag{5.29}\]

where \(I^{v}\) is the CO2 intensity of the vehicle (gCO2 per km; BEVs 0; used ICEVs the used intensity) and \(I^{piv}\) the pivot. Below the pivot the feebate is a rebate. The pivot is blank in the first two years, then either

  • Average: last year’s sales-weighted intensity extrapolated by its own trend, \(I^{piv}_t = \bar{I}_{t}\,\bar{I}_{t}/\bar{I}_{t-1}\), where \(\bar{I}_t\) is the intensity of last year’s sales (new and used, weighted by their ICE shares); or
  • Manual: TrFbPivot<k> in the feebate year, then lower by TrFbPivotChg<k> gCO2 per km each year, at least 0 (0 before the feebate year).

The EV subsidy (policy scenarios only) is \(-\)TrSubAmount<k> in its year, then changes by TrSubChg<k> a year, never positive:

\[ sub_{k,t} = \pi_t \cdot \begin{cases} -A_k & t = t^{sub}_k \\ \min(0,\ sub_{k,t-1} - \Delta_k) & t > t^{sub}_k \\ 0 & t < t^{sub}_k \end{cases} \tag{5.30}\]

5.9.2 Cost components

The TCO of a new vehicle adds five parts. With \(P\) the price after policies, \(d = 0.30\) the first-year depreciation, \(L\) the useful life, \(n\) the ownership period and \(r\) the discount rate:

\[ \begin{aligned} D^{1} &= d\,P && \text{first-year depreciation} \\ D^{ann} &= \frac{P - dP}{L - 1}\cdot\frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-(L-1)}}{r} && \text{depreciation annuity of the rest} \\ SV &= \min\!\left(0,\ -P\,\frac{(L - n)/L\,(1 - d)}{(1 + r)^{n}}\right) && \text{salvage value (passenger only)} \\ IM &= \frac{P\,\iota}{r}\left(1 - (1 + r)^{-(n+1)}\right)\theta + m^{v}\,K && \text{insurance and maintenance} \\ FU &= \left(\frac{p}{fe^{new}} + \tau^{km}\right)K && \text{fuel or electricity and distance tax} \end{aligned} \tag{5.31}\]

where \(\iota = 1.5\%\) is the insurance rate, \(\theta = 0.5\) the insurance factor, \(m^{v}\) the maintenance cost per km (ICEV or EV), \(p\) the average ICE fuel price over the fuel mix and \(fe^{new}\) the average new fuel economy (BEVs: the EV electricity price and new BEV fuel economy). Then

\[ TCO^{v}_{k,t} = D^1 + D^{ann} + SV + IM + FU + \mathbb{1}[v = BEV]\,RA_{k,t}, \qquad c^{v}_{k,t} = \frac{TCO^{v}_{k,t}}{K_{k,v,t}} \tag{5.32}\]

Used passenger vehicles are bought at their resale price after taxes, \(P^{res} = P^{0}\left(1 - \big(d + \frac{n - 1}{L - 1}(1 - d)\big)\right) \times\) (the same tax factors) + the feebate (BEV: + the subsidy). Their TCO has no first-year depreciation and no salvage value: the annuity is over the used ownership period less one year, \(13 - 1 = 12\) years, for ICEVs and BEVs alike; insurance runs over 13 years; fuel uses the used fuel economy. A used BEV carries range anxiety scaled by its price, \(RA^{used} = RA\,P^{used}/P^{new}\).

Freight vehicles and 2-3 wheelers have no salvage term; freight pays no VAT and charges at the industrial EV price, 2-3 wheelers at the residential one.

Per-fuel ICE TCO (for the fuel split of Section 5.10) uses the cost per km of each fuel and shares the feebate by intensity: \(TCO_f = K\,c^{km}_f + (D^1 + D^{ann} + SV) + IM + FB\,(I_f/\bar{I})\).

Plug-in hybrids take the BEV’s capital cost (no PHEV price exists in the data): BEV depreciation and salvage, insurance on the BEV price, ICEV maintenance over the ICEV distance, and the PHEV fuel cost; they carry no range anxiety, since they refuel with gasoline.

5.10 Choice: the EV share and the ICE fuel split

Buyers choose between an ICEV and a BEV by their TCO per km. The model uses a binary logit, as the power model does for dispatch and investment (methodology section 3.4.2.4.1); among ICEVs, the fuel mix shifts with the relative TCO of each fuel (M5.2, A3.7).

EV share of purchases. For each class (new passenger, used passenger, freight, 2-3 wheelers):

\[ s^{ICE}_{k,t} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(-k_k\,\dfrac{c^{BEV}_{k,t} - c^{ICE}_{k,t}}{\bar{c}_{k,t}}\right)}, \qquad s^{BEV}_{k,t} = 1 - s^{ICE}_{k,t}, \qquad \bar{c}_{k,t} = s^{ICE}_{k,t-1}\,c^{ICE}_{k,t} + (1 - s^{ICE}_{k,t-1})\,c^{BEV}_{k,t} \tag{5.33}\]

where \(k_k\) is the logit parameter (3 passenger, 2 freight, 4 2-3 wheelers) and \(\bar{c}\) the average TCO per km weighted by last year’s ICE share (base year: 1 − the data EV share). This is the two-option form of the equation list’s multinomial logit. In data years the actual shares replace the logit. The passenger EV share of purchases is \(u\,s^{BEV}_{pau} + (1 - u)\,s^{BEV}_{pas}\), the actual share where there is one.

ICE fuel split of passenger purchases. In data years the shares are the base-year distance split scaled to the ICE total; the PHEV share is (EV − BEV purchase share) × the passenger share \(\sigma\), at least 0. Afterwards each fuel’s share moves with its own TCO change and the TCO change of the other fuels:

\[ \tilde{s}_{f,t} = \tilde{s}_{f,t-1}\left(\frac{TCO_{f,t}}{TCO_{f,t-1}}\right)^{-1.265}\left(\frac{\sum_{g \ne f}\tilde{s}_{g,t-1}\,TCO_{g,t}/TCO_{g,t-1}}{\sum_{g \ne f}\tilde{s}_{g,t-1}}\right)^{0.5725} \tag{5.34}\]

over \(f, g \in\) {gasoline, diesel, LPG, CNG, PHEV}. If no other fuel has a share the share is held. The shares are then scaled to the ICE part of purchases, \(s_{f,t} = \tilde{s}_{f,t} / \sum_g \tilde{s}_{g,t}\,(1 - s^{EV}_t)\).

Stock shares turn over slowly: each year the ownership period’s share of the stock is replaced,

\[ s^{stock}_{f,t} = s^{stock}_{f,t-1}\,\frac{Y - 1}{Y} + \frac{s_{f,t}}{Y} \tag{5.35}\]

with \(Y\) the ownership years (\(u\,L_{pas} + (1-u)\,n\); 13.57 for Egypt) for passenger vehicles and \(L_{fre} = 10\) for freight; data years take the data. Divided by their sum plus the EV stock share, they give the vehicles by fuel (million) = passenger stock × share × population / 1,000.

Freight follows the same steps with diesel, LPG and LNG, scaled to \(1 - s^{BEV}_{fre}\). 2-3 wheelers have only gasoline ICEVs.

5.11 Stock fuel economy, distance driven and fuel use

Fuel use is vehicles × distance per vehicle / fuel economy of the stock (M5.3, A3.8). Each factor is projected separately.

Stock fuel economy turns over with the purchases: the data in data years, then

\[ fe^{stock}_{k,f,t} = \tau_k\, fe^{new}_{k,f,t} + (1 - \tau_k)\, fe^{stock}_{k,f,t-1} \tag{5.36}\]

with the turnover share \(\tau_{pas} = 1/\big(u\,n + (1-u)\,L_{pas}\big)\) (7 years used, 20 new), \(\tau_{fre} = 1/10\), \(\tau_{cyc} = 1/7\). The opening value is last year’s closing value. The BEV stock fuel economy turns over in the same way, with the share of this year’s BEV purchases in the closing BEV stock and the new BEV fuel economy (passenger: new and used BEVs weighted by their purchases).

Distance per vehicle is the data in data years (times the calibration factor of Equation 5.40), then responds to the cost per km of the stock and to income:

\[ v_{k,f,t} = v_{k,f,t-1}\left(\frac{c^{stock}_{k,f,t}}{c^{stock}_{k,f,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_U}\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}}\right)^{\eta_k}, \qquad c^{stock}_{k,f,t} = \frac{p_{f,t}}{fe^{stock,o}_{k,f,t}} + \tau^{km}_{k,t} \tag{5.37}\]

where \(fe^{stock,o}\) is the opening stock fuel economy, \(\epsilon_U\) the usage elasticity and \(\eta_k\) the income elasticity of distance (2-3 wheelers: −0.4, so they are driven less as income rises). The base distance is the Transport1 average distance (passenger and light blended; freight from the fuel shares, Section 5.3.3; BEVs with their uplift).

Distance driven and fuel use:

\[ B_{k,f,t} = \frac{V_{k,f,t}\, v_{k,f,t}}{1000}, \qquad F_{k,f,t} = \frac{B_{k,f,t}}{fe^{stock}_{k,f,t}} \tag{5.38}\]

where \(V\) is vehicles (million), \(B\) billion km and \(F\) billion liters (BEVs: billion kWh). Here \(fe\) is km per liter, so the equation list’s \(F = \sum VKT \cdot FE\) (M5.3) reads with \(FE = 1/fe\). PHEV distance is split: gasoline use \(B_{phv}/fe_{phv}\) is added to gasoline, electricity use is \(B_{phv}(1 - \phi)/fe^{BEV}\). CO2 by class is fuel use × the emission factor.

5.12 Calibration to the energy balance

The bottom-up fleet (Transport1 ownership × distance / fuel economy) rarely adds up to the road fuel use of the energy balance. Two calibration steps close the gap in the base year; their factors are then held (A3.8, A3.9).

Step 1: distance factors by class. The bottom-up base-year fuel use, grossed up for buses, is compared with the energy balance fuel by fuel:

\[ Q^{bu}_{f} = \frac{F^{0}_{f,t_0}\, 10^9 / \lambda_f}{1 - b_f}, \qquad q_f = \frac{F^{bal}_{f,t_0}}{Q^{bu}_f} \tag{5.39}\]

where \(F^{0}_{f,t_0}\) is uncalibrated fuel use (billion liters: gasoline = passenger + 2-3 wheelers; diesel, LPG and gas = passenger + freight), \(b_f\) the bus share of the fuel (gasoline 0), and \(q_f = 1\) when either is zero. The distance factor of a class is the fuel-use-weighted average of the \(q_f\):

\[ \kappa_{pas} = \frac{\sum_f F^{0}_{pas,f}\, q_f}{\sum_f F^{0}_{pas,f}}, \qquad \kappa_{fre} = \frac{\sum_f F^{0}_{fre,f}\, q_f}{\sum_f F^{0}_{fre,f}}, \qquad \kappa_{cyc} = q_{gso} \tag{5.40}\]

The factors multiply the data distance of each class in Equation 5.37 (BEVs too) and are held after the base year.

Step 2: a residual factor by fuel. After buses (Section 5.13) the calibrated total still differs from the energy balance, because a class factor averages over fuels. A second factor by fuel closes it:

\[ \gamma_f = \frac{F^{bal}_{f,t_0}}{F_{f,t_0}\,10^9/\lambda_f}, \qquad F^{T}_{f,t} = F_{f,t}\,\frac{10^9}{\lambda_f}\,\gamma_f \tag{5.41}\]

where \(F_{f,t}\) is road use of the fuel (billion liters: gasoline = passenger + 2-3 wheelers; diesel, LPG and gas = passenger + freight + buses) and \(F^{T}_{f,t}\) the result in ktoe. In the base year \(F^{T}\) equals the energy balance. Electricity has a factor only if recon_ele = Yes (default No, factor 1):

\[ E^{T}_t = E_t\,\frac{1000}{\omega}\,\gamma_{e} \tag{5.42}\]

with \(E_t\) road electricity (billion kWh: buses, 2-3 wheelers, freight, passenger), \(\omega\) GWh per ktoe.

The factors are large where the data explain little of road fuel use: for Egypt, passenger distance × 2.67 (freight 1.70, 2-3 wheelers 3.30), then gasoline × 1.23 (diesel 0.86, gas 1.14). The workbook reports them.

5.13 Buses

Buses are not modelled as a fleet. Their base-year fuel use (from the Transport1 fuel shares) moves with passenger energy use:

\[ F_{bus,f,t} = F_{bus,f,t_0}\,\frac{\sum_g F^{kt}_{pas,g,t}}{U_{t_0}}, \qquad E_{bus,t} = \Big(\sum_f F^{kt}_{bus,f,t_0} + E^{kt}_{bus,t_0}\Big)\frac{E^{kt}_{pas,t}}{U_{t_0}} \tag{5.43}\]

where

  • \(F_{bus,f,t_0}\) is base-year bus use of diesel, LPG and gas (billion liters);
  • \(F^{kt}_{pas,g,t}\) is passenger fuel use in ktoe and \(E^{kt}_{pas,t}\) passenger electricity in ktoe;
  • \(U_{t_0}\) is base-year passenger fuel and electricity use (ktoe, held);
  • \(E^{kt}_{bus,t_0}\) is base-year bus electricity: the passenger EV stock share of 2024 × base-year bus fuel.

Bus electricity is converted to billion kWh. All base-year bus energy electrifies with passenger electricity, as legacy.

5.15 Caveats

  • The engineer model is off by default. It is a calibration that has not been reviewed for every country; results are the elasticity model’s unless a scenario sets TrModel = Engineer.
  • CPAT Python computes the engineer model for the co-benefits module but cannot yet run with it switched on.
  • Calibration factors are large where Transport1 explains only part of road fuel use (for Egypt 30-60%: passenger distance × 2.67, then gasoline × 1.23). Review the data before using the engineer results.
  • Range anxiety is fitted to one year (2025) of observed EV sales in scenario 1; it is the main lever on the EV share and absorbs whatever the TCO misses.
  • Freight distance in the TCO is the Transport1 average (Egypt 48,630 km), floored at 50,000 km, which binds in every projection year; fuel use uses the distance derived from fuel shares (Egypt 8,091 km × 1.70). Kept as legacy (open question B14, Section 5.16.5).
  • Data years of inputs are kept as legacy: VAT on vehicles and customs duties of 2024, import shares of 2022, bus electricity from the 2024 EV share.
  • The ownership decline limit of 10% a year is a placeholder.
  • Not built: mode shift, and the road externalities of legacy sheet Transport driven by the engineer model’s distance and fuel economy (the co-benefits module works from fuel use instead).
  • Parameters are legacy values; the methodology note proposes others (Section 5.3.2).
