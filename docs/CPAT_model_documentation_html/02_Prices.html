2  Energy prices

This chapter explains how the model builds the price of every fuel in every sector, year by year. Prices are the first link of the model’s chain: a carbon tax, an ETS, an excise reform or a subsidy reform changes fuel use, power dispatch, emissions and revenue only through the prices computed here.

The chapter follows the order of the calculation. It starts with international prices, builds the domestic price before new policies (supply cost, excise and other taxes, VAT), adds the existing carbon pricing, the subsidy and price-control reforms and the new policies, and ends with the average prices and price indices that the model reports. The welfare use of the Pigouvian prices and the revenue raised at these prices belong to Chapter 7; electricity prices belong to Chapter 4.

The methodology describes prices in methodology, section 3.2 and, in more detail, in Appendix C. This chapter says where the model follows, simplifies or departs from it.

Assumptions you need here. The notation, dimensions and policy paths are set out in Chapter 1. Four of them matter for every equation of this chapter:

  • Every price, tax and policy value is in real USD of the results year \(R\) (ResultsYear, 2026), per GJ unless stated. A constant real price is a constant number; fuel use responds only to real price changes.
  • Prices up to the last historical price year \(L\) (Settings LastHistPriceYear, default 2024, as legacy) are data; later years are projected. Fuel use is data in the base year \(t_0 = 2022\) and modelled from 2023.
  • Each equation holds for every scenario \(o\) and country \(c\); both indices are omitted. Scenario 1 is the baseline.
  • Prices are quantity-free: everything up to the after-tax price is computed for all years before the year loop. Only the averages of Section 2.16 read fuel use, and they are computed inside the year loop.

2.1 The price chain at a glance

A fuel price is built in layers. The first layers are prices before new policies: what the country pays today, projected forward with the international price. The last layers are wedges that policies add, each passed through to the consumer with VAT.

2.2 Price blocks: fuels and price sectors

Prices are kept by price block: a fuel and a price sector, as in the IMF price dataset. There are 12 blocks. Coal and natural gas have one block each for power, residential and industry; the oil products and biomass have a single block for all sectors. Electricity prices are left to the power module (Chapter 4), but the electricity rows ecy.res and ecy.ind appear where a policy path is set per price fuel (fuel price reform, Pigouvian taxes), which makes 14 policy rows.

Each of the 16 subsectors takes the price of its price sector. Coal and gas use the block of that price sector; every other fuel uses its .all block, whatever the subsector. Jet fuel is priced at the kerosene block (with its own GJ per liter, 0.03486).

The road and residential sectors therefore pay the residential coal and gas prices; food, services and the transport modes other than road pay the industry prices.

Layout of a block, as a model concept. Each block is a small stack of rows that reads from top to bottom:

  1. its settings: the pass-through, the VAT rate, GJ per price unit and the position of its international price;
  2. the international reference price gpi, the fixed supply cost spf, the producer subsidy ps;
  3. the build-up before new policies: sp, txf, txo, fadtx, cs, etx, esub, esubpu, vat, ending with pbc, the retail price before new policies;
  4. the subsidy-reform wedges pcsw and ppcw;
  5. the new policies averaged over the subsectors priced by the block (pnq, pctx, etsnew, pntx, pxcd, pvatn, pnce) and the retail price including new policies rpn (Section 2.16).

New policies themselves act by subsector and fuel (rows 9 of the chain), because coverage, exemptions and emission factors differ by subsector. The block shows their fuel-use weighted averages.

2.3 Units, real values and the two indices

All money values are converted to real USD of the results year as soon as they are read. Two different indices do the conversion, both equal to 1 in the results year:

\[ \iota_t = \frac{\text{CPI}^{US}_R}{\text{CPI}^{US}_t}, \qquad \delta_t = \frac{\text{Defl}^{US}_R}{\text{Defl}^{US}_t} \tag{2.1}\]

where

  • \(\iota_t\) is the CPI index (code infl; US CPI from the IMF WEO), used for nominal domestic values: the IMF price dataset, margins and production costs, producer subsidies, existing carbon tax rates and permit prices, and a nominal carbon price;
  • \(\delta_t\) is the deflator index (code defl; US GDP deflator, WEO), used for nominal international prices;
  • \(R\) is the results year (2026).

This is the legacy split (legacy rows 456/725 for the CPI index, 467 for the deflator). The two differ by up to 1.2% before 2030 (2022: 1.137 against 1.124; 2030: 0.918 against 0.929), so one index alone would not reproduce legacy. The CPI index is held flat after 2031 (the WEO horizon); the deflator is not.

Where each input comes from, and in what units.

Input Stored as Converted with

The model computes to 2041 (the legacy horizon of the power block). Values after 2030 are not compared with legacy: the legacy CPI is flat after 2031 and some legacy rules misbehave over long horizons.

2.4 International prices

International prices drive the projection of every supply cost. The model reads a forecast of each commodity from the source chosen for the scenario, converts it to real terms and applies the High or Low adjustment. The methodology describes the sources in Appendix C, choices of the sources.

\[ gp_{m,t} = gpn_{m,t}\;\delta_t\;h_{m,t}, \qquad h_{m,t} = \begin{cases} 1 & t \le L \text{ or Base} \\ h^{High}_m \text{ or } h^{Low}_m & t > L \end{cases} \tag{2.2}\]

where

  • \(gp_{m,t}\) is the real international price of commodity \(m\) (code gp, source units);
  • \(gpn_{m,t}\) is the nominal price of \(m\) from the chosen source (code gpn; equation list A2.3); it is 1 for biomass, whose “commodity” is a flat line;
  • \(\delta_t\) is the deflator index (Equation 2.1);
  • \(h_{m,t}\) is the adjustment factor (code gp.adj holds the choice: 1 none, 2 High, 3 Low).

The commodity \(m\) of a block is crude oil for the five oil products, coal for coal, the gas of the country’s gas market for natural gas, and the flat line for biomass. The block reads its commodity’s price as its reference price gpi.

Choice of source. ScenarioDefinition IntEnerPricForeSource names the source; the asterisk of a default option is dropped (IMF-WB* is the average of IMF and WB). IntEnerPricForecastAdjustment chooses Base, High or Low. Both are per scenario. The Egypt runs use IMF.

The series by source. Each series covers 2021-2045. Up to a source’s last data year the series is data; after it the series is extended by one of two rules.

In words: the extension keeps a series constant in real terms (growth with US inflation), except EIA, which continues its own last trend. Averages skip a missing source. A blank data cell reads as 0; a year past a series’ data and before its extension year reads as missing. The global gas price is the average of LNG, North America and Europe (under Manual, the IMF global price before 2026).

The country’s gas market comes from sheet Prices_int (regional assumptions, “Natural gas market assumption”): LNG reads the LNG series, Europe the Europe series shifting to LNG (Section 2.5), North Am the North American series, and any other value the global average. Egypt’s market is Global.

2.5 Europe gas market: shift to LNG

For countries in the European gas market the model follows Europe’s move away from Russian pipeline gas. The gas price these countries face moves from the European hub price towards the LNG price as the share of gas from outside Russia grows.

\[ gpn^{eus}_t = gpn^{eur}_t\,(1 - s_t) + gpn^{lng}_t\,s_t \tag{2.3}\]

where

  • \(gpn^{eus}_t\) is the gas price of the European market (series nga.eus, by source);
  • \(gpn^{eur}_t\), \(gpn^{lng}_t\) are the Europe and LNG series of the same source;
  • \(s_t\) is the share of gas from outside Russia (Inputs_pint row 3; legacy rows 699, 706).
Year before 2022 2022 2023 2024 2025 and after

The 2024 value is \(1 - (0.303 + 0.229)/2\) and the 2025 value adds 0.1 to it; the share is held from 2025.

2.6 Historical domestic prices and forecasting coefficients

Up to the last historical year the domestic price of each block is data: the IMF price dataset gives the supply cost, the excise and other taxes, the VAT rate and the retail price. The same data step also sets the coefficients the projection uses: the pass-through, the fixed part of the supply cost and the VAT rate. The methodology describes the data in section 3.2.3 and Appendix C, price reconstruction.

2.6.1 Supply cost and taxes in the historical years

For each historical year \(y\) (data 2021 to \(L\)):

\[ sp_y = \frac{sp^{n}_y\,\iota_y}{u}, \qquad txo_y = \frac{\iota_y}{u}\begin{cases} \dfrac{rp^{n}_y}{1 + vatrate_y} - sp^{n}_y & \text{coal, gas, biomass} \\[2mm] txo^{n}_y & \text{oil products} \end{cases} \tag{2.4}\]

where

  • \(sp^n_y\), \(rp^n_y\), \(txo^n_y\) are the dataset’s supply cost, retail price and excise and other taxes, nominal USD per price unit (mit.sp.<block>, mit.rp.<block>, mit.txo.<block>);
  • \(vatrate_y\) is the VAT rate of the year (below);
  • \(\iota_y\) is the CPI index of the year and \(u\) GJ per price unit (A2.4).

So for coal, gas and biomass the taxes are the residual of the dataset’s retail price after VAT and supply cost; for the oil products they are the dataset’s own tax column, and the retail price is rebuilt as \((sp + txo)(1 + vatrate)\), as legacy does.

Manual values. Where Settings DomPrTax is on, a value typed in the manual price tables replaces the dataset value for the codes sp, vatrate, txo, rp and pros. A blank dataset cell reads as 0.

The pass-through \(pcc\) says how much of a change in the supply cost reaches the consumer: 1 in a liberalised market, 0 where the government fixes the retail price. It is set once per block. Settings GovPriceControls chooses how:

2.6.3 Fixed part of the supply cost

The supply cost has a fixed part (margins over the international price and, for coal and gas, the cost of domestic production) and a part that floats with the international price. The fixed part is set once:

\[ fixsp = \frac{mar\;\iota_{y^m} + dsh\;pcost\;\iota_{t_0}}{u} \tag{2.5}\]

where

  • \(fixsp\) is the fixed part of the supply cost (code spf, USD/GJ real, constant over the years);
  • \(mar\) is the margin over international prices (mit.mar.<block>, nominal USD per price unit) read in year \(y^m\): 2024 for coal and gas (a typed year, as legacy: issue I101), the base year for the oil products; 0 for biomass;
  • \(dsh\) is the share of the fuel produced domestically (mit.coa.prod, mit.nga.prod in the base year, clipped to \([0, 1]\); 0 for the other fuels);
  • \(pcost\) is the domestic production cost (mit.coa.prod.cost, mit.nga.prod.cost, base year; 0 for the other fuels);
  • \(\iota_{y^m}\), \(\iota_{t_0}\) are the CPI indices of the margin year and of the base year.

Other oil products take their pass-through and margin from the dataset like the other oil products (Egypt: 0 and 7.95 USD/bbl).

2.6.4 VAT rate

The VAT rate of a block is the dataset’s rate where it is filled. Where the cell is blank, consumers pay the general VAT rate of the country (VAT_WEO of the year; Egypt 14%) and firms pay none:

Block VAT rate where the data are blank
coa.res, nga.res; gso.all, die.all, lpg.all, ker.all, oop.all general rate
coa.pow, coa.ind, nga.pow, nga.ind 0 (VAT is credited to firms)
bio.all 0 (largely informal)

The rate used in the projection is the rate of year \(L\). For gasoline, diesel, LPG and kerosene it is then multiplied by the household share of the product (sheet OilProductShares; a blank share counts as 1), because only households pay VAT on the product; firms reclaim it:

\[ vatrate = vatrate_L \times hsh \tag{2.6}\]

where \(hsh\) is the household share of the oil product (Egypt: gasoline 1, diesel 0.03, LPG 0.99, kerosene 0.39). Other oil products and the other fuels take the full rate.

2.7 Producer subsidies

A producer subsidy lowers the cost at which a fuel is supplied, so it sits inside the supply cost, before any tax. The dataset gives each producer subsidy as a total amount; the model spreads it over the demand that carries it, to get a subsidy per unit. The methodology treats producer subsidies as part of the supply price (section 3.2.4).

Amount. For each producer fuel (coal, gas, oil, biomass, electricity):

\[ pros_{f,t} = \begin{cases} PROS^{n}_{f,t}\;\iota_t \times 1000 & t \le L \\ pros_{f,t-1} & t > L \end{cases} \tag{2.7}\]

where \(pros_{f,t}\) is the producer subsidy before the phase-out (USD million real) and \(PROS^n_{f,t}\) the dataset value (mit.pros.<fuel>.all, USD billion nominal, or a manual value).

Demand that carries it. The subsidy is spread over:

Fuel Demand \(D_f\) (code psd) Unit
Oil world demand × liters per ktoe (1,108,792) liters
Coal average of world demand and domestic consumption × GJ per ktoe (41,868) GJ
Gas average of world demand and domestic consumption × GJ per ktoe GJ
Biomass domestic consumption × GJ per ktoe GJ

World demand is the global production of the fuel in the IEA scenario chosen by GlobalEnergyDemand (stated policies, announced pledges or sustainable development; Mtoe × 1000). Domestic consumption is the energy use of the fuel in sheet EnergyCons, excluding electricity output and international bunkers.

Per unit, after the phase-out.

\[ psu_{f,t} = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{pros_{f,t} \times 10^6}{D_{f,t}} & t \le L \\[2mm] psu_{f,L}\;psf_{f,t} & t > L \end{cases} \tag{2.8}\]

where

  • \(psu_{f,t}\) is the producer subsidy per unit after the phase-out (negative; USD per liter for oil, USD/GJ for coal, gas and biomass);
  • \(psf_{f,t}\) is the producer subsidy factor of the fuel: the share of the subsidy kept (Equation 2.15; 1 without a reform).

Per block. Each block reads the subsidy of its international-price commodity and converts it to USD/GJ:

\[ ps_{g,f,t} = \frac{psu_{m(f),t} \times (\text{liters per barrel if } f = \text{oop})}{u} \tag{2.9}\]

where \(m(f)\) is oil for the five oil products, coal, gas, or biomass, and liters per barrel = 158.987. The producer subsidy enters the supply cost (Equation 2.10), so its phase-out raises the supply cost; the floating taxes then absorb \((1 - pcc)\) of the rise, as for any other change in the supply cost (A2.5).

The fiscal cost of producer subsidies after the phase-out, \(-\sum_f pros_f\,psf_f\) (code rpsu), is in the revenue totals (Chapter 7). The electricity producer subsidy is allocated in the power module (Chapter 4). Egypt has no producer subsidy data, so \(ps = 0\).

2.8 Supply cost

After the last historical year the supply cost moves with the international price. Only its floating part moves: the fixed part (margins, production cost) stays, and the producer subsidy is added back at its current level.

\[ sp_{t} = fixsp + \left(sp_{t-1} - fixsp - ps_{t-1}\right)\frac{gp_t}{gp_{t-1}} + ps_t, \qquad t > L \tag{2.10}\]

where

  • \(sp_t\) is the supply cost of the block (code sp, USD/GJ real; data up to \(L\), Equation 2.4);
  • \(fixsp\) is its fixed part (Equation 2.5);
  • \(gp_t\) is the block’s international reference price (gpi, Equation 2.2);
  • \(ps_t\) is the producer subsidy of the block (Equation 2.9).

This is the equation list’s M2.1. The methodology writes it as three parts, \(sp = fixsp + fltsp + ps\) with \(fltsp_t = fltsp_{t-1}\,gp_t / gp_{t-1}\) (Appendix C, calculations); the model’s equation is the same with \(fltsp_{t-1} = sp_{t-1} - fixsp - ps_{t-1}\).

Biomass has a flat international price, so its supply cost stays at its value of year \(L\).

2.9 Excise and other taxes: pass-through

The excise and other taxes txo are the net of every existing tax and consumer subsidy other than VAT. Their projection carries the price control: the share of a supply-cost change that is not passed through is absorbed by the taxes (or by the subsidy, which then floats). The methodology calls this the floating portion of taxes and subsidies (Appendix C, price liberalization).

Floating taxes. Each year the taxes absorb \((1 - pcc)\) of the change in the supply cost:

\[ \widetilde{txo}_t = \widetilde{txo}_{t-1} - \left(sp_t - sp_{t-1}\right)\left(1 - pcc\right), \qquad \widetilde{txo}_t = txo_t \text{ for } t \le L \tag{2.11}\]

which adds up to \(\widetilde{txo}_t = txo_L + (sp_L - sp_t)(1 - pcc)\) (M2.2).

Floor on a positive tax. A tax that was positive in year \(L\) does not fall below that level; a subsidy floats freely:

\[ txo_t = \begin{cases} \max\left(txo_L,\ \widetilde{txo}_t\right) & txo_L \ge 0 \\ \widetilde{txo}_t & txo_L < 0 \end{cases} \tag{2.12}\]

where \(\widetilde{txo}_t\) is the floating value (code txf) and \(txo_t\) the excise and other taxes before new policies (code txo, USD/GJ real; A2.1).

The two extreme cases make the rule clear:

  • \(pcc = 1\) (liberalised). Supply-cost changes are passed on in full; the taxes stay at \(txo_L\).
  • \(pcc = 0\) (fixed price). The price before new policies stays at its value of year \(L\); the subsidy (or the tax) absorbs every change in the supply cost. For Egypt’s gas and oil products, a fall in the oil price shrinks the subsidy and a rise widens it.

Here txo holds existing taxes and subsidies only. The methodology’s \(txo\) also holds the existing and new carbon prices and the new excise; the model keeps those apart as wedges (Section 2.12, Section 2.13).

WarningDifferences from legacy CPAT
  • L14 (fixed). With a pass-through of 0.5 or 0.8, legacy applies \((1 - pcc)\) twice in the first projected year (the floating subsidy is multiplied by \((1 - pcc)\) a second time), so other taxes jump. The model applies it once. No effect for Egypt (pass-through 0 or 1).
  • Methodology. The methodology averages the fixed and floating portions over five historical years (2018-2022) and multiplies the floating part by the price-control phase-out factor. The model, as legacy, projects from the single year \(L\) and keeps the phase-out as a separate wedge (Section 2.11).

2.10 VAT, the price before new policies and existing taxes

VAT is charged on the supply cost plus the other taxes. With it the block reaches its retail price before new policies, the price a consumer would pay if no new policy were introduced.

\[ vat_t = \left(sp_t + txo_t\right) vatrate, \qquad p^{0}_t = \left(sp_t + txo_t\right)\left(1 + vatrate\right) \tag{2.13}\]

where

  • \(vat_t\) is the VAT payment before new policies (code vat, USD/GJ real);
  • \(vatrate\) is the block’s VAT rate (Equation 2.6; code vr in CPAT Python);
  • \(p^0_t\) is the retail price before new policies (code pbc; M2.3).

Existing taxes and subsidies. The net txo is split by sign, so that taxes and subsidies can be reported and reformed separately. Subsidies are negative throughout the model.

2.11 Subsidy and price-control phase-outs

Two reforms remove the existing consumer support. The consumer subsidy reform removes the fixed subsidy; the price-control phase-out (price liberalisation) removes the floating part, so the consumer comes to pay the supply-cost changes. Both are separate policy lines, by price block: the price before new policies pbc is not changed, and the reforms are wedges added to the after-tax price like the new policies. The methodology describes them in Appendix C, fossil fuel subsidies reform and price liberalization.

2.11.1 Phase-out factors

There is one factor per reform \(k\): producer subsidies (ps), consumer subsidies (cs) and price controls (pc). Each is the share of the original support still kept, the same in every year for every line:

\[ pof_{k,t} = \begin{cases} \max\!\left(1 - \bar{s}_k,\ 1 - \bar{s}_k\,\dfrac{t - T_k}{\max(N_k, 1)}\right) & \text{the reform is on and } t > T_k \\[2mm] 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{2.14}\]

where

  • \(pof_{k,t}\) is the phase-out factor (code pof; M1.7);
  • \(\bar{s}_k\) is the share to phase out (po.sh), \(T_k\) the start year and \(N_k\) the period in years (po.n).

The inputs come from ScenarioDefinition. Scenario 1 reads the baseline switch, period and share; the other scenarios read the policy switch and period, with the share the larger of the baseline and the policy share (legacy rows 264-274). The price-control phase-out applies only if its policy switch is on and either the scenario is a policy scenario or its baseline switch is on. It uses the consumer-subsidy shares (issue I48) and the same period in every scenario. A switch counts as on if it is TRUE or starts with “Yes”.

Which blocks and fuels a reform covers. Inclusion switches on ScenarioDefinition, per scenario, let a reform cover some fuels and sectors only: ConsSubPhaseScope_<block> (\(csi\)) and PriceCtrlPhaseScope_<block> (\(pci\)) for the 12 price blocks, and ProdSubPhaseScope_<fuel> (\(psi\)) for coal, gas, oil, biomass and electricity. Each turns the reform factor into the factor of its line:

\[ csf = 1 - csi\,(1 - pof_{cs}), \qquad pcf = 1 - pci\,(1 - pof_{pc}), \qquad psf_f = 1 - psi_f\,(1 - pof_{ps}) \tag{2.15}\]

2.11.2 The wedges

Each block splits its taxes into the fixed part fadtx and the floating part cs (Section 2.10). The consumer subsidy reform removes the share \((1 - csf)\) of a fixed subsidy; the price-control phase-out removes the share \((1 - pcf)\) of the floating part, whether that is a subsidy or a tax:

\[ pcsw_t = -\left(1 - csf_t\right)\min\left(fadtx_t,\ 0\right), \qquad ppcw_t = -\left(1 - pcf_t\right) cs_t \tag{2.16}\]

where

  • \(pcsw_t\) is the consumer subsidy reform wedge of the block (USD/GJ, before VAT);
  • \(ppcw_t\) is the price-control phase-out wedge of the block (USD/GJ, before VAT).

Because the two wedges act on different parts of txo, using both reforms does not count a subsidy twice. For Egypt every subsidised fuel has pass-through 0, so its whole subsidy floats: only the price-control phase-out removes it, as in legacy.

Every subsector reads the wedges of its block as csw and pcw and adds them, with VAT, to its after-tax price (Equation 2.24). The revenue of the reforms, \(F\,\theta\,(csw + pcw)(1 + vatrate)\) (code rsr: the subsidy saved plus the VAT on it), and the producer-subsidy line rpsu are in Chapter 7; the net existing revenue rnet stays the pre-reform one.

2.12 Existing carbon pricing

Some countries already price carbon, with a carbon tax, a national ETS or the EU ETS. The IMF retail prices already contain whatever existing carbon pricing a country had, so the model adds only the change against that level: growth after the last data year, new data, or a scheme switched off. A country’s history is never counted twice. The methodology describes existing carbon pricing in section 3.2.4 and Appendix C, policy options.

2.12.2 Factors, level and change

ScenarioDefinition decides, per scenario, whether each scheme applies and how its real price grows after the last data year \(T_x\) (2025):

\[ \varphi^{ct}_t = \mathbb{1}[\texttt{ExistingCTApply}]\,(1 + \gamma^{ct})^{\max(0,\,t - T_x)}, \qquad \varphi^{ets}_t = \mathbb{1}[\texttt{ExistingETSApply}]\,(1 + \gamma^{ets})^{\max(0,\,t - T_x)} \tag{2.18}\]

where \(\gamma^{ct}\) is ExistingCTGrowth and \(\gamma^{ets}\) is ExistingETSGrowth (real growth per year; codes xct.p, xets.p).

Then, per subsector and fuel:

\[ xcp_{s,f,t} = ef_{s,f}\left(XT_{j,f,t^*}\,\varphi^{ct}_t + XE_{j,f,t^*}\,\varphi^{ets}_t\right), \qquad \Delta xcp_{s,f,t} = xcp_{s,f,t} - ef_{s,f}\,XCP^{0}_{j,f} \tag{2.19}\]

where

  • \(xcp_{s,f,t}\) is the level of existing carbon pricing (code xcp, USD/GJ);
  • \(\Delta xcp_{s,f,t}\) is its change against the price data (code xcpd, USD/GJ; A2.2);
  • \(ef_{s,f}\) is the calibrated CO2 emission factor (tCO2/GJ; Chapter 6);
  • \(t^* = \min(t, T_x)\) is the data column of the year (code xct.c), \(j = j(s,f)\) the ECP sector;
  • \(XCP^0_{j,f} = XT_{j,f,y_e} + XE_{j,f,y_e}\) is the existing carbon pricing embedded in the price data, the tables in the year \(y_e = \min(L, T_x)\) (2024).

Only \(\Delta xcp\) enters the after-tax price (Equation 2.24). Switching a scheme off therefore lowers the price by its embedded level; growth or new data raise it.

Power. The same change per kWh (the power row of the tables) is part of the variable cost before new policies, so dispatch, investment and electricity prices respond (Chapter 4).

New carbon tax beside an existing ETS. If CTaxComplimentaryToETS = Yes (legacy rows 2270-2288), the new carbon tax of a sector covers 1 − the national ETS sector coverage of the year (Chapter 1). Otherwise new instruments add to existing ones.

Revenue. The existing-tax revenue includes \(\Delta xcp\,(1 + vatrate)\); a memo shows the level, fuel use × \(xcp\). Existing ETS revenue is valued at the full permit price, as legacy; legacy’s free-allocation and revenue data (ECP rows 560-597, 1225-1322) are not used, in legacy’s model either (Chapter 7).

2.13 New policies and the after-tax price

The new policies of a scenario act per subsector and fuel, because coverage, exemptions and emission factors differ by subsector. Each policy is turned into a cost per GJ and added, with VAT, to the price before new policies of the subsector’s block. The result is the after-tax price, the price that drives fuel use. The methodology describes the new carbon price and the new excise in section 3.2.4 and in Appendix C, policy coverage and exemptions.

2.13.1 New carbon tax, ETS cost and excise per GJ

\[ nct_{s,f} = NCT_s\;ef_{s,f}\;\varphi_{NCT,f}\;x_{s,f}, \qquad netsp_{s,f} = NETSP_s\;ef_{s,f}\;x_{s,f}, \qquad nexc_{s,f} = \frac{F^{x}_{c(s,f)}}{u_f}\;x_{s,f} \tag{2.20}\]

where

  • \(nct_{s,f}\), \(netsp_{s,f}\), \(nexc_{s,f}\) are the new carbon tax, the new ETS cost and the new excise (codes ctxnew, ets, ntx; USD/GJ; M2.4);
  • \(NCT_s\) is the new carbon tax rate of the sector (USD/tCO2; code ctr): the explicit carbon price where the sector is covered and not in a new ETS (Chapter 1);
  • \(NETSP_s\) is the ETS cost in the price of the sector (code etr): the tax-equivalent permit price × ETS coverage × the auctioned share (Chapter 6);
  • \(ef_{s,f}\) is the calibrated CO2 emission factor (tCO2/GJ);
  • \(\varphi_{NCT,f}\) is the carbon-tax coverage of the fuel (code ctcov: 1 if covered, else the exemption phase-out path; biomass is covered with emission factor 0);
  • \(F^x_c\) is the new excise of the price fuel \(c(s,f)\) in price units (code ntxb; Equation 2.21), and \(u_f\) GJ per price unit of the fuel row;
  • \(x_{s,f}\) is the exemption factor (code exf, below).

The new excise. The fuel price reform path fpr (Chapter 1, M1.5) is set per price fuel in price units (USD/liter, USD/bbl, USD/GJ). It acts only in the policy scenarios:

\[ F^x_{c,t} = \mathbb{1}[o \ne B]\;ntxg_{c,t}, \qquad ntxg_{c,t} = fpr_{c,t} + \mathbb{1}[\texttt{AddEfficientTaxes}]\;pigx_{c,t} \tag{2.21}\]

where \(ntxg\) is the gross new excise (code ntxg) and \(pigx\) the Pigouvian tax (Section 2.15; with AddEfficientTaxes = Yes the formula of \(F^x\) changes, Equation 2.31). Jet fuel reads the kerosene reform per liter and divides it by its own GJ per liter.

Total new policy:

\[ nce_{s,f} = nct_{s,f} + netsp_{s,f} + nexc_{s,f} \tag{2.22}\]

Residential LPG and kerosene always exempted. ScenarioDefinition AlwaysExemptResLPGKer (row 268, by scenario; default No) exempts these two rows from every new policy. If the switch starts with “Yes” (Yes or Yes), the exemption factor of the residential LPG and kerosene rows is 0; every other row has factor 1:

\[ x_{s,f} = \begin{cases} 0 & f \in \{\text{LPG, kerosene}\},\ s = \text{residential},\ \texttt{AlwaysExemptResLPGKer} \text{ starts with "Yes"} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{2.23}\]

Their new carbon tax, ETS cost and new excise are then 0, so neither is the VAT on them, nor their new-policy revenue (rnew, the excise revenue rex and the VAT on new policies in rvt). Existing taxes, the change in existing carbon pricing and the subsidy phase-outs stay, as legacy (row 2651, the price before new policies). The shadow price is not exempted, as legacy. ETS-covered emissions still count them where the residential sector is in an ETS (Chapter 6), and the residential leakage to biomass reads the row’s own nce, so an exempt fuel does not leak (Chapter 3).

2.13.2 The after-tax price

\[ p_{s,f} = \max\!\Big(p^{0}_{g,f} + \big(nce_{s,f} + \Delta xcp_{s,f} + csw_{s,f} + pcw_{s,f}\big)\left(1 + vatrate_{g,f}\right),\ \underline{p}\Big) \tag{2.24}\]

where

  • \(p_{s,f}\) is the after-tax price of fuel \(f\) in subsector \(s\) (code atp, USD/GJ real; M2.5);
  • \(g\) is the price sector of \((s,f)\), so \(p^0_{g,f}\) is the price before new policies of the block (Equation 2.13) and \(vatrate_{g,f}\) its VAT rate;
  • \(\Delta xcp_{s,f}\) is the change in existing carbon pricing (Equation 2.19);
  • \(csw_{s,f}\), \(pcw_{s,f}\) are the subsidy and price-control wedges of the block (Equation 2.16);
  • \(\underline{p}\) is the price floor (PriceFloor, 0.01 USD/GJ, legacy’s).

New policies are passed through in full and pay VAT, as legacy’s VAT on txo with nce inside it. With the exemption, the residential LPG and kerosene price is \(\max(p^0 + (\Delta xcp + csw + pcw)(1 + vatrate), \underline{p})\).

2.14 The shadow price

Some policies change behaviour without changing what consumers pay: feebates, efficiency regulations and the part of a new ETS that is allocated free. The model represents them as a shadow price per GJ. Demand responds to it on the efficiency margin only, and it raises no revenue.

\[ sh_{s,f} = \Sigma_s\;ef_{s,f} \tag{2.25}\]

where

  • \(sh_{s,f}\) is the shadow price per GJ (code shp; M2.6);
  • \(\Sigma_s\) is the shadow price of sector \(s\) per tonne (code shpx; M1.4): the feebate rate × the share of the feebate on the efficiency margin, plus the shadow price of regulations, plus the tax-equivalent ETS price × ETS coverage × the share allocated free (Chapter 1).

The demand equation uses the after-tax price twice: alone in the usage term, and with the shadow price in the efficiency term, \(\big((p + sh)_t / (p + sh)_{t-1}\big)^{\varepsilon_F (1 + \varepsilon_U)}\) (Chapter 3). The shadow price is not exempted by AlwaysExemptResLPGKer, as legacy.

2.15 Pigouvian components

The model can compute an efficient (Pigouvian) tax per price unit: the cost of the local air pollution, the climate damage and the road-transport externalities of one more unit of the fuel. Here they are prices; their welfare use and the efficient prices of the subsidy estimates are in Chapter 7. The methodology describes the external costs in section 3.7.2.

The components are computed in every run; they enter prices only if ScenarioDefinition AddEfficientTaxes = Yes, which CPAT Python does not support yet (Section 2.17). All are by price code \(c\) (the 12 blocks and the two electricity rows) in USD per price unit (A1.2).

2.15.1 Phase-in

\[ \phi_t = \min\!\left(\frac{\max(t - T_0 + 1,\ 0)}{\max(n,\ 1)},\ 1\right) \tag{2.26}\]

where \(\phi_t\) is the phase-in share (code pig.phi), \(T_0\) the carbon-pricing start year (CPIntro) and \(n\) AddEfficientTaxesPhaseInYears.

2.15.2 Air pollution

The marginal damage of air pollution per GJ of fuel \(f\) in subsector \(s\) is the value of averted PM2.5 times the PM2.5 concentration one GJ adds:

\[ mc^{ap}_{s,f,t} = v_t\;\Big[(1 - w_t)\,d^{(0)}_{s,f} + w_t\,d^{(1)}_{s,f}\Big]\;\frac{10^9}{\theta \cdot 10^6} \tag{2.27}\]

where

  • \(mc^{ap}_{s,f,t}\) is the marginal air-pollution damage (code airpol.mc, USD/GJ; power: by plant type);
  • \(v_t\) is the value of averted modelled ambient PM2.5 (code pig.vpm, USD billion per µg/m³, from the air pollution module, typed FromAirPollution; the base year reads the next year, because legacy has no change in PM2.5 in 2022);
  • \(d^{(0)}\), \(d^{(1)}\) are the µg/m³ per ktoe of the fuel: the module’s concentration coefficients \(\kappa\) of the source (road, ground level for residential, services and construction, food, other; coal, gas and other power plants) times the combustion emission factors of each pollutant, with base-year and final (EFEndYear, 2039) factors;
  • \(w_t\) is the weight of the final factors (code pig.efw): EFFinShare × \((t - t_0)/(\text{EFEndYear} - t_0)\), with EFFinShare 0 for constant factors, 1 for variable and 0.5 otherwise;
  • \(\theta\) is PJ per ktoe.

By price code (code pig.ap), the subsector damages are averaged with base-year fuel use as weights (the plain average where all weights are 0) and converted to price units:

Price code Average over Notes

2.15.3 Climate

\[ cc_{c,t} = SCC^{nat}_t\;\overline{ef}_{f(c)}\;u_c \tag{2.28}\]

where \(cc_{c,t}\) is the climate damage per price unit (code cc.code), \(SCC^{nat}_t\) the national social cost of carbon (M1.6) and \(\overline{ef}_f\) the generic, uncalibrated CO2 factor of the fuel (averaged over its emission-factor sectors; IEA for other oil products; issue I116).

2.15.4 Road transport

Accidents, congestion and road damage are costs per vehicle-km. The model turns them into costs per liter with the base-year kilometres driven per liter of the fuel’s total use, then lets them grow with the cost per km:

\[ tr_{c,t} = \sum_{k} cost^{km}_{k,t_0}\;kpl_f\;\big(1 - \omega_f\big)^{[k = rdm]}\;\frac{cost^{km}_{k,t}}{cost^{km}_{k,t_0}}, \qquad kpl_f = \overline{fe}_f\;\mu_f\;\rho_f \tag{2.29}\]

where

  • \(tr_{c,t}\) is the road-transport cost per liter (code pig.tr; gasoline: accidents and congestion; diesel: also road damage, for the freight share only, issue I118; 0 for the other price codes);
  • \(cost^{km}_{k,t}\) is the cost per vehicle-km of kind \(k\) (accidents, congestion, road damage) from the transport co-benefits module (typed FromTransportCB); the ratio is the growth against the base year (codes pig.trg.*); the products \(cost^{km}_{k,t_0}\,kpl_f\) are the base-year costs per liter (codes pig.trb.*);
  • \(\overline{fe}_f\) is the base-year fuel economy (code pig.fe0, km/liter): passenger and light vehicles weighted by ownership, then mixed with 2-3 wheelers (gasoline) or freight (diesel) by the share \(\omega_f\);
  • \(\omega_f\) is the passenger and light share of the road use of the fuel (code pig.wsh): gasoline 1 − the cycle share; diesel 1 − freight / (1 − bus);
  • \(\mu_f\) is the driving share of the fuel’s response (code pig.mu0): gasoline \(\varepsilon_U / (\varepsilon_U + \varepsilon_F)\); diesel \((\varepsilon^{die}_U + \omega(\varepsilon^{gso}_U - \varepsilon^{die}_U)) / (\varepsilon^{die}_U + \varepsilon^{die}_F + \omega(\varepsilon^{gso}_U + \varepsilon^{gso}_F - \varepsilon^{die}_U - \varepsilon^{die}_F))\);
  • \(\rho_f\) is the base-year road share of the fuel’s use over all subsectors (code pig.rsh0).

2.15.5 The Pigouvian tax and its netting

\[ pigx_{c,t} = \phi_t\left(ap_{c,t} + \mathbb{1}[\texttt{ClimateEffTax}]\;cc_{c,t} + tr_{c,t}\right) \tag{2.30}\]

where \(ap_{c,t}\) is the air-pollution cost per price unit (pig.ap). With AddEfficientTaxes = Yes the gross new excise \(ntxg = fpr + pigx\) (Equation 2.21) is netted against the existing taxes, so the Pigouvian tax tops up what is already there:

\[ F^x_{c,t} = \mathbb{1}[o \ne B]\;\min\!\Big(ntxg_{c,t},\ \max\big(0,\ ntxg_{c,t} - xpre_{c,t}\,u_c\big)\Big), \qquad xpre_{c,t} = txo_{c,t} + pcsw_{c,t} + ppcw_{c,t} + xcpb_{c,t} \tag{2.31}\]

where

  • \(xpre_{c,t}\) is the existing taxes before new policy of the block (code xpre, USD/GJ; 0 for electricity);
  • \(xcpb_{c,t} = \overline{ef}_f\,(XT\,\varphi^{ct}_t + XE\,\varphi^{ets}_t - XCP^0)\) is the change in existing carbon pricing of the block, at the generic emission factor and the block’s own ECP sector and fuel (code xcpb; 0 for electricity).

The block’s ECP key for xcpb: coal and gas by price sector; gasoline and diesel transport; LPG, kerosene and biomass buildings; other oil products industry.

2.16 Average prices and price indices

Prices differ by subsector, so a single price per fuel needs weights. The model reports two kinds of averages: the average new-policy wedges of each price block, and average prices and price indices by fuel. Both are computed in the year loop, because they read fuel use. The methodology’s aggregate price is the fuel-use weighted average (section 3.2.5).

2.16.1 New policies by price block

Over the subsectors \(s \in c\) priced by block \(c\), weighted by their fuel use \(F_{s,f}\) (ktoe):

\[ \bar{p}_c = \frac{\sum_{s \in c} p_{s,f}\,F_{s,f}}{\sum_{s \in c} F_{s,f}} \tag{2.32}\]

where \(\bar{p}_c\) is the retail price including new taxes and subsidies of the block (code rpn; \(p^0_c\) where the subsectors use none; A2.6). The block also shows:

Code Meaning
pnq \(\sum_{s \in c} F_{s,f}\), the weights (ktoe)
pctx, etsnew, pntx, pxcd fuel-use weighted averages of ctxnew, ets, ntx, xcpd
pnce \(\bar{p}_c - p^0_c\), the total new policy including VAT
pvatn pnce − (pctx + etsnew + pntx + pxcd + pcsw + ppcw): the VAT on the new policies and wedges, and the effect of the price floor

These are weighted averages with each scenario’s own quantities, not price indices.

2.16.2 Average prices and indices by fuel

For each fuel (coal, gas, gasoline, diesel, LPG, kerosene, jet fuel, other oil products, biomass), for electricity and for all energy, the model sums over the 16 subsectors (electricity: the residential and industry user groups). Subscript 1 is scenario 1 in the same year; subscript 0 is the base year, whose quantities and prices are data and so equal in every scenario.

The unit-value ratio against scenario 1 equals the price index times the mix effect. Legacy reports the unit-value ratio as the price change (issue I31); here the two parts are separate.

\[ \frac{uv}{uv_1} = las \times mix, \qquad las = \frac{\sum_s p_s\,F_{1,s}}{\sum_s p_{1,s}\,F_{1,s}}, \qquad time = \frac{\sum_s p_s\,F_{0,s}}{\sum_s p_{0,s}\,F_{0,s}} \tag{2.33}\]

Units. Fuels: USD/GJ × ktoe for spending, USD/GJ for the unit value. Electricity: USD/kWh and GWh. All energy: spending in USD million (fuel spending × PJ per ktoe, plus electricity spending USD/kWh × GWh), fuel use in ktoe with electricity converted (GWh × GJ per kWh / PJ per ktoe), so its unit value is in USD million per ktoe.

The legacy average (pav.leg, legacy rows 16293-16405):

  • Coal and gas average three sector prices with scenario-1 quantities of the same year: residential rpn (weight: scenario-1 residential subsector use, pavl.qr), industry rpn (the 8 industry subsectors, pavl.qi), and power (weight: scenario-1 power generation fuel use, pavl.qp). The power price is the pre-policy price plus the policy cost per GJ of the power type: \(pp = p^0_{pow} + G \cdot M \cdot pc / (FU \cdot \theta)\) (code pavl.pp; \(G\) generation in MWy, \(M\) million kWh per MWy, \(pc\) the new policy cost per kWh xac, \(FU\) the power fuel use). This is a Laspeyres index on three sectors; it falls back to the unit value where the weights are 0.
  • Oil products take the single price rpn of their .all block.
  • Electricity averages residential and industry with each scenario’s own consumption (= pav.uv).
  • Biomass, jet fuel and all energy have no legacy average: their unit value, and their reported index is the Laspeyres one.
