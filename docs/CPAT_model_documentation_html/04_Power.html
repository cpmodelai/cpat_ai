4  Power

The power block is CPAT’s techno-economic (“engineer”) power model. Every year it works out how much electricity the economy needs, which plants generate it, which plants retire, what is built to replace them, what it all costs and what electricity users pay.

It is the largest block of the model and the one where most of the sector’s policy effects arise: a carbon price changes the cost of each generation type, which changes dispatch and investment, which changes the generation mix, emissions, the cost of supply and, a year later, electricity prices and demand.

The methodology describes the power models in section 3.4: power prices and generation costs in 3.4.1, the engineer model in 3.4.2, and the data in 3.4.4. The methodology also has an elasticity-based power model (3.4.3) and, for it, the “old” generation costs of Appendix C. Excel-AI and CPAT Python implement the engineer model only; the elasticity model is not part of this book. (The methodology’s corrections of 2026-10-31 to these two parts, DOC5 and DOC15, therefore have no counterpart here; the corrections that concern the engineer model are linked in the sections below: corrections.)

4.1 The power block at a glance

4.1.1 Generation types

The model has nine generation types. Two of them, coal and gas, are dispatched by cost. The other seven run at their capacity factor whenever they exist.

4.1.2 Notation

The chapter uses the notation of the methodology where it has a symbol, and the symbol of the equation list (Appendix A) otherwise.

  • Indices. Generation type \(f\); user group \(g \in \{\text{res}, \text{ind}\}\) (residential, industry); demand group \(d \in \{\text{tra}, \text{res}, \text{ind}, \text{srv}\}\) (transport, residential, industry, services); subsector \(s\); year \(t\). Every equation holds for each scenario \(o\) and country \(c\); these two indices are omitted. A value of the baseline in the same year carries the superscript \(B\).
  • Years. \(t_0\) is the base year (first year of calculations, 2022), \(L\) the last historical price year (2024 for the current data), \(T = 2041\) the last model year.
  • Units. Generation and capacity in MW (generation as MWy, i.e. average MW over the year); demand in ktoe; costs in real US dollars of the results year (2026) per kWh (USD/kWh); capital costs in USD/kW; money flows in USD million.
  • Constants. \(H = 8{,}760\) hours per year; \(\theta_e = 0.0036\) GJ per kWh; 8.76 million kWh per MWy (MWyMkWh); \(\theta_g = 11.63\) GWh per ktoe (KtoeGWh); 0.041868 PJ per ktoe.
  • Minimum cost. \(c_{min}\) = MinPostSubsidyPowerPrice (default 0.01 USD/kWh) is the floor on costs that enter a ratio (dispatch and investment logits) and on end-user prices.

The CPAT variable code of each quantity is given in code font (cap, tic, …): it is the row code of Excel-AI and the variable of CPAT Python.

4.1.3 Inputs by scenario

Every power input of the scenario sheet is read in the scenario’s own column. This covers capacity-factor and efficiency bounds, WACC options, discounted lifetimes, investment permissions and start years, scale-up and retirement rates, the early-retirement factor, the capex decline scenario, storage, PPAs, renewable subsidies, rebates and excises. The sub-table A0 (Inputs of the scenario) applies them to the power data of the country.

Scenario 1 is the baseline of every scenario. A technical input (logit constants, WACC, capacity-factor bounds, storage, cost decline) changed in a policy scenario therefore changes that scenario only, and its difference from scenario 1 mixes the input change with the policy. Technical inputs are not usually changed across scenarios.

Not read, because the model has no such feature: CostBreakdownYear (the cost breakdown is computed for every year instead), ElasticityModel, PowGenCostsOldNew, UseElasticityModelPowerDemandInEngineerModel. The final deficit to gas (Investment) is a global setting, not a scenario input.

4.2 Order of the calculation within a year and across years

The power block runs once per year, inside the year loop, after the GDP level of the year and before fuel use. A few rows that depend only on prices and policies are computed for all years before the loop. Everything else is computed year by year, and the chain is free of circular references because two links are lagged by one year.

The methodology summarises the algorithm in section 3.4.2.1.

Solid arrows run within year \(t\); dotted arrows carry a value of year \(t\) into year \(t+1\): investment becomes capacity, the average generation cost becomes the supply cost of next year’s prices, and the prices drive next year’s demand.

Within year \(t\) the order is:

  1. Demand (H). Electricity demand of year \(t\) from last year’s demand, this year’s GDP growth and the change in last year’s electricity prices. Required generation follows.
  2. Capacity (K1). Capacity of year \(t\) = capacity of \(t-1\) less retirement of \(t-1\) plus investment of \(t-1\). Capacity bought in year \(t\) is available in \(t+1\).
  3. Dispatch (I). Generation by type from capacity, required generation and the variable costs of year \(t\).
  4. Storage (J). The VRE share of generation sets the marginal storage cost of year \(t\).
  5. Costs (B, C, E). Fixed costs of the existing stock (vintages) and of new plants; the average generation cost and the average policy cost per kWh.
  6. Prices (G). End-user prices of year \(t\) use the average generation cost of \(t-1\).
  7. Retirement (K). Retirement of year \(t\), including early coal retirement, which compares the coal variable cost with the wind and solar investment costs of year \(t\).
  8. Investment (L). Investment of year \(t\) fills the gap between expected requirement and capacity after retirement, allocated by a logit over the levelised costs of year \(t\). Its share in the stock, \(\Phi\), feeds the vintage averages of \(t+1\).
  9. Revenues (M). Power revenues of year \(t\).

The levelised variable cost of year \(t\) averages the variable cost from \(t\) to the end of the horizon. Variable costs depend only on prices and policies, not on the generation mix, so it is computed before the year loop, backwards from the last year.

Two consequences shape the design:

  1. End-user prices depend on generation costs only through the weighted average (E) and the transmission add-on. Variable, amortised and storage costs matter for prices only as far as they enter that average.
  2. The systems cost of renewables is an interaction inside the block. The VRE share sets the marginal storage cost, which enters the cost of wind, solar and other renewables twice: amortised, through vintages, in the cost of the existing stock (B), which reaches end-user prices; and levelised, in the cost of new plants (C), which drives investment. A scenario that adds renewables therefore raises their storage cost per kWh, which feeds back into the average generation cost and, a year later, into prices.

4.3 Electricity demand and required generation

Electricity demand grows with GDP, falls with efficiency gains and responds to the electricity price of the previous year. It is computed for 15 subsectors, grouped into four demand groups that share elasticities and a price. Required generation adds what the grid needs on top of final demand: net exports, losses, own use and statistical differences.

The methodology describes this in section 3.4.2.3, with the general fuel-demand equation of section 3.3.3. The model follows the corrected form: the GDP elasticity is \(\epsilon_Y\), adjusted for the income level (corrections, DOC4).

4.3.1 Demand groups and the demand price

Each subsector belongs to one demand group. The group’s price is a blend of last year’s residential and industrial end-user prices:

\[ \bar{p}_{d,t} = w_d\, p^T_{res,t-1} + (1 - w_d)\, p^T_{ind,t-1} \tag{4.1}\]

where

  • \(\bar{p}_{d,t}\) is the electricity price seen by demand group \(d\) in year \(t\) (USD/kWh; pdem);
  • \(p^T_{g,t-1}\) is the end-user price of user group \(g\) of the previous year (USD/kWh; rp, End-user prices);
  • \(w_d\) is the weight of the residential price: 0.5 for transport, 1 for residential, 0 for industry and services.

In the base year the price is that of the year before (2021) from the price data. Using last year’s price is what keeps demand free of a circular reference with this year’s price (link I6).

Demand group \(d\) Subsectors \(s\) Weight \(w_d\)
Transport (tra) road, rail, domestic aviation, domestic navigation 0.5
Residential (res) residential 1
Industry (ind) food and forestry, mining and chemicals, iron and steel, non-ferrous metals, machinery, cement, other manufacturing, construction, other energy use 0
Services (srv) services 0

4.3.2 The demand multiple

Demand grows each year by a multiple of four factors: efficiency, GDP, price and an optional shock. It is built in two steps.

The efficiency multiple combines the autonomous efficiency gain of the group and any additional efficiency gain of the scenario:

\[ a_{d} = \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha_d + \alpha^{+}}\right)^{1 + \epsilon_{U,d}} \tag{4.2}\]

The demand multiple of year \(t\) is then

\[ M_{d,t} = a_{d}\, \left(1 + \gamma_t\right)^{\epsilon_{Y,d}\, inc_t}\, \left(\frac{\bar{p}_{d,t}}{\bar{p}_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{U,d} + \epsilon_{F,d} + \epsilon_{U,d}\epsilon_{F,d}}\, \left(1 + \Psi_t\, \sigma\, \psi\right) \tag{4.3}\]

where

  • \(a_d\) is the efficiency multiple (aeef);
  • \(\alpha_d\) is the autonomous annual efficiency improvement of electricity in group \(d\) (share per year) and \(\alpha^{+}\) the additional efficiency gain of the scenario (AdditionalEfficiencyPower, default 0);
  • \(\epsilon_{Y,d}\), \(\epsilon_{U,d}\), \(\epsilon_{F,d}\) are the income, usage and efficiency elasticities of electricity demand of the group (the country’s elasticities, Chapter 3);
  • \(\gamma_t\) is real GDP growth of year \(t\) after the GDP feedback (gdp.pos.pct, Chapter 7);
  • \(inc_t\) is the income factor on the GDP elasticity (inc.ela.adj; Year paths), 1 if IncElAdj is not Yes;
  • \(\Psi_t\) is the exogenous demand shock (shock): the legacy calibration for 2023 and 2024, 0 in other years;
  • \(\sigma\) is the global switch that applies the shock (Excel-AI Settings C19, on by default) and \(\psi\) the scenario input EngineerModelProportionOfCovidFactor.

The multiple is 1 in the base year. The price term uses the total price elasticity \(\epsilon_{U} + \epsilon_{F} + \epsilon_{U}\epsilon_{F}\), the product of the usage and efficiency responses. This is the methodology’s equation with its two price terms combined: \((p_t/p_{t-1})^{\epsilon_U}\,(p_t/p_{t-1})^{\epsilon_F(1+\epsilon_U)}\). The equation list has it as (M4.1).

4.3.3 Demand by subsector

Each subsector’s demand grows with the multiple of its group:

\[ E_{s,t} = E_{s,t-1}\, M_{d(s),t}, \qquad E_{s,t_0} = \text{base-year data} \tag{4.4}\]

where \(E_{s,t}\) is electricity demand of subsector \(s\) (ktoe; dem) and \(d(s)\) its demand group. Total demand is \(E_t = \sum_s E_{s,t}\) (demtot), and demand by group (demg) is a memo.

  • Base year. Demand by subsector comes from the energy balance (flow ecy of the subsector’s balance flow) or, if BalancesOrConsumption is Consumption, from the CPAT energy consumption data.
  • Road. Where the road engineer model is on (Chapter 5), road electricity demand is taken from it instead of the multiple.
  • Memo lines. Electricity demand by subsector, by sector and in total is shown in the sector blocks; it is not part of the fuel totals.

4.3.4 Required generation

Generation must cover final demand plus net exports, transmission and distribution losses, statistical differences and transfers, and the energy industry’s own use. The model holds each of these at its base-year share of final demand:

\[ req_t = \rho\, E_t, \qquad \rho = \left(1 + s_{nxp} + s_{tsd} + s_{eou} + s_{add}\right) \frac{\theta_g \cdot 1000}{H} \tag{4.5}\]

where

  • \(req_t\) is required generation (MWy; req);
  • \(\rho\) is MWy of generation per ktoe of final demand (req_factor; \(\theta_g \cdot 1000/H = 1.3276\) MWy per ktoe);
  • \(s_{nxp}\), \(s_{tsd}\), \(s_{eou}\) are the base-year shares of net exports, of transmission, distribution and statistical differences, and of own use in final electricity demand; \(s_{add}\) is the residual that makes the sum equal base-year generation over base-year demand (Parameters).

In the base year, required generation is actual generation, \(req_{t_0} = \sum_f gen_{f,t_0}\). Because \(1 + s_{nxp} + s_{tsd} + s_{eou} + s_{add}\) is base-year generation over base-year demand, the two definitions agree in the base year. The equation list has this as (M4.2).

4.4 Generation costs

What a kWh costs depends on the question asked. The methodology therefore uses four cost concepts (section 3.4.1.3), and the model computes each of them by generation type:

4.4.1 Inputs of the scenario

Sub-table A0 applies the power inputs of the scenario to the power data of the country (Power data). The results are constant over the years of a scenario.

Capacity factor. The actual base-year capacity factor is used when it lies strictly inside the bounds of the scenario; otherwise the value of the studies is used:

\[ cf_f = \begin{cases} cf^{a}_f & \text{if } cf^{lo}_f < cf^{a}_f < cf^{hi} \\ cf^{s}_f & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{4.6}\]

where \(cf^a_f\) is base-year generation over base-year installed capacity, \(cf^s_f\) the studies value, \(cf^{lo}_f\) = CFOverrideIfBelowWndSol (default 0.1) for wind and solar and CFOverrideIfBelowFosOth (0.01) for the others, and \(cf^{hi}\) = CFOverrideIfAbove (1). Code cfu.

Efficiency. The actual efficiency (base-year output over input) is used unless it is below MinimumThermalEfficiency (default 0.1) or there is no input; then the studies value is used. Wind, solar, hydro and other renewables have efficiency 1 by convention. Code \(\nu_f\), nuu.

WACC. The option WACCSource of the scenario chooses the discount rate:

\[ wacc_f = \begin{cases} w_c + adj_{coal,i(c)} & \text{Country*} \\ w_c + adj_{f,i(c)} & \text{Tech} \\ \max\left(w^{user}, w^{min}\right) & \text{User} \\ \max\left(w_{i(c)}, w^{min}\right) & \text{Income*} \\ \max\left(w^{s}_f, w^{min}\right) & \text{otherwise (studies)} \end{cases} \tag{4.7}\]

where \(w_c\) is the country’s real WACC (cost-of-capital data, 2022), \(adj_{f,i}\) the technology premium of type \(f\) for the country’s income group \(i(c)\), \(w^{user}\) = WACCUserGlobal (default 0.075), \(w_i\) the WACC of the income group (HIC 0.075, UMIC 0.10, LMIC 0.125, LIC 0.15), \(w^s_f\) the studies value and \(w^{min}\) = MinimumWACC (0.01). The default is Tech. Note that Country* adds the coal premium to every type. Code wac; the WACC is constant over time (WACC).

Discounted lifetime. The annuity factor turns a capital cost into a cost per year of life:

\[ dlf_f = \frac{1 - (1 + wacc_f)^{-N_f}}{1 - (1 + wacc_f)^{-1}} = \sum_{k=0}^{N_f - 1} (1 + wacc_f)^{-k} \tag{4.8}\]

where \(N_f\) is the lifetime (years; the methodology’s \(lif_f\)). Code dlf. Renewables’ storage costs use \(dlf_{ren} = (dlf_{wnd} + dlf_{sol})/2\) (dlfr).

Fuel per kWh. \(k_f = \theta_e/\nu_f\) GJ of fuel per kWh generated for types with a fuel price (coal, gas, oil, biomass), 0 for the others (kwh). The CO2 intensity of generation is \(e_f k_f\) tCO2 per kWh, with \(e_f\) the calibrated CO2 emission factor of the fuel in power (tCO2/GJ, Chapter 6).

Base-year capacity. \(cap_{f,t_0} = gen_{f,t_0}/cf_f\) (MW; cap0): the capacity consistent with base-year generation at the capacity factor used.

4.4.2 Capital cost of new plants

The capital cost of a new plant follows the capex time factor of its type:

\[ cax_{f,t} = cax^0_f\, \tau_{f,t} \tag{4.9}\]

where \(cax^0_f\) is the capital cost at time factor 1 (USD/kW) and \(\tau_{f,t}\) the capex time factor of the decline scenario (tcf): falling for wind and solar, 1 for coal, gas, oil and nuclear (medium scenario), and the scenario’s path for hydro, other renewables and biomass (Capex time factors). Code cax.

4.4.3 Amortised fixed costs of the existing stock

The existing stock is a mix of plants built in different years at different capital costs. The model tracks the average capital cost of the stock, and the average storage cost attached to it, as vintage averages: each year the new plants of last year enter the average with their share in the stock.

\[ cax^{av}_{f,t} = cax^{av}_{f,t-1}\,(1 - \Phi_{f,t-1}) + cax_{f,t-1}\, \Phi_{f,t-1}, \qquad cax^{av}_{f,t_0} = cax_{f,t_0} \tag{4.10}\]

\[ sto^{av}_{f,t} = sto^{av}_{f,t-1}\,(1 - \Phi_{f,t-1}) + \ell_f\, msc_t\, \Phi_{f,t-1}, \qquad sto^{av}_{f,t_0} = \ell_f\, msc_{t_0} \tag{4.11}\]

where

  • \(cax^{av}_{f,t}\) is the weighted average capital cost of the stock (USD/kW; caxav);
  • \(sto^{av}_{f,t}\) the weighted average storage cost of the stock (USD/kWh; stoav);
  • \(\Phi_{f,t-1}\) the new investment of last year as a share of the stock after retirements (phi, Equation 4.55);
  • \(\ell_f\) the VRE flag (1 for wind, solar, other renewables);
  • \(msc_t\) the marginal storage cost of the current year (Equation 4.60). New vintages take the storage cost of the year they join the stock.

The amortised fixed cost per kWh then adds capital amortisation, average interest, decommissioning, storage and fixed O&M:

\[ fix_{f,t} = \underbrace{\frac{cax^{av}_{f,t}}{cf_f\,H\,N_f}}_{\text{amortisation}} + \underbrace{\frac{\bar{s}\; cax^{av}_{f,t}\; wacc_f}{cf_f\,H}}_{\text{interest}} + \underbrace{\frac{dtc_f}{cf_f\,H\,N_f}}_{\text{decommissioning}} + sto^{av}_{f,t} + opf_f \tag{4.12}\]

where

  • \(fix_{f,t}\) is the amortised fixed cost (USD/kWh; fix);
  • \(\bar{s} = 0.5\) is the average outstanding share of capital under straight-line repayment (CapAvgShr), so the interest term is the methodology’s \(0.5\, cax^{av}\, wacc/(cf \cdot 8760)\);
  • \(dtc_f\) the decommissioning (and, for nuclear, waste disposal) cost (USD/kW), not discounted;
  • \(opf_f\) the fixed O&M cost per kWh, fixed O&M per kW-year over \(cf_f H\).

This is the methodology’s \(fix_B = acc + int + dec + sto + opf\) (section 3.4.1.6).

4.4.4 Levelised fixed costs of new plants

A new plant’s fixed costs are levelised over its discounted lifetime. The levelised fixed cost adds the capital cost, decommissioning and a share of transmission capex, all annuitised, plus fixed O&M, the renewable subsidy and the current marginal storage cost:

\[ fix^{fl}_{f,t} = \frac{cax_{f,t} + dtc_f + \kappa\, tcx_f}{cf_f\,H\,dlf_f} + opf_f + rns_{f,t} + \ell_f\, msc_t \tag{4.13}\]

where

  • \(fix^{fl}_{f,t}\) is the forward-looking levelised fixed cost (USD/kWh; lfx);
  • \(tcx_f\) the levelised transmission capex (USD/kW) and \(\kappa\) = TransmissionCostShare the share of it included (default 1; legacy IncludeTransmissionCostsInGenerationCostsPercent);
  • \(rns_{f,t}\) the renewable subsidy per kWh, negative for a subsidy (Equation 4.14).

This is the methodology’s \(fix^{fl}\) with \(sto = msc\), \(pusRen = rns\) and the levelised transmission cost as the \(tcx\) term (section 3.4.1.5). Because every component is constant in real terms over the plant’s life, the levelised cost is the cost of one year divided by one year’s output: the methodology’s constant-growth simplification (3.4.1.5.2) with \(\tau = 0\).

4.4.5 Renewable subsidies

A scenario can pay a subsidy per kWh to renewable generation. A base subsidy applies in every scenario; an additional subsidy applies in policy scenarios from its own start year and phases out linearly:

\[ rns_t = -\left( s^{BL} + \mathbb{1}[o \ne B,\ t \ge T_s]\; s^{add} \max\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{t - T_s}{n_s}\right)\right) \iota_t \tag{4.14}\]

\[ rns_{f,t} = h_f\, rns_t \tag{4.15}\]

where

  • \(rns_t\) is the subsidy per kWh (USD/kWh, negative = subsidy; rnsp) and \(rns_{f,t}\) by type (rns);
  • \(h_f\) is 1 for wind, solar, other renewables and biomass; for hydro 1 if RenewableSubsidyHydro = Yes, else 0; 0 for the other types;
  • \(s^{BL}\) = RenewableSubsidyBL (default 0), \(s^{add}\) = RenSubsidyAddUSDkwh, \(T_s\) = RenSubsidyAddStartYear (default 2027), \(n_s\) = RenSubsidyAddPhaseoutYrs;
  • \(\iota_t\) the CPI index: the inputs are nominal and are converted to real (Reforms).

The subsidy enters the levelised fixed cost (investment), not the cost of the existing stock and not the end-user price. Its fiscal cost is \(rns_{f,t}\) times generation by type (Power revenues). The equation list has it in (A4.7).

4.4.6 Current variable costs

The variable cost is what a kWh costs to run: fuel, variable O&M, existing carbon pricing, and the new policies of the scenario. It drives dispatch and, levelised, investment. Variable costs depend only on prices and policies, so they are computed for all years before the year loop. The methodology describes them in section 3.4.1.4, with the fuel price before new policies as in the corrected text (corrections, DOC12).

Fuel price for power. The price is the retail price before new policies of the type’s price fuel, averaged over the year and the four years before, within the model’s years:

\[ \bar{p}_{f,t} = \frac{1}{t - \max(t_0, t - 4) + 1} \sum_{\tau = \max(t_0,\,t-4)}^{t} p_{f,\tau} \tag{4.16}\]

where \(p_{f,t}\) is the retail price before new policies of the price fuel of type \(f\) (USD/GJ; fpn; Chapter 2) and \(\bar{p}_{f,t}\) the price used (fpp). The average spans one year in 2022 and five from 2026. With spot prices switched on, \(\bar{p}_{f,t} = p_{f,t}\).

Change in existing carbon pricing. The price data already contain the existing carbon tax and ETS price of the data year. A change in either after that year changes the cost per kWh:

\[ \Delta xcp_{f,t} = e_f\, k_f \left( XCT_{f,t}\,\phi^{ct}_t + XETS_{f,t}\,\phi^{ets}_t - XCP^0_f \right) \tag{4.17}\]

where \(XCT\) and \(XETS\) are the existing carbon tax and ETS price of power and the type’s fuel (USD/tCO2), weighted by their coverage, \(\phi^{ct}\), \(\phi^{ets}\) their factors of the year, and \(XCP^0_f\) the existing pricing embedded in the price data (Chapter 2). Code xck; 0 for Egypt.

Variable cost before new policies.

\[ vc_{f,t} = vop_f + ffx_f + \bar{p}_{f,t}\, k_f + \Delta xcp_{f,t} + \mathbb{1}[f = \text{coal}]\; ipc_t \tag{4.18}\]

where \(vop_f\) is variable O&M (USD/kWh), \(ffx_f\) the fixed fuel cost (nuclear 0.004 USD/kWh, others 0), and \(ipc_t\) the coal implicit cost (Coal implicit cost). Code vbc. This is the methodology’s \(vc = vop + p/\nu + ipc\).

New policy cost per kWh. The carbon tax, the ETS and the fuel price reform of the scenario, per kWh generated:

\[ ncp_{f,t} = e_f\, k_f \left( T_{pow,t}\; cov_{f,t} + A_{pow,t} \right) + \frac{F_{f,t}\, k_f}{u_f} \tag{4.19}\]

where

  • \(T_{pow,t}\) is the carbon tax rate of power (carbon price × sector coverage × (1 − ETS coverage), USD/tCO2; ctr) and \(cov_{f,t}\) the fuel coverage of the carbon tax;
  • \(A_{pow,t}\) the ETS cost rate of power (tax-equivalent ETS price × ETS coverage × (1 − OBR share); etr);
  • \(F_{f,t}\) the new tax of the fuel price reform of the type’s price fuel (price units; ntxb), \(u_f\) GJ per price unit.

Code xac. The policy rates are those of Chapter 1; the same rates apply to the fuel blocks.

Shadow price and rebate. Feebates on power and the output-based part of an ETS act as a shadow price: they change the choice between generation types without raising the average cost. The shadow price per kWh is

\[ sh_{f,t} = \Sigma_{pow,t}\; e_f\, k_f \tag{4.20}\]

where \(\Sigma_{pow,t}\) is the shadow price of power (feebate rate × share impacting efficiency + tax-equivalent ETS price × ETS coverage × OBR share; shpx). The rebate returns it in proportion to base-year generation shares, per kWh generated:

\[ reb_t = -\sum_f sh_{f,t}\; s_{f,t_0}, \qquad reb_{t_0} = 0 \tag{4.21}\]

where \(s_{f,t_0}\) is the base-year generation share. Codes shk, reb.

Total variable cost. Dispatch uses

\[ tvc_{f,t} = vc_{f,t} + ncp_{f,t} + sh_{f,t} + reb_t \tag{4.22}\]

(USD/kWh; tvc). It is the methodology’s \(vc_P = vc_B + ncp\), plus the shadow price and its rebate.

Levelised variable cost. Investment needs the expected variable cost over a plant’s life. The model takes the discounted average of the total variable cost from the year to the end of the horizon:

\[ \overline{vc}_{f,t} = \frac{\sum_{\tau = t}^{T} \beta_{f,\tau}\, tvc_{f,\tau}}{\sum_{\tau = t}^{T} \beta_{f,\tau}}, \qquad \beta_{f,\tau} = (1 + wacc_f)^{-(\tau - t_0)} \tag{4.23}\]

(USD/kWh; lvc). It is computed backwards from \(T\) with the sum of discount factors \(D_{f,t} = \sum_{\tau \ge t} \beta_{f,\tau}\) (dsum):

\[ \overline{vc}_{f,t} = \frac{\beta_{f,t}\, tvc_{f,t} + \left(D_{f,t} - \beta_{f,t}\right)\overline{vc}_{f,t+1}}{D_{f,t}} \tag{4.24}\]

This is the methodology’s levelised variable cost with the corrected discount factor (corrections, DOC3); the capacity factor of the methodology’s weights is constant and cancels.

Levelised investment cost.

\[ tic_{f,t} = \max\left(fix^{fl}_{f,t} + \overline{vc}_{f,t},\ c_{min}\right) \tag{4.25}\]

(USD/kWh; tic). The equation list has \(vc\) and \(tic\) as (M4.3) and the full variable cost as (A4.5).

4.4.7 Total costs

The cost-recovery generation cost of a type adds its amortised fixed cost and its variable cost before new policies:

\[ gnc_{f,t} = fix_{f,t} + vc_{f,t} - \mathbb{1}[f = \text{coal},\ \texttt{PwCoalICTariff} = \text{No}]\; ipc_t \tag{4.26}\]

The average generation cost weights it by generation shares, and the average new policy cost per kWh consumed adds the losses and other uses between generation and consumption:

\[ gnc^{av}_t = \sum_f s_{f,t}\, gnc_{f,t}, \qquad ncp^{av}_t = \frac{G_{t_0}}{D_{t_0}} \sum_f s_{f,t}\, ncp_{f,t} \tag{4.27}\]

where

  • \(gnc_{f,t}\) is the generation cost before new policies (USD/kWh; gnc), the methodology’s \(gnc_B = vc_B + fix\);
  • \(s_{f,t}\) the generation share of type \(f\) (Dispatch);
  • \(gnc^{av}_t\) the average generation cost (gncav);
  • \(ncp^{av}_t\) the average new policy cost per kWh consumed (ccpav), with \(G_{t_0}/D_{t_0}\) base-year generation over base-year final demand (gen_per_cons, Egypt 1.266).

Carbon and other new policy costs are kept out of the average generation cost and added separately to end-user prices. This lets a rebate be computed against them (methodology 3.4.1.7).

4.5 Dispatch

Dispatch decides how much each plant generates this year. Plants that cannot or need not follow costs (renewables, nuclear, oil, biomass) run at their capacity factor. Coal and gas, the flexible plants, cover the rest, shared by their variable costs. Contracts that oblige coal and gas plants to run (PPAs) override part of that choice.

The methodology describes this in section 3.4.2.4.2, with the logit function of 3.4.2.4.1.

4.5.1 Capacity of the year

Capacity is the stock of the previous year, less its retirements, plus its investment:

\[ cap_{f,t} = cap_{f,t-1} - ret_{f,t-1} + inv_{f,t-1}, \qquad cap_{f,t_0} = gen_{f,t_0}/cf_f \tag{4.28}\]

where \(cap_{f,t}\) is nameplate capacity (MW; cap), \(ret\) retirement and \(inv\) new nameplate investment (MW; Capacity and retirement, Investment). Effective capacity, the generation the capacity is expected to deliver, is

\[ cap^e_{f,t} = cf_f\, cap_{f,t} \tag{4.29}\]

(MW; effcap). This is the methodology’s stock equation in capacity, with no sum over types (corrections, DOC11) and the equation list’s (M4.5).

4.5.2 Fixed-output types and the coal and gas requirement

Wind, solar, hydro, other renewables, oil, nuclear and biomass generate their effective capacity: \(gen_{f,t} = cap^e_{f,t}\) for \(f \notin CG\). Coal and gas must cover what is left of required generation:

\[ req^{cg}_t = \max\left(req_t - \sum_{f \notin CG} cap^e_{f,t},\ 0\right) \tag{4.30}\]

(MWy; reqcg).

4.5.3 Minimum runs and the logit share

Each of coal and gas can generate at most its capacity at the maximum capacity factor:

\[ g^{max}_{f,t} = \mu_f\, cap_{f,t}, \qquad f \in CG \tag{4.31}\]

where \(\mu_{coal}\) = MaximumCoalCF and \(\mu_{gas}\) = MaximumGasCF (both 0.9 by default). Code gmx. Note that the maximum applies to nameplate capacity: it lets coal and gas run above their base-year capacity factor when needed.

Each type first gets the minimum it must supply when the other runs at its maximum:

\[ g^{min}_{f,t} = \min\left(\max\left(req^{cg}_t - \sum_{j \in CG,\ j \ne f} g^{max}_{j,t},\ 0\right),\ g^{max}_{f,t}\right) \tag{4.32}\]

(gmin). The remainder is shared by a logit on relative variable costs:

\[ rem_t = \max\left(req^{cg}_t - \sum_{f \in CG} g^{min}_{f,t},\ 0\right) \tag{4.33}\]

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{f,t} &= \frac{e^{-K_{dispatch} H_{f,t}}}{\sum_{j \in CG} e^{-K_{dispatch} H_{j,t}}} \\ H_{f,t} &= \frac{\max(tvc_{f,t},\ c_{min})}{vc^{lc}_t}, \qquad vc^{lc}_t = \max\left(\min_{j \in CG} tvc_{j,t},\ c_{min}\right) \end{aligned} \tag{4.34}\]

where

  • \(rem_t\) is coal and gas generation left after the minima (MWy; rem);
  • \(\lambda_{f,t}\) the logit share of type \(f\) (lgd is its weight \(e^{-K H}\));
  • \(K_{dispatch}\) = kDispatch (default 2) the steepness of the logit;
  • \(H_{f,t}\) the variable cost relative to the cheapest of coal and gas, \(vc^{lc}_t\) (vcmin).

The equation list has it as (M4.4). Generation before PPAs is the minimum plus the logit share of the remainder, capped at the maximum:

\[ gen^{l}_{f,t} = \min\left(g^{max}_{f,t},\ g^{min}_{f,t} + \lambda_{f,t}\, rem_t\right), \qquad f \in CG \tag{4.35}\]

(glg).

4.5.4 Power purchase agreements

A share of coal and gas generation can be under power purchase agreements (PPAs): it runs at its capacity factor, scaled to the need, whatever its cost. The PPA share of the year phases out linearly after a start year if the scenario says so:

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_t &= \omega^0\, \phi^{ppa}_t \\ \phi^{ppa}_t &= \begin{cases} \max\left(0,\ 1 - \max(0,\ t - T_p)/n_p\right) & \text{phase-out switch Yes or Yes*} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \tag{4.36}\]

where \(\phi^{ppa}_t\) is the phase-out factor, \(\omega^0\) = PPAProportionCoalGas (default 0), \(T_p\) = YearPPACoalGas, \(n_p\) = YearsToPhaseOutPPAs, and the switch is PhaseOutCoalAndGasPPAsBaseline in scenario 1 and PhaseOutCoalAndGasPPAsPolicy in the other scenarios. Code ppa.

The PPA part runs at effective capacity scaled by the ratio of the coal and gas need to their effective capacity:

\[ \Omega_t = \begin{cases} \dfrac{req^{cg}_t}{\sum_{j \in CG} cap^e_{j,t}} & \sum_{j \in CG} cap^e_{j,t} > 0 \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{4.37}\]

\[ gen_{f,t} = (1 - \omega_t)\, gen^l_{f,t} + \omega_t\, \Omega_t\, cap^e_{f,t}, \qquad f \in CG \tag{4.38}\]

where \(\Omega_t\) is the downscaling factor (ppr) and \(gen_{f,t}\) generation (MWy; gen_mwy). In the base year generation is the data: \(gen_{f,t_0}\) = base-year output of the energy balance. This is the methodology’s \(\gamma = (1-\omega)\tilde\gamma + \omega\,\Omega\,(cap \cdot cf)\).

PPAs also protect coal plants from early retirement (Early coal retirement).

4.5.5 Generation shares, fuel use and CO2

Total generation, shares, fuel use and CO2 follow:

\[ \begin{aligned} G_t &= \sum_f gen_{f,t}, \qquad s_{f,t} = \frac{gen_{f,t}}{G_t} \\ FU_{f,t} &= \frac{8.76\; gen_{f,t}}{\theta_g\, \nu_f}, \qquad CO2_{f,t} = 0.041868\; FU_{f,t}\, EF_f \end{aligned} \tag{4.39}\]

where

  • \(G_t\) is total generation (MWy; gtot) and \(s_{f,t}\) the generation share (shr);
  • \(FU_{f,t}\) fuel use (primary energy) of generation (ktoe; pe): MWy × 8.76 gives GWh, divided by 11.63 GWh per ktoe and by the efficiency;
  • \(CO2_{f,t}\) CO2 from combustion (MtCO2; co2), with \(EF_f\) the calibrated emission factor (tCO2/GJ; Chapter 6).

Only coal, gas, oil and biomass enter the fuel totals (pet); nuclear, wind, solar, hydro and other renewables have no fuel. The increase in total generation, \(\Delta G_t = \max(0, G_t - G_{t-1})\) (0 in the base year; dgen), is used, lagged, by early coal retirement.

4.6 Capacity, retirement and capacity needed

Each year part of the stock retires: plants reach the end of their lives, coal plants may follow a closure schedule, and coal plants may close early when wind and solar are cheaper than running them. What retires, together with growth in demand, sets how much new capacity is needed.

The methodology describes this in section 3.4.2.4.3. The capacity stock itself is Equation 4.28.

4.6.1 Retirement

Retirement of a type is either its planned retirement (data) or a fixed share of its capacity; coal adds early retirement; and nothing retires beyond the capacity there is:

\[ \begin{aligned} ret_{f,t} &= \min\left(b_{f,t} + \mathbb{1}[f = \text{coal}]\; early_t,\ cap_{f,t}\right) \\ b_{f,t} &= \begin{cases} pret_{f,t} & \text{planned retirement used for } f \\ r_f\, cap_{f,t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \tag{4.40}\]

where

  • \(ret_{f,t}\) is retirement of nameplate capacity (MW; ret);
  • \(pret_{f,t}\) the planned retirement of the data, in nameplate MW per year (pret), used where the type’s planned-retirement switch is Yes;
  • \(r_f = 1/N_f\) the retirement rate (0 for hydro if HydroDoesntRetire = Yes): retirement is exponential;
  • \(early_t\) early coal retirement (Equation 4.43).

Effective retirement is \(ret^e_{f,t} = cf_f\, ret_{f,t}\) (rec), so it is capped like the nameplate retirement. The equation list has the rate-based form in (M4.5).

The planned-retirement data come from the coal plant tracker (plant-level retirement years, plants cancelled, shelved or mothballed removed), averaged over five years (Year paths). The data exist for coal only; the switch exists for every type. Coal is switched on where its averaged planned retirement over 2020-2030 is at least 0.1 MW per year (the data flag of the country).

4.6.2 Early coal retirement

Coal plants may retire before the end of their lives when the variable cost of running them exceeds the full cost of replacing them with new wind and solar. The model builds this in three steps: how much coal wind and solar could replace this year, what share of it the costs favour, and the resulting nameplate retirement.

Step 1: room for early retirement. Wind and solar can grow by at most their scale-up limits (Equation 4.46). Part of that growth is already needed for last year’s increase in generation and to replace last year’s retirements of the other types. What is left is the room for replacing coal:

\[ \begin{aligned} room_t &= \max\left(0,\ iv^{max}_{wnd,t} + iv^{max}_{sol,t} - \Delta G_{t-1} - \sum_{f \ne \text{coal}} ret^e_{f,t-1}\right) \\ room_{t_0} &= \max\left(0,\ iv^{max}_{wnd,t_0} + iv^{max}_{sol,t_0}\right) \end{aligned} \tag{4.41}\]

where

  • \(room_t\) is the room for early coal retirement (effective MW; egrow);
  • \(iv^{max}_{f,t}\) the maximum effective investment (scale-up limit) of wind and solar (Equation 4.46);
  • \(\Delta G_{t-1}\) the increase in total generation of last year (dgen);
  • \(ret^e_{f,t-1}\) last year’s effective retirements of the types other than coal.

Step 2: the share that costs favour. A logit compares the coal variable cost with the levelised investment costs of wind and solar, each relative to the lowest of the three:

\[ \begin{aligned} s^{ec}_t &= \frac{e^{-K_i\, tvc_{coal,t}/c^{lo}_t}} {e^{-K_i\, tvc_{coal,t}/c^{lo}_t} + e^{-K_i\, tic_{wnd,t}/c^{lo}_t} + e^{-K_i\, tic_{sol,t}/c^{lo}_t}} \\ c^{lo}_t &= \min\left(tvc_{coal,t},\ tic_{wnd,t},\ tic_{sol,t}\right) \end{aligned} \tag{4.42}\]

where

  • \(s^{ec}_t\) is the coal share of the logit (ecshr): high when running coal is cheap compared with building wind and solar;
  • \(K_i\) = kInvestment (default 2);
  • \(c^{lo}_t\) the lowest of the three costs (ecmin).

Equivalently, \(s^{ec}_t = 1/\left(1 + e^{-K_i(tic_{wnd} - tvc_{coal})/c^{lo}} + e^{-K_i(tic_{sol} - tvc_{coal})/c^{lo}}\right)\), the form of the workbook. The share replaced, \(1 - s^{ec}_t\), is the methodology’s \(rep_{coa}\).

Step 3: early retirement. At most a fixed share \(\bar{e}\) of coal’s effective capacity can be replaced in a year. The replaced effective capacity is converted to nameplate MW and scaled by the early-retirement factor; plants under PPA do not retire early:

\[ early_t = \frac{\max\left(0,\ \min\left(\bar{e}\, cap^e_{coal,t},\ room_t\right)\left(1 - s^{ec}_t\right)\right)}{cf_{coal}}\; f^{early}\,(1 - \omega_t) \tag{4.43}\]

where

  • \(early_t\) is cost-based early coal retirement (nameplate MW; early);
  • \(\bar{e}\) = 0.8 the maximum share of coal effective capacity (EarlyMaxShare);
  • \(cf_{coal}\) the coal capacity factor, which turns effective MW into nameplate MW;
  • \(f^{early}\) the early-retirement factor of the scenario (erf: MaxCostBasedEarlyRetirement, default 0.8, and 0 in scenario 1 unless EarlyRetirementInBaseline = Yes);
  • \(\omega_t\) the PPA share (Equation 4.36).

Early retirement enters coal retirement in Equation 4.40. The equation list has it as (A4.9).

4.6.3 Capacity needed

Capacity bought in year \(t\) comes online in \(t+1\). The investment of year \(t\) must therefore cover next year’s requirement. The model estimates it from this year’s growth, so that no later year is read, and subtracts the effective capacity left after this year’s retirements:

\[ K^{after}_t = \sum_f cap^e_{f,t} - \sum_f ret^e_{f,t} \tag{4.44}\]

\[ need_t = \max\left(req_t\, \frac{req_t}{req_{t-1}} - K^{after}_t,\ 0\right) \tag{4.45}\]

where \(K^{after}_t\) is effective capacity after this year’s retirements (MW; kafter) and \(need_t\) the effective capacity investment needed (MW; need). In the base year the growth ratio is 1. Investment is sized on this need (Investment).

4.7 Investment

Investment fills the capacity needed with new plants. The cheapest types by levelised cost take most of it, but no type can grow faster than its scale-up limit, and only types the scenario permits are built. What the first, cost-weighted round cannot place goes, in a second round, to the cheapest types with room left.

The methodology describes this in section 3.4.2.4.4.

4.7.1 Investment permitted and scale-up limits

A type can be built in year \(t\) when the scenario permits it and \(t\) has reached its first year of investment (\(\zeta_{f,t}\), Inputs of the scenario). The options of NewInv<type> are:

Option Meaning
If Present* (default) Permitted if the type has more than 0.1% of base-year capacity
Yes Permitted from the year NewInv<type>Year
No Never built
Manual Built along a capacity path typed by the user (Manual capacity); no logit investment

The scale-up limit caps each type’s new effective capacity at a share of total effective capacity:

\[ iv^{max}_{f,t} = q_f\, \zeta_{f,t} \sum_j cap^e_{j,t} \tag{4.46}\]

where \(iv^{max}_{f,t}\) is the maximum effective investment (MW; maxinv) and \(q_f\) the scale-up rate: 5% of total for coal, gas and hydro, 2% for oil, nuclear, other renewables and biomass, and the wind and solar rate of the scenario (country default 2%) (Inputs of the scenario). The limit is linear: it is a share of the system’s total, in gross additions, not net of retirements.

4.7.2 First round: the logit

The need, net of any manual investment, is shared among the permitted types by a logit on levelised investment costs relative to the cheapest permitted type:

\[ \lambda^{i}_{f,t} = \frac{\zeta_{f,t}\, e^{-K_i\, tic_{f,t}/tic^{lc}_t}}{\sum_j \zeta_{j,t}\, e^{-K_i\, tic_{j,t}/tic^{lc}_t}}, \qquad tic^{lc}_t = \max\left(\min_{j:\ \zeta_{j,t} = 1} tic_{j,t},\ c_{min}\right) \tag{4.47}\]

\[ inv^{(1)}_{f,t} = \min\left(iv^{max}_{f,t},\ need^m_t\, \lambda^i_{f,t}\right) \tag{4.48}\]

where

  • \(\lambda^i_{f,t}\) is the investment share of type \(f\) (pni; its weight is lgi);
  • \(tic^{lc}_t\) the lowest levelised investment cost of the permitted types (cmin);
  • \(K_i\) = kInvestment (default 2);
  • \(need^m_t\) the need net of manual investment (Equation 4.53);
  • \(inv^{(1)}_{f,t}\) new effective investment of the first round (MW; first).

This is the methodology’s \(ivp_f/\sum ivp = \zeta_f\, e^{-K_i H_i}/\sum e^{-K_i H_i}\) with \(H_i = tic/tic^{lc}\), and the equation list’s (M4.6).

4.7.3 Second round: cheapest first

What the scale-up limits leave unplaced is the deficit. It goes to the types with space left under their limits, cheapest first, each taking as much as its space allows:

\[ \begin{aligned} deficit_t &= \max\left(need^m_t - \sum_f inv^{(1)}_{f,t},\ 0\right) \\ space_{f,t} &= \left(iv^{max}_{f,t} - inv^{(1)}_{f,t}\right) \zeta_{f,t} \end{aligned} \tag{4.49}\]

\[ \begin{aligned} inv^{(2)}_{f,t} &= \zeta_{f,t}\, \max\left(0,\ \min\left(space_{f,t},\ deficit_t - S_{f,t}\right)\right) \\ S_{f,t} &= \sum_{j:\ tic_{j,t} + tb_j\, <\, tic_{f,t} + tb_f} space_{j,t} \end{aligned} \tag{4.50}\]

where

  • \(deficit_t\) is investment left for the second round (MW; deficit);
  • \(space_{f,t}\) the remaining space under the scale-up limit (MW; space);
  • \(S_{f,t}\) the space of all cheaper types;
  • \(tb_f\) a tie breaker, \(1.356 \times 10^{-6}\) USD/kWh times the type’s position minus one (coal 0, …, biomass 8), so that two types of equal cost are ranked in the type order (TieBreak);
  • \(inv^{(2)}_{f,t}\) new effective investment of the second round (MW; second).

A type takes what is left of the deficit after all cheaper types have filled their space.

4.7.4 Final deficit to gas

If even the second round cannot place the whole need (all permitted types at their limits), the rest is unplaced, unless the global setting PwDeficitGas is Yes (default No). Then it goes to gas:

\[ d^{gas}_t = \mathbb{1}[\texttt{PwDeficitGas} = \text{Yes}] \left(deficit_t - \sum_f inv^{(2)}_{f,t}\right) \tag{4.51}\]

(MW; dgas).

4.7.5 Manual capacity and investment

Where a scenario sets NewInv<type> = Manual, the type follows a capacity path typed by the user on the sheet ‘Manual inputs’ (one block for scenario 1, one for the other scenarios). It gets no logit investment (its permit is 0), and its investment replaces next year’s increase in the path plus this year’s retirement:

\[ inv^{m}_{f,t} = \max\left(0,\ cap^{man}_{f,t+1} - cap^{man}_{f,t} + ret_{f,t}\right) cf_f, \qquad inv^m_{f,T} = 0 \tag{4.52}\]

\[ need^m_t = \max\left(need_t - \sum_f inv^m_{f,t},\ 0\right) \tag{4.53}\]

where \(cap^{man}_{f,t}\) is the manual capacity path (MW; mancap; blank = 0), \(inv^m_{f,t}\) manual new effective investment (MW; mani) and \(need^m_t\) the need left for the logit and second rounds (needm). The methodology describes the option as “New Nameplate Investments = Capacity data entered by the user + Planned Retirement”.

4.7.6 New investment, its cost and its share in the stock

New effective investment sums the rounds; nameplate investment divides by the capacity factor:

\[ \begin{aligned} inv^e_{f,t} &= inv^{(1)}_{f,t} + inv^{(2)}_{f,t} + \mathbb{1}[f = \text{gas}]\; d^{gas}_t + inv^m_{f,t} \\ inv_{f,t} &= inv^e_{f,t}/cf_f \end{aligned} \tag{4.54}\]

The spending and the share of new plants in next year’s stock follow:

\[ Inv^{USD}_{f,t} = \frac{inv_{f,t}\, cax_{f,t}}{1000}, \qquad \Phi_{f,t} = \frac{inv_{f,t}}{cap_{f,t} + inv_{f,t} - ret_{f,t}} \tag{4.55}\]

where

  • \(inv^e_{f,t}\) is new effective investment (MW; nei) and \(inv_{f,t}\) new nameplate investment (MW; nim), which becomes capacity in \(t+1\) (Equation 4.28);
  • \(Inv^{USD}_{f,t}\) investment spending (USD million; nid) at the capital cost of the year;
  • \(\Phi_{f,t}\) new investment as a share of the stock after retirements (phi), used by the vintage averages of \(t+1\) (Equation 4.10).

Totals over types are nimtot and usdtot. The equation list has the second round in (A4.9).

4.7.7 PPAs and investment

PPAs do not enter the investment rounds directly. They act on the year’s dispatch (Equation 4.38) and keep coal plants from retiring early (Equation 4.43); the PPA share \(\omega_t\) is defined in Equation 4.36.

4.8 Systems integration cost of renewables: storage

The model has no hourly profiles, so it cannot see when wind and solar fall short. Instead it charges variable renewables a systems integration cost that rises with their share of generation: the cost of the storage the system needs at the margin. Short-term storage (batteries) is needed at any share; long-term storage (electrolysers and hydrogen tanks) only above a threshold, and it is costly.

The methodology describes this in section 3.4.2.5: short-term storage in 3.4.2.5.1, long-term storage in 3.4.2.5.2, and its levelised cost in 3.4.2.5.3.

4.8.1 VRE share

\[ v_t = \sum_f \ell_f\, s_{f,t} = s_{wnd,t} + s_{sol,t} + s_{ore,t} \tag{4.56}\]

(vre): the share of wind, solar and other renewables in generation of the year, from dispatch. Hydro and biomass are not variable renewables.

4.8.2 Short-term storage

Total short-term storage is a quadratic function of the VRE share, \(sst = h_{100}\, v^2\) hours of storage per kW of average generation, with \(h_{100}\) = 9 hours for a 100% VRE system. The marginal need is its derivative, \(d\,sst/dv = 2\, h_{100}\, v = 18\, v\) (corrections, DOC6). A share \(a_{st}\) of it is allocated to the power system. The cost of one marginal hour per kWh is

\[ c^{ph}_t = \frac{c^{bat}_t + c^{int}_t / r_{st}}{dlf_{ren}\, H} + \frac{o^{bat}_t}{H} \tag{4.57}\]

where

  • \(c^{ph}_t\) is the short-term storage cost per marginal hour (USD/kWh; cph);
  • \(c^{bat}_t\) the battery capital cost (USD/kWh of storage), \(c^{int}_t\) the battery interface capital cost (USD/kW), \(o^{bat}_t\) the battery fixed operating cost (USD/kWh/year) (Year paths);
  • \(r_{st}\) = StorageToInterfaceRatioSTStorage (default 2 hours): kWh of storage per kW of interface, a low, conservative ratio;
  • \(dlf_{ren}\) the discounted lifetime of renewables (average of wind and solar).

4.8.3 Long-term storage

Long-term storage is measured by its interface, the electrolysers (kW per kW of average generation). It is zero below a VRE share \(x\) and rises quadratically to \(u_{lt}\) at 100% VRE:

\[ slt = u_{lt}\left(\frac{\max(0,\ v - x)}{1 - x}\right)^2, \qquad \frac{d\,slt}{dv} = u_{lt}\, \frac{2 \max(0,\ v - x)}{(1 - x)^2} \tag{4.58}\]

where \(x\) = StartingPointWhenLTStorageIsNeeded (default 0.75) and \(u_{lt}\) = LongTermStorageFor100pcVRE (default 1). With the defaults the marginal units needed are:

VRE share \(v\) 75% or less 85% 90% 95% 100%
Marginal kW of electrolysers per kW 0 3.2 4.8 6.4 8.0

(corrections, DOC7). The cost of one marginal unit is

\[ c^{el}_t = \frac{c^{elx}_t + c^{tank}_t\, h_{lt}}{dlf_{ren}\, H} + \frac{o^{el}_t}{H} + v^{el}_t \tag{4.59}\]

where

  • \(c^{el}_t\) is the long-term storage cost per marginal unit (USD/kWh; cel);
  • \(c^{elx}_t\) the electrolyser capital cost (USD/kW), \(c^{tank}_t\) the storage tank capital cost (USD/kWh), \(o^{el}_t\) the electrolyser fixed opex (USD/kW/year), \(v^{el}_t\) its variable opex (USD/kWh);
  • \(h_{lt}\) = StorageHoursForLTStorage (default 1,000 hours of storage per kW).

4.8.4 Marginal storage cost

The marginal storage cost of variable renewables adds the two:

\[ msc_t = a_{st}\, 2\, h_{100}\, v_t\; c^{ph}_t + \frac{2 \max(0,\ v_t - x)}{(1 - x)^2}\; c^{el}_t\; a_{lt}\, u_{lt} \tag{4.60}\]

where

  • \(msc_t\) is the marginal storage cost (USD/kWh; msc), charged to wind, solar and other renewables, 0 for the other types;
  • \(a_{st}\) = AllocateSTStorageToVRE (default 1) and \(h_{100}\) = TotalHoursStorageFor100pcVRE (default 9); their product with 2 is the row stm;
  • \(a_{lt}\) = AllocateLTStorageToVRE (default 0.33), the share of long-term storage allocated to the power system (batteries and hydrogen have other uses); \(a_{lt} u_{lt}\) is the row ltu.

The marginal storage cost enters the cost of renewables in two places: the vintage storage cost of the existing stock (Equation 4.11), which reaches the average generation cost and prices, and the levelised fixed cost of new plants (Equation 4.13), which drives investment. The storage-inclusive cost of renewables can therefore rise over time as their share grows, even while their capital cost falls. The system is assumed to have invested optimally in the storage of the current VRE share.

4.9 End-user electricity prices

Electricity prices start from observed retail prices and then move with the modelled cost of supply. Up to the last historical price year \(L\) the price is the data. After \(L\), the price before new policies changes year by year with the supply cost and with the subsidy components the model tracks, passed through to users at the scenario’s pass-through rate. New policy costs and excises are then added on top, with VAT.

The methodology describes this as concept 4 in section 3.4.1.7. Its markup \(mu^T = hrp - gnc^{av} - tmc\), with the corrected sign (corrections, DOC2), is the gap that the model holds through the subsidy components below, so that the base-year price equals the historical price.

There are two user groups, residential and industry; industry stands for all non-residential users.

4.9.1 Consumption and supply cost

Consumption of each group is its electricity demand in GWh:

\[ cons_{res,t} = \theta_g\, E_{res,t}, \qquad cons_{ind,t} = \theta_g \left(E_t - E_{res,t}\right) \tag{4.61}\]

(GWh; cons). The supply cost adds a transmission and distribution allowance to last year’s average generation cost:

\[ sp^T_{g,t} = gnc^{av}_{t-1} + tmc_g, \qquad sp^T_{g,t_0} = gnc^{av}_{t_0} + tmc_g \tag{4.62}\]

where \(sp^T_{g,t}\) is the supply cost (USD/kWh; sc) and \(tmc_g\) the transmission and distribution allowance: 0.040 USD/kWh for residential and 0.015 for industrial users (IEA). The one-year lag (link I5) keeps prices free of a circular reference with dispatch.

4.9.2 Historical price components

Up to \(L\), the observed price is split into the supply cost and the components that explain the rest. All are held after \(L\), except where stated.

where \(hrp_{g,t}\) is the historical retail price including VAT, \(vatrate_g\) the electricity VAT rate of the group (of the last historical year), \(entx_{g,t}\) the manual electricity excise (Equation 4.68) and \(\phi^{ps}_t\) the phase-out factor of producer subsidies (psf; M1.7). The residual is the methodology’s “correction for under- or over-estimated transmission and distribution costs”.

Producer subsidy. The electricity producer subsidy of the data, \(PROS_t\) (USD million, positive), is allocated to the user groups by their pre-tax gap, the amount by which the supply cost exceeds the price before taxes:

\[ a_{g,t} = \frac{\max\left(0,\ sp^T_{g,t} - (\tilde{p}_{g,t} - x_{g,t})\right) cons_{g,t}} {\sum_{g'} \max\left(0,\ sp^T_{g',t} - (\tilde{p}_{g',t} - x_{g',t})\right) cons_{g',t}} \tag{4.63}\]

\[ ps_{g,t} = \max\left(\underline{ps},\ -\frac{PROS_t\; a_{g,t}}{cons_{g,t}}\right), \qquad t \le L \tag{4.64}\]

where \(a_{g,t}\) is the share of group \(g\) (psesh; if the gaps are all zero, its share of consumption; if that fails, 1 for residential), held after \(L\), and \(\underline{ps} = -0.3\) USD/kWh the floor (PsElFloor). The part beyond the floor is a memo outside prices, \(-PROS_t - \sum_g cons_{g,t}\, ps_{g,t}\) (USD million; pser), scaled by \(\phi^{ps}_t\) after \(L\). The rows psegap and psegs hold the numerator and denominator of Equation 4.63.

Consumer subsidy phase-out. Where the consumer tax is a subsidy (\(x_{g,L} < 0\)) and the scenario’s PowerSubsidyExemptInclude is Include (the indicator “Include” below), it phases out with the consumer-subsidy factor \(\phi^{cs}_t\) (pof.cs):

\[ cs_{g,t} = \mathbb{1}\left[t > L,\ x_{g,t} < 0,\ \text{Include}\right]\; x_{g,t}\left(\phi^{cs}_t - 1\right) \tag{4.65}\]

4.9.3 Price before new policies

Up to \(L\) the price is the data; after \(L\) it moves with the change in the supply cost and the subsidy components:

\[ \begin{aligned} p^{0,T}_{g,t} &= \begin{cases} hrp_{g,t} & t \le L \\ p^{0,T}_{g,t-1} + \pi \left(X_{g,t} - X_{g,t-1}\right)(1 + vatrate_g) & t > L \end{cases} \\ X_{g,t} &= sp^T_{g,t} + ps_{g,t} + cs_{g,t} + ores_{g,t} \end{aligned} \tag{4.66}\]

where \(p^{0,T}_{g,t}\) is the price before new policies including VAT (USD/kWh real; rppre) and \(\pi\) = PowerSectorPriceChangePassOn the pass-through of cost changes (default 1). This telescoped form is the legacy price sum (supply cost + producer subsidy + other tax + residual, then VAT) written as changes; components held at their last historical values cancel out of the change.

4.9.4 Excises and the rebate switch

Three settings of the scenario add to the price.

Electricity excise. In the scenario “Electricity excise” of the scenario table, a per-kWh excise equal to the carbon price times the grid’s base-year CO2 intensity:

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{e}_t &= \mathbb{1}[\text{policy scenario with electricity excise}]\; \epsilon^{grid}\, \tau_t \\ \epsilon^{grid} &= \frac{\sum_f in_{f,t_0}\, 0.041868\, EF_f}{\sum_f out_{f,t_0}} \end{aligned} \tag{4.67}\]

where \(\tau_t\) is the carbon price (USD/tCO2; cptraj), \(in_{f,t_0}\) the fuel input to power and \(out_{f,t_0}\) power output of the base-year energy balance (ktoe, GWh), and \(\epsilon^{grid}\) the grid’s CO2 intensity (tCO2/kWh; Egypt 0.00038). Code exs.

Manual excise. In policy scenarios with AdditionalExcise = Yes, the new electricity excise typed on ‘Manual inputs’ (real, or nominal × the deflator index if NomorReal is not Real), plus the electricity fuel price reform of the scenario:

\[ entx_{g,t} = \mathbb{1}[o \ne B]\left(\chi^{ae}\; x^{man}_{g,t}\, \delta_t + FPR_{ecy,g,t}\right) \tag{4.68}\]

where \(\chi^{ae}\) is 1 if AdditionalExcise = Yes, else 0, \(x^{man}_{g,t}\) the typed excise (entxd), \(\delta_t\) 1 or the deflator index, and \(FPR_{ecy,g,t}\) the fuel price reform path of electricity for the group (Reforms). Code entx. Like legacy, it enters the price as part of the price before policy: up to \(L\) the price data absorb it, and afterwards its change is passed through. The excise in the price, before VAT, is

\[ pex_{g,t} = x^e_t + \mathbb{1}[t > L]\; \pi\left(entx_{g,t} - entx_{g,L}\right), \qquad pex_{g,t_0} = x^e_{t_0} \tag{4.69}\]

(pex).

Rebate switch. In policy scenarios with PowerRebates = Yes (or Yes*), or in a feebate scenario of the scenario table (Feebates, Power Supply Feebates, Power feebate), the carbon cost is not passed to electricity users: \(rb = 1\) (rbs); otherwise \(rb = 0\).

4.9.5 End-user price

The end-user price adds the new policy cost passed through (unless rebated) and the excise, with VAT, and is floored:

\[ p^T_{g,t} = \max\left(p^{0,T}_{g,t} + \left(ncp^{av}_t\, \pi\, (1 - rb) + pex_{g,t}\right)(1 + vatrate_g),\ c_{min}\right) \tag{4.70}\]

where \(p^T_{g,t}\) is the end-user price (USD/kWh real; rp), \(ncp^{av}_t\) the average new policy cost per kWh consumed (Equation 4.27) and \(c_{min}\) = MinPostSubsidyPowerPrice (0.01). VAT is charged on the new policy terms, as for fuels. The equation list has the price as (M4.7) and with the subsidy components and floor as (A4.8).

4.9.6 Subsidy gap and the supply cost of the subsidy lines

The subsidy gap compares the price before VAT with the modelled supply cost; a negative gap means the price is below cost, a subsidy:

\[ gap_{g,t} = \frac{p^{0,T}_{g,t}}{1 + vatrate_g} - sp^T_{g,t} \tag{4.71}\]

(USD/kWh; sgap). The supply cost read by the subsidy and efficient-price lines of the welfare block (Chapter 7) removes the residual and adds the policy terms:

\[ sp^{e}_{g,t} = sp^T_{g,t} + ores_{g,t} + ncp^{av}_t\, \pi\, (1 - rb) + pex_{g,t} \tag{4.72}\]

(spe).

4.10 Power revenues

The power sector adds to government revenue in two ways: through the electricity bills of users (VAT, excises, the carbon cost passed through, rebates, the subsidy gap) and through the fuels and emissions of generation (taxes and subsidies on generation fuels, existing carbon pricing, new policies, renewable subsidies). Both are computed here, in USD million real, and added to the revenue totals in Chapter 7.

4.10.1 By user group

Each term is a per-kWh amount times consumption \(cons_{g,t}\) (GWh; GWh × USD/kWh = USD million):

Revenue Per kWh Code
VAT on the price before new policies \(p^{0,T}_{g,t}\, vatrate_g/(1 + vatrate_g)\) rvat
VAT on the new-policy cost and excise passed through \(\left(ncp^{av}_t\, \pi\, (1 - rb) + pex_{g,t}\right) vatrate_g\) rnvat
Rebate (negative): the carbon cost returned to users \(-rb\; ncp^{av}_t\, \pi\) rreb
Excise \(pex_{g,t}\); of which carbon-based excise \(x^e_t\) (rexs, all users) and manual excise (rexm = total − rexs) rpex
Subsidy gap (signed: negative = fiscal cost) \(gap_{g,t}\) rgap
New-policy cost (memo, ETS at the tax-equivalent price) \(ncp^{av}_t\) rcarb

4.10.2 By generation type

With a fuel flag \(\chi_f\) = 1 for coal, gas, oil and biomass and 0 otherwise:

\[ \begin{aligned} R^{tax}_{f,t} &= \chi_f\, FU_{f,t}\, \kappa_{PJ}\, (etx + vat)_{c(f),t} \\ R^{sub}_{f,t} &= \chi_f\, FU_{f,t}\, \kappa_{PJ}\, esub_{c(f),t} \\ R^{xc}_{f,t} &= 8.76\; gen_{f,t}\; \Delta xcp_{f,t} \\ R^{new}_{f,t} &= 8.76\; gen_{f,t}\; ncp_{f,t} - CO2_{f,t}\, A_{pow,t}\, (1 - \rho^{ets}_t) \\ R^{xl}_{f,t} &= CO2_{f,t} \left(XCT_{f,t}\,\phi^{ct}_t + XETS_{f,t}\,\phi^{ets}_t\right) \\ R^{rns}_{f,t} &= 8.76\; gen_{f,t}\; rns_{f,t} \end{aligned} \tag{4.73}\]

where

  • \(R^{xc}\) the change in existing carbon pricing against the price data (prxc);
  • \(R^{new}\) new-policy revenue (prnew): carbon tax, ETS and fuel price reform per kWh times generation, less the part of the ETS cost not collected, so that the ETS is valued at the permit price as in the subsectors; \(\rho^{ets}_t\) is the ETS revenue factor (ets.rf, Chapter 6);
  • \(R^{xl}\) the level of existing carbon pricing (memo; prxl);
  • \(R^{rns}\) renewable subsidies (negative; prrns).

4.11 The coal implicit cost

A model with one period per year cannot see why many countries burn less coal than its cost advantage suggests: environmental regulation of coal plants, and the flexibility gas offers. CPAT therefore adds an implicit cost to coal, calibrated so that the model reproduces observed coal shares.

The methodology describes the calibration in section 3.4.2.4.5 and writes the cost \(ipc\) (corrections, DOC12).

The cost moves linearly from a short-term value in the base year to a long-term value in 2030:

\[ ipc_t = ipc^{S} + \left(ipc^{L} - ipc^{S}\right) \min\left(1,\ \frac{t - t_0}{T_{ic} - t_0}\right) \tag{4.74}\]

where

  • \(ipc_t\) is the coal implicit cost (USD/kWh; cic);
  • \(ipc^{S}\) and \(ipc^{L}\) are the short-term and long-term values (cics, cicl), chosen by the scenario input AdditionalCoalIntangibleCost: No gives 0; Manual gives ManualCoalIntangibleCostST and ManualCoalIntangibleCostLT; otherwise the country’s values in the GoalSeekCoal table (85 countries; 0 if the country is absent, as for Egypt);
  • \(T_{ic}\) = 2030 (CoalICYr).

The cost is added to the coal variable cost (Equation 4.18), so it acts on dispatch, on the levelised variable cost and so on investment, and on early coal retirement. It is also part of the generation cost and so of tariffs, unless the setting PwCoalICTariff is No (default Yes, as legacy; Equation 4.26). It appears in the system cost memo.

The methodology’s calibration minimises the squared difference between modelled and observed (IEA) coal shares in 2019 and 2020, raising the coal price where the model overestimates the coal share (only positive values); the GoalSeekCoal table reports the results for both years, and IEA’s 2030 projection of the coal share is reported for information. The model uses legacy’s stored results; the calibration itself (a goal seek) is not part of the model.

4.13.2 Energy balance: generation, efficiency, capacity factor and capacity

Base-year generation, fuel input and installed capacity give the actual efficiency and capacity factor (methodology, 3.4.4.1.2):

\[ \begin{aligned} gen_{f,t_0} &= out_f\, \frac{1000}{H}, \qquad & \nu^{a}_f &= \frac{out_f}{in_f\, \theta_g} \\ cf^{a}_f &= \frac{gen_{f,t_0}}{IC_f}, \qquad & cap_{f,t_0} &= \frac{gen_{f,t_0}}{cf_f} \end{aligned} \tag{4.76}\]

where

  • \(out_f\) is power output of the energy balance (GWh, flow eloutput) and \(in_f\) fuel input to power (ktoe, minus the flow power), for the type’s fuels (oil: other oil products, gasoline, diesel, kerosene, LPG, jet fuel);
  • \(gen_{f,t_0}\) base-year generation (MWy);
  • \(\nu^a_f\) the actual efficiency, used unless below the minimum or without input (Section 4.4.1);
  • \(IC_f\) installed capacity of the base year (MW, Power tab installed-capacity block);
  • \(cf^a_f\) the actual capacity factor, used within its bounds (Equation 4.6);
  • \(cap_{f,t_0}\) base-year capacity at the capacity factor used.

4.13.4 WACC

The shares of final demand come from the base-year energy balance of electricity:

\[ \begin{aligned} s_{nxp} &= -\frac{M + X}{D}, \qquad s_{tsd} = -\frac{SD + TR + DL}{D}, \qquad s_{eou} = -\frac{OU}{D} \\ s_{add} &= \frac{G}{D} - 1 - s_{nxp} - s_{tsd} - s_{eou} \end{aligned} \tag{4.78}\]

where \(M\), \(X\), \(SD\), \(TR\), \(DL\), \(OU\) are imports, exports, statistical differences, transfers, distribution losses and own use of electricity (ktoe, with the energy balance’s signs), \(G = \sum_f gen_{f,t_0} \cdot 8.76/11.63\) base-year generation (ktoe) and \(D\) final electricity demand (ktoe): the sum over the flows road, rail, domestic aviation, domestic navigation, residential, food and forestry, services, mining and chemicals, iron and steel, non-ferrous metals, machinery, cement, other manufacturing, construction and other non-specified (fuel transformation, own use and non-energy use excluded). So \(1 + s_{nxp} + s_{tsd} + s_{eou} + s_{add} = G/D\) exactly. The equation list has them as (A4.3).
