6  Emissions and emissions trading

This chapter turns the fuel use of Chapter 3, Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 into emissions. It covers carbon dioxide from fuel combustion, the other greenhouse gases of the national inventory, methane by source with the methane fee, the comparison with the country’s NDC, and the local air pollutants. It closes with the new emissions trading system (ETS): how its cap becomes a permit price, and how that price enters fuel prices and revenue.

Emissions matter twice in the model. They are an output (the GHG path of each scenario, the change against the baseline, the gap to the NDC) and they feed back: CO2 emission factors convert every carbon price into a price per unit of fuel (Chapter 2), and covered emissions set the ETS price. The carbon pricing indicators (coverage of the carbon tax and the ETS, effective carbon rates, revenue per tonne) are described with the revenues in Chapter 7.

The methodology describes emissions in methodology, section 3.5, the local pollutants in methodology, section 6.4 and the ETS price in methodology, section 3.2.4 and Appendix C.

6.1 Overview

6.1.1 Order of calculation

The ETS price is set before the year loop, with the other policies: it depends only on inputs and on stored baseline emissions, never on the fuel use of the year. Inside each year, the emission blocks run after fuel use and power, in this order.

The CO2 of each subsector and fuel is computed inside the fuel-use block (Chapter 3), and the CO2 of each generation type inside the power block (Chapter 4); this chapter gives the equation once and builds the totals. The NDC rows run after the year loop because they read the baseline in the NDC target year.

6.1.2 Notation and units

Every equation holds for each scenario \(o\) and country \(c\); these indices are omitted. A value of the baseline (scenario 1) in the same year carries the superscript \(B\); the previous year carries \(t-1\). Other indices:

Index Set
\(s\) subsector (16; Chapter 3), or a coverage sector \(e\) (power and the 16 subsectors, 17 in all)
\(f\) fuel (8, plus jet fuel for CO2), or generation type \(k\) (9; Chapter 4)
\(u\) UNFCCC inventory category: energy, industrial processes (IPPU), agriculture, LULUCF, waste, other
\(p\) gas: CO2, CH4, N2O, F-gases; or local pollutant: PM2.5, NOx, SO2, VOC, BC, OC, CO
\(j\) methane source: oil and gas, downstream gas, coal mining, biomass, livestock, rice, waste
\(t\) year, from the base year \(t_0\) to 2041; \(Y\) is the last year of the country’s GHG inventory

Units: CO2 in Mt; other greenhouse gases in Mt CO2e with the IPCC AR6 GWP100 (fossil methane 29.8, non-fossil methane 27.2); methane by source in kt CH4; local pollutants in kt; fuel use in ktoe. \(\theta\) is PJ per ktoe (0.041868), so fuel use (ktoe) \(\times\ \theta\ \times\) a factor in t CO2 per GJ gives Mt CO2. For Egypt \(Y\) = 2024.

6.2 Energy CO2

Energy CO2 is fuel use times an emission factor, for every subsector, fuel and generation type. The factors are those of the country, scaled once so that the model’s base year matches the national inventory. The methodology describes this in methodology, sections 3.5.3 and 3.5.4.1.

6.2.1 Emission factors

Each fuel and EF sector has one CO2 factor. The EF sectors are power, road, residential and industry; every subsector reads one of them:

EF sector Subsectors
power power generation (by generation type)
road road, rail, domestic aviation, domestic navigation
residential residential, food & forestry, services
industry the eight industrial subsectors and other energy use

The factor is looked up in the IIASA (GAINS) table of the country, else of its World Bank region, else of the world:

\[ ef^{0}_{s,f} = \begin{cases} ef^{IIASA}_{c \to r \to w}(f', s') / 1000 & \text{fossil fuels} \\ ef^{IEA}_{jet} & f = \text{jet fuel, in every sector} \\ 0 & f = \text{biomass} \end{cases} \tag{6.1}\]

where

  • \(ef^{0}_{s,f}\) is the CO2 factor before calibration, t CO2 per GJ;
  • \(ef^{IIASA}_{c \to r \to w}\) is the factor of sheet EF_GHG in kg CO2 per GJ, for the country \(c\), else its World Bank region \(r\), else the world \(w\) (0 if none), divided by 1000 kg per t;
  • \((f', s')\) is the fuel and EF sector the row reads: the row’s own, except for the substitutions below;
  • \(ef^{IEA}_{jet}\) = 0.0715 t CO2 per GJ (IEA, 19.5 tC per TJ \(\times\) 44/12), used for jet fuel in every sector and in domestic aviation.

Some fuel and sector pairs have no factor of their own. They read another, as the methodology’s table of missing factors:

Fuel and sector Factor used
coal in road coal in power
LPG in power LPG in industry
LPG in road LPG in residential
kerosene in power and in road kerosene in residential
other oil products in road diesel in road
biomass, every sector 0

The factors of this table are the model’s data step (A7.1). The local-pollutant and combustion-methane factors follow a different chain, described in Section 6.7.1.

6.2.2 Calibration to the GHG inventory

The IIASA factors include some process and fugitive emissions, and fuel-use data differ between sources. The model therefore scales every CO2 factor by one number, so that modelled base-year CO2 equals the inventory. This is legacy CPAT’s rule (issue I32, replicated by user decision).

\[ \chi = \frac{CO2^{inv}_{t_0 - 1}}{CO2^{B,raw}_{t_0}}, \qquad ef_{s,f} = \chi\, ef^{0}_{s,f}, \qquad ef_k = \chi\, ef^{0}_k \tag{6.2}\]

where

  • \(\chi\) (EFCalib) is the calibration factor, 1 when the switch is off;
  • \(CO2^{inv}_{t_0 - 1}\) is the inventory energy CO2 of the year before the base year (Mt);
  • \(CO2^{B,raw}_{t_0}\) is scenario-1 energy CO2 of the base year with the uncalibrated factors: the base-year energy balance times \(ef^{0}\) (Mt);
  • \(ef_{s,f}\), \(ef_k\) are the calibrated factors of subsectors and generation types.

The switch is on by default (Yes*), as legacy, whose two switches EFsAdjustmentAnnexI and EFsAdjustmentNonAnnexI are both Yes*. The model has one switch for all countries.

Why the baseline value is data. A live formula for \(CO2^{B,raw}\) would be circular: the factors feed the carbon prices, which feed fuel use. The uncalibrated scenario-1 CO2 is therefore stored as data, keyed by country, and refreshed by the build, like the ETS baseline (Section 6.9.2). This is exact: scenario-1 fuel use does not depend on the factors (there is no new carbon price in the baseline, and existing carbon pricing changes only after the historical years), so the calibrated baseline is the uncalibrated one times \(\chi\). Another country finds no stored rows until the build has been run for it.

What follows from \(\chi\). Everything downstream uses the calibrated factors: CO2 by subsector, fuel and generation type; the carbon tax, the ETS cost and existing carbon pricing per unit of fuel and their revenue; the ETS baseline; energy CO2 in the GHG totals. Scenario-1 fuel use and prices do not change. In policy scenarios, carbon prices per unit of fuel change by the factor (7% lower for Egypt), so fuel use, revenue and the GDP effect change too.

6.2.3 CO2 by subsector, fuel and coverage sector

With the factors in hand, CO2 is fuel use times the factor (M7.1):

\[ em_{s,f,CO_2} = \theta\, F_{s,f}\, ef_{s,f}, \qquad em_{k,CO_2} = \theta\, PE_k\, ef_k \tag{6.3}\]

where

  • \(em_{s,f,CO_2}\) is the CO2 of subsector \(s\) and fuel \(f\) (Mt), co2;
  • \(F_{s,f}\) is fuel use (ktoe), ener (Chapter 3);
  • \(PE_k\) is the fuel input of generation type \(k\) (ktoe), pe (Chapter 4), and \(ef_k\) its factor.

The totals sum over fuels, subsectors and generation types:

\[ em_{CO_2} = \sum_{s}\sum_{f} em_{s,f,CO_2} + \sum_{k} em_{k,CO_2} \tag{6.4}\]

The block also reports CO2 by sector group (transport, buildings, industry, other energy use, power), by fuel (power fuels included), the baseline value of the same year (co2.ref), the change against it (co2.pct, co2.chg) and a check that the totals by group and by fuel both equal the total (co2.chk = 0). CO2 by coverage sector (co2.sec) feeds the ETS check of Section 6.9.2:

\[ em_{e} = \sum_{f} em_{e,f,CO_2} \ (e \ne \text{power}), \qquad em_{pow} = \sum_k em_{k,CO_2} \tag{6.5}\]

6.3 Methane by source and the methane fee

Methane is the second greenhouse gas of most inventories and the one a methane fee targets. The model splits the inventory’s methane between seven sources, projects each with its own activity, lets the fee abate it along the source’s cost curve, and collects the fee revenue. The methodology describes this in methodology, section 3.5.4.2 (energy methane) and sections 3.5.5.2 and 3.5.5.3.1 (agriculture and waste).

Source \(j\) Category GWP Activity Curve
oil and gas extraction (og) energy fossil oil and gas production oil and gas
downstream gas (dg) energy fossil gas use oil and gas
coal mining (cm) energy fossil coal production coal
biomass use (bm) energy fossil biomass use oil and gas
livestock (liv) agriculture non-fossil index of income and population livestock
rice (ric) agriculture non-fossil index of income and population rice
waste (wst) waste non-fossil index of income and population waste

Biomass and downstream gas use the oil-and-gas curve, as legacy. The four energy sources together are the fuel-supply methane (ch4.sup).

6.3.2 The methane fee

The fee is a price per tonne of CO2-equivalent methane. It follows a linear path that keeps its slope after the target year, applies only in policy scenarios, and can add the carbon price (M1.8):

\[ MF_t = \Lambda(t;\, T^{m}_0, T^{m}_1, x^{m}_0, x^{m}_1)\ \mathbb{1}[o \ne B] \;+\; NCT_t\ \mathbb{1}[\texttt{CTforMethane} = \text{Yes}] \tag{6.6}\]

where

  • \(MF_t\) is the methane fee (USD per t CO2e, real), mfee;
  • \(\Lambda\) is the linear policy path of Chapter 1, from MPLevelStart in MPIntro to MPLevelTarget in MPOutro, continued linearly after MPOutro (as legacy);
  • \(\mathbb{1}[o \ne B]\) is 1 in policy scenarios (scenario number above 1, legacy row 2238);
  • \(NCT_t\) is the carbon price (USD per t CO2), added when CTforMethane = Yes. This part has no scenario condition, as legacy row 2239.

6.3.3 Allocation of inventory methane to sources

The inventory reports methane by category, not by source. The model splits the methane of the last inventory year \(Y\) between the sources of each category. For the four energy sources, each source’s input share is capped by ten times its share of energy activity, and the capped weights are normalised:

\[ w_j = \min\!\left(\mu\,\frac{X^{0}_j}{\sum_{i \in \text{fossil}} X^{0}_i},\ sh_j\right), \qquad \pi_j = \frac{w_j}{\sum_{i \in \text{fossil}} w_i} \quad (j \in \text{fossil}) \tag{6.7}\]

where

  • \(sh_j\) is the country’s input share of source \(j\) (CH4Inputs: oil row per_upstream_og, per_downstream_g, per_biomass; coal row per_upstream_c), 0 if missing;
  • \(X^{0}_j\) is the base-year activity of the source (data): oil plus gas production and coal production from Balances; gas and biomass use from the energy-consumption data of the first year (every column but electricity);
  • \(\mu\) = 10 (MethShareCap, legacy row 899);
  • \(\pi_j\) is the share of energy methane allocated to source \(j\).

Livestock and rice take their input shares of agricultural methane (\(\pi_j = sh_j\), rows per_livestock, per_rice); waste takes all waste methane (\(\pi_{wst} = 1\)). The base emissions of each source, in kt CH4, are then

\[ M_{j,0} = M^{inv}_{u(j),Y}\ \pi_j\ \frac{1000}{GWP_j} \tag{6.8}\]

where

  • \(M^{inv}_{u(j),Y}\) is the inventory methane of the source’s category (energy, agriculture or waste) in year \(Y\), Mt CO2e (ghg.invb);
  • \(GWP_j\) is 29.8 for the fossil sources and 27.2 for livestock, rice and waste.

The shares and base emissions are data of the country: they are the same in every scenario, so the calibration is to the observed inventory.

6.3.4 Activity

Each source has an activity that moves its emissions. Downstream gas and biomass follow the scenario’s total use of the fuel (power included), so fuel switching changes their methane. Livestock, rice and waste follow an index driven by income and population:

\[ X_j(t) = X_j(t-1)\,(1 + g^{pc}_t)^{\epsilon^{Y}_{u(j)}}\,(1 + n_t)^{\epsilon^{N}_{u(j)}}, \qquad X_j(t_0) = 1 \qquad (j \in \{\text{liv}, \text{ric}, \text{wst}\}) \tag{6.9}\]

where

  • \(g^{pc}_t\) is GDP per capita growth and \(n_t\) population growth (Equation 6.24);
  • \(\epsilon^{Y}\), \(\epsilon^{N}\) are the income and population elasticities of the category: agriculture 0.15 and 0.9, waste 0 and 1 (Section 6.5).

The two extraction sources follow fuel production, \(X_{og} = Prd_{oil} + Prd_{gas}\) and \(X_{cm} = Prd_{coal}\), described next.

Fuel production for supply methane

The model has no production model for fossil fuels. Production follows world demand of the global demand scenario, and responds to the producer price change that the methane fee causes, as legacy rows 6132-6149 (issue I66). First, the fee per ktoe produced, after abatement:

\[ fk_j(t) = MF_t\, GWP_j\, \iota_j \cdot 1000 \cdot \frac{M_{j,0}}{X^{0}_j}\,(1 - a_j(t)), \qquad fk_j(t_0) = 0 \qquad (j \in \{\text{og}, \text{cm}\}) \tag{6.10}\]

where \(\iota_j\) is 1 if the fee applies to the source (fossil fuel production switch), else 0, and \(a_j\) the abatement share of Equation 6.13. \(fk_j\) is in USD per ktoe. Its change raises the producer price index:

\[ \Pi_f(t) = 1 + \frac{fk_{j(f)}(t) - fk_{j(f)}(t-1)}{p^{int}_f(t)\ \gamma_f}\ \phi^{pass}, \qquad \Pi_f(t_0) = 1 \tag{6.11}\]

where

  • \(j(f)\) is the oil-and-gas source for oil and gas, coal mining for coal;
  • \(p^{int}_f\) is the international price of the fuel in its own unit (oil per barrel, gas per MMBtu, coal per tonne; Chapter 2);
  • \(\gamma_f\) is price units per ktoe: 41,868 GJ per ktoe divided by 6.1408 GJ per barrel, 1.055056 GJ per MMBtu or 29.3076 GJ per tonne;
  • \(\phi^{pass}\) is the pass-through (Manual inputs J194, default 0, so \(\Pi_f = 1\) by default).

Production holds the base-year value in the base year and the next, then grows with the price response and with world demand of the year before:

\[ Prd_f(t) = \begin{cases} Prd^{0}_f & t \le t_0 + 1 \\ Prd_f(t-1)\ \Pi_f(t)^{\varepsilon_f}\ \dfrac{WD_f(t-1)}{WD_f(t-2)} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{6.12}\]

where

  • \(Prd^{0}_f\) is base-year production of oil products, coal and gas (Balances, ktoe);
  • \(\varepsilon_f\) is the price elasticity of production (Manual inputs J190:J192);
  • \(WD_f\) is world production of the fuel in the IEA scenario GlobalEnergyDemand of scenario 1 (Stated Policies, Announced Pledges, Sustainable Development, Net Zero), Mtoe. As legacy, the third and fourth options both read the Net Zero row.

The source shares use base-year production as their activity, so the shares do not depend on this projection (no loop).

6.3.5 Abatement and emissions

The fee abates methane along a power-function cost curve of the source (A7.3):

\[ a_j(t) = \min\!\Big(1,\ \max\!\big(0,\ A_j\,(MF_t\, GWP_j\, \iota_j)^{B_j}\big)\Big) \tag{6.13}\]

where

  • \(a_j\) is the abatement share (ch4.abt);
  • \(MF_t\,GWP_j\) is the fee per tonne of CH4 (USD);
  • \(A_j\), \(B_j\) are the curve coefficients of CH4Inputs (avg_pow_a, avg_pow_b) for the country and the source’s curve row; where the country value is 0 or missing, the CH4Inputs average (AVG) is used, as legacy (issue I152);
  • \(\iota_j\) is the fee switch of the source (Section 6.3.1).

Methane intensity also declines on its own, from the last inventory year:

\[ I_j(t) = (1 - \delta_j)^{\max(0,\ t - Y)} \tag{6.14}\]

where \(I_j\) is the intensity index (ch4.int) and \(\delta_j\) the source’s rate of autonomous technological improvement (Manual inputs J185:J188; oil and gas also for downstream gas and biomass, issue I141). The emissions of each source combine base emissions, activity, intensity and abatement:

\[ M_j(t) = M_{j,0}\ \frac{X_j(t)}{X^{0}_j}\ I_j(t)\ \big(1 - a_j(t)\big) \tag{6.15}\]

where \(M_j(t)\) is the methane of source \(j\) in kt CH4 (ch4.em), 0 if \(X^{0}_j = 0\). For livestock, rice and waste \(X^{0}_j = 1\). Fuel-supply methane is \(M^{sup}(t) = \sum_{j \in \text{fossil}} M_j(t)\).

6.3.6 Flaring and the fee revenue

Part of the abated oil-and-gas methane is flared: it burns to CO2 and is reported as energy CO2.

\[ CO2^{flr}_t = r_{fl}\ \phi_{fl}\ M_{og,0}\ \frac{X_{og}(t)}{X^{0}_{og}}\ a_{og}(t)\ /\ 1000 \tag{6.16}\]

where \(CO2^{flr}\) is in Mt (ghg.flr), \(r_{fl}\) = 44/16 = 2.75 t CO2 per t CH4 and \(\phi_{fl}\) the flared share (CH4Inputs oil row per_flare). The fee raises revenue on the methane that remains:

\[ R^{mf}_t = MF_t \sum_j M_j(t)\, GWP_j\, \iota_j\ \frac{1000}{10^{6}} \tag{6.17}\]

where \(R^{mf}\) is in USD million (ch4.rev), part of total revenue (Chapter 7).

6.4 Energy GHGs: combustion methane, N2O and F-gases

The energy category of the inventory holds CO2, methane, N2O and F-gases. CO2 comes from Section 6.2 plus flaring. Methane combines combustion and fuel supply and is scaled to the inventory. N2O and F-gases follow simple rules. The methodology describes these in methodology, sections 3.5.4.2 and 3.5.4.3.

6.4.1 Combustion methane

Every fuel burnt emits some methane. The factor moves on a straight line from its base-year value towards its final value in EFEndYear, and the line continues after that year, as legacy:

\[ w_t = \omega\ \frac{t - t_0}{T^{EF} - t_0} \tag{6.18}\]

\[ CH4_{s,f}(t) = F_{s,f}(t)\ \frac{e^{0}_{s,f} + (e^{1}_{s,f} - e^{0}_{s,f})\, w_t}{1000}, \qquad CH4_k(t) = PE_k(t)\ \frac{e^{0}_{k} + (e^{1}_{k} - e^{0}_{k})\, w_t}{1000} \tag{6.19}\]

where

  • \(w_t\) is the weight of the final factor, 0 in the base year;
  • \(\omega\) (EFFinShare) follows the factor selection EFSel: Constant 0, Variable 1, Average 0.5;
  • \(T^{EF}\) = 2039 (EFEndYear, the legacy column of the final factor; later years extrapolate the line);
  • \(e^{0}\), \(e^{1}\) are the GAINS CH4 factors of the base year and the final year, t per ktoe, looked up as for the local pollutants (Section 6.7.1);
  • \(CH4_{s,f}\), \(CH4_k\) are in kt CH4 (ch4); power uses the fuel input of the coal, gas, oil and biomass types.

The combustion methane of all subsectors and power is \(CH4^{c}(t)\) (ch4.c), also reported by sector group.

6.4.2 Energy methane, calibrated to the inventory

Combustion plus supply methane, in CO2e, is scaled by one factor so that it equals the inventory in the last inventory year:

\[ k_{CH_4} = \frac{M^{inv}_{enr,Y}}{\big(CH4^{c,B}_{Y} + M^{sup,B}_{Y}\big)\, GWP_F / 1000} \tag{6.20}\]

\[ E_{enr,CH_4}(t) = k_{CH_4}\ \big(CH4^{c}(t) + M^{sup}(t)\big)\ \frac{GWP_F}{1000} \tag{6.21}\]

where

  • \(k_{CH_4}\) is the calibration factor (ghg.k);
  • \(M^{inv}_{enr,Y}\) is the inventory energy methane in year \(Y\) (Mt CO2e);
  • \(CH4^{c,B}_{Y}\), \(M^{sup,B}_{Y}\) are the scenario-1 combustion and supply methane of year \(Y\) (kt), stored as data (sheet BaselineCO2 rows ch4c, ch4sup, refreshed by the build), so that the factor, used in every year, reads no later model year (no circular reference);
  • \(GWP_F\) = 29.8, the fossil GWP;
  • \(E_{enr,CH_4}\) is energy methane in Mt CO2e.

Energy methane thus equals the inventory in \(Y\) and then moves with fuel use, supply activity and abatement.

6.4.3 Energy CO2, N2O and F-gases

\[ E_{enr,CO_2}(t) = em_{CO_2}(t) + CO2^{flr}_t \tag{6.22}\]

Energy CO2 is always the model’s (fuel combustion, power included, plus flaring), also in inventory years. Energy N2O and F-gases take the inventory while it has the year; after it, N2O moves with energy CO2 and F-gases are held:

\[ E_{enr,N_2O}(t) = E_{enr,N_2O}(t-1)\ \frac{em_{CO_2}(t)}{em_{CO_2}(t-1)}, \qquad E_{enr,F}(t) = E_{enr,F}(t-1) \tag{6.23}\]

(the ratio is 1 if last year’s CO2 is 0).

6.5 Other inventory categories

The non-energy categories take the inventory while it has the year. After that, each grows with its own driver: industrial energy CO2 for industrial processes, income and population for agriculture, waste and other, an exogenous decline for LULUCF. The methodology describes these in methodology, section 3.5.5 (A7.2, M7.2).

6.5.1 Drivers

\[ n_t = \frac{pop_t}{pop_{t-1}} - 1, \qquad g^{pc}_t = \frac{1 + g_t}{1 + n_t} - 1, \qquad \psi_t = \begin{cases} em_{CO_2}(t) / em^{B}_{CO_2}(t) & \texttt{AdditionMitigationPoliciesNonEnergy} = \text{Yes} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{6.24}\]

where

  • \(pop_t\) is UN WPP population (thousands), \(n_t\) its growth (pop.g);
  • \(g_t\) is real GDP growth after the GDP feedback (Chapter 7), \(g^{pc}_t\) GDP per capita growth (gdppc.g);
  • \(\psi_t\) is the mitigation effort in non-energy sectors (ghg.m): the scenario’s energy CO2 over the baseline’s, if the scenario’s switch is on. This option, in effect, extends the carbon price’s effect to non-energy GHGs.

The growth factor of a category is

\[ d_{u,t} = (1 + g^{pc}_t)^{\epsilon^{Y}_u}\,(1 + n_t)^{\epsilon^{N}_u}\ \frac{\psi_t}{\psi_{t-1}}, \qquad d_{u,t_0} = 1 \tag{6.25}\]

with the elasticities of sheet Manual inputs K325:M327, at the legacy addresses (a text entry such as “na” counts as 0):

Category \(u\) Income elasticity \(\epsilon^{Y}\) (row 327) Population elasticity \(\epsilon^{N}\) (row 326)
Agriculture 0.15 0.9
Waste 0 (“na”) 1
Other 0 (“na”) 1

These values reproduce the cached results of the legacy run exactly. Legacy Mitigation uses the same values; it never reads the manual cells, whose formula is broken (#REF!).

  • \(L^{NDC}\) is the target (ndc.lvl), for \(u\), \(c\) and \(sel\), as legacy rows 18494-18496.

6.6.3 Progress against the NDC

For every scenario and year:

\[ red_t = 1 - \frac{GHG^{inc}_t}{GHG^{inc,B}_t}, \qquad gap_t = \frac{red_t}{r_{sel}}, \qquad over_t = GHG^{inc}_t - L^{NDC}_{sel} \tag{6.30}\]

where

  • \(red_t\) is the reduction of GHGs including LULUCF against scenario 1 (ndc.red);
  • \(gap_t\) is the share of the NDC reduction achieved (ndc.gap, legacy “Gap to NDC”; 1 if no reduction is required);
  • \(over_t\) is the excess over the chosen target (ndc.over); in the target year it is the legacy goal-seek difference (G18501).

The rows also give the target against base-year GHGs including LULUCF (ndc.idx).

6.7.2 Emissions and totals

The factor path is that of combustion methane (Equation 6.18). Emissions are fuel use times the weighted factor, summed over fuels:

\[ AP_{p,s}(t) = \sum_f F_{s,f}(t)\ \frac{(1 - w_t)\, e^{0}_{p,s,f} + w_t\, e^{1}_{p,s,f}}{1000}, \qquad AP_{p,pow}(t) = \sum_k PE_k(t)\ \frac{(1 - w_t)\, e^{0}_{p,k} + w_t\, e^{1}_{p,k}}{1000} \tag{6.31}\]

where

  • \(AP_{p,s}\) is the emission of pollutant \(p\) by subsector \(s\) (kt), ap;
  • \(e^{0}\), \(e^{1}\) are the base-year and final factors (t per ktoe);
  • fuel use is the modelled one, so prices, policies and scenarios carry through.

Transport, buildings and industry sum their subsectors, navigation included. A pollutant’s total is all subsectors plus power: \(AP_p = \sum_s AP_{p,s} + AP_{p,pow}\).

The forcing memo converts the emissions with the GWP100 of the country’s GAINS region:

\[ F^{ap}_p = AP_p\ GWP_{p,r} / 1000, \qquad F^{ap} = \sum_{p \in \{BC, OC, NOx, SO2, VOC, CO\}} F^{ap}_p \tag{6.32}\]

in Mt CO2e (apf). Cooling pollutants (OC, SO2, NOx) have a negative GWP, so the net forcing can be negative. Energy GHGs including the forcing are \(E_{enr} + F^{ap}\) (ghg.enrp, legacy “Total GHGs and PM”, rows 18310 and 18321).

6.9 The new emissions trading system

A new ETS caps the CO2 of the sectors it covers. The model does not solve for the market price inside the year loop. It sets a permit price from the cap with a closed-form estimate, lets that price act on fuel use like a carbon tax (for the auctioned part) or like a feebate (for the part allocated free), and then reports how close covered emissions come to the cap, with the price that would close the gap. The methodology treats the new ETS price with the carbon prices in methodology, section 3.2.4 and its goal seek in section 3.9.3.5; the user guide in section 1.3.2.4.2.

6.9.2 Coverage, benchmarks and the cap

The ETS is on from its start year, and a sector counts once it is covered and the ETS is on (M1.3):

\[ on_t = \mathbb{1}[\texttt{NewETS} = \text{Yes and } t \ge T^{E}_0], \qquad \kappa_{e,t} = on_t\ \mathbb{1}[\text{sector } e \text{ covered}] \tag{6.38}\]

The benchmark is the share of a sector’s emission intensity allocated free. It runs linearly from the start to the target year and is flat after; the output-based (OBR) share of a sector is its group’s benchmark, bounded to 0-1:

\[ b_{G,t} = \bar\Lambda\big(t;\, T^{E}_0, T^{E}_1,\, 1 - \alpha_0,\, 1 - \alpha_1\big), \qquad \omega_{e,t} = \min\!\big(1, \max(0, b_{G(e),t})\big) \tag{6.39}\]

where

  • \(b_{G,t}\) is the benchmark of group \(G\) (etsb), 0 before the start year;
  • \(\bar\Lambda\) is the linear path held flat after the target year (Chapter 1);
  • \(\alpha_0\), \(\alpha_1\) are the auctioned proportions at the start and target year, the same for every group, as legacy (issue I21). The default is \(\alpha = 0\): nothing auctioned, benchmark 1, as legacy;
  • \(\omega_{e,t}\) is the OBR share (obr); the auctioned share is \(1 - \omega_{e,t}\).

The cap is a change against baseline covered emissions:

\[ bce_t = \sum_e CO2^{B}_{e,t}\ \kappa_{e,t}, \qquad chg_t = \bar\Lambda\big(t;\, T^{E}_0, T^{E}_1, chg_0, chg_1\big) \tag{6.40}\]

\[ cap_t = \begin{cases} on_t\ cap_{t-1} & t > T^{E}_1 \text{ and } \texttt{ETSCapCont} = \text{Constant} \\ on_t\ bce_t\,(1 + chg_t) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{6.41}\]

where

  • \(CO2^{B}_{e,t}\) is the stored scenario-1 CO2 of ETS sector \(e\) (bco2, Mt), \(bce_t\) baseline covered emissions (ets.bce);
  • \(chg_t\) is the cap change (ets.chg, negative for a cut), held at \(chg_1\) after the target year, so that “any other” continuation keeps the relative change;
  • \(cap_t\) is the cap (ets.cap, Mt).

6.9.3 Fast price estimate and the permit price

Price volatility makes an ETS less effective than a tax at the same price. The model expresses this as a volatility adjustment, the tax-equivalent value of one dollar of permit price:

\[ V = \frac{1 + risk \cdot imp}{1 + vol \cdot imp} \tag{6.42}\]

where \(V\) is ets.vadj, \(risk\) the carbon tax policy risk and \(vol\), \(imp\) the values of the labels above. At Medium: \((1 + 0.2 \times 0.5)/(1 + 0.42 \times 0.5) = 1/1.1\).

The covered semi-elasticity weights each covered sector’s semi-elasticity by its baseline emissions and discounts the free part by the effectiveness of output-based allocation:

\[ \bar\sigma_t = \frac{\sum_e CO2^{B}_{e,t}\ \kappa_{e,t}\ \sigma_e\ \big(1 - (1 - \vartheta)\,\omega_{e,t}\big)}{bce_t} \tag{6.43}\]

where \(\bar\sigma_t\) is ets.se (0 if \(bce_t = 0\)), \(\sigma_e\) the semi-elasticity of the sector’s group (per USD per t CO2) and \(\vartheta\) = 0.5 the effectiveness of output-based allocation (auctioned allowances count fully).

If emissions respond to the price as \(\ln(\text{covered}/bce) = \bar\sigma\, V\, p\), the price that meets the cap is (A1.1):

\[ \hat p^{E}_t = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\ln(cap_t / bce_t)}{\bar\sigma_t\ V} & on_t = 1,\ 0 < cap_t < bce_t,\ \bar\sigma_t < 0,\ V > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{6.44}\]

The permit price used is the override row if the switch is on, else the fast estimate:

\[ p^{E}_t = on_t \times \begin{cases} ovr_t \ (\text{blank: the carbon price path } NCT_t) & \texttt{ETSPriceOverride} = \text{Yes} \\ \hat p^{E}_t & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{6.45}\]

\[ \tilde p^{E}_t = p^{E}_t\, V, \qquad \rho = 1 / V \tag{6.46}\]

where \(\tilde p^{E}\) is the tax-equivalent price (ets.pe) and \(\rho\) the revenue factor (ets.rf), the permit price per dollar of tax-equivalent price. All prices are USD per t CO2, real.

6.9.4 Effect on fuel use and revenue

The ETS replaces the carbon tax in covered sectors: the new carbon tax applies only where \(\kappa_{e,t} = 0\) (Chapter 2). The tax-equivalent price splits by the OBR share (M1.3, M1.4):

\[ A_{e,t} = \tilde p^{E}_t\ \kappa_{e,t}\ (1 - \omega_{e,t}), \qquad \Sigma_{e,t} = \big(b^{fb}_{G(e),t} + reg_{e,t}\big)\, a\ \mathbb{1}[\text{feebate covers } e] + \tilde p^{E}_t\ \kappa_{e,t}\ \omega_{e,t} \tag{6.47}\]

where

  • \(A_{e,t}\) is the price wedge, the auctioned part (etr, USD per t CO2). Times the CO2 factor of each fuel, it enters the after-tax fuel price, so usage and efficiency both respond, as for a carbon tax (Chapter 2, Chapter 3);
  • \(\Sigma_{e,t}\) is the shadow price (shpx): the ETS part, the free (OBR) share, is added to the feebate and regulation shadow price. It acts only on the efficiency margin of fuel use, as feebates do, and raises no revenue.

New-policy revenue includes the price wedge times the revenue factor \(\rho\): the permit price on the auctioned part of covered emissions. In power, the new-policy revenue deducts \(em_{k,CO_2}\, A_{pow}\, (1 - \rho)\). VAT on new policies is levied on the tax-equivalent ETS cost. The revenue equations are in Chapter 7.

6.9.5 Meeting the cap

The fast estimate uses one semi-elasticity, so the full model, where the response comes from prices and elasticities by fuel and subsector, does not hit the cap exactly. After CO2 is known, the model reports covered emissions, the gap and a proposed price for the next iteration:

\[ cov_t = \sum_e em_{e,t}\ \kappa_{e,t}, \qquad gap_t = \frac{cov_t}{cap_t} - 1 \tag{6.48}\]

\[ lr_t = \frac{\ln(cap_t / bce_t)}{\ln(cov_t / bce_t)}, \qquad p^{next}_t = \begin{cases} \hat p^{E}_t & p^{E}_t \le 0 \text{ or } lr_t = 0 \\ p^{E}_t\ lr_t^{\,\beta} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{6.49}\]

where

  • \(cov_t\) is covered CO2 (co2.ets), \(em_{e,t}\) the CO2 of ETS sector \(e\) (Equation 6.5);
  • \(gap_t\) is co2.gap (0 if there is no cap), 0 when the cap is met;
  • \(lr_t\) is the log ratio (ets.lr), 0 if \(cap_t \le 0\), \(cap_t \ge bce_t\) or \(cov_t \ge bce_t\) (the cap does not bind);
  • \(\beta\) = 0.5 is the convergence exponent (Settings C15), as legacy’s PricesAdjustedByErrorFactor;
  • \(p^{next}_t\) is the proposed price (ets.next).

To meet the cap, the proposed prices are fed back into the override row (with the override switch on) and the model is run again. The goal seek automates this with legacy’s damped log-space step:

  1. start from the fast estimate, or from the override row if it holds prices (setting ETSStartFreshContinue: StartFresh always starts from the fast estimate);
  2. step \(p \leftarrow p\,(p^{next}/p)^{\alpha}\) (a linear step if a price is 0);
  3. mix with the previous iterate (weight 0.3) from the second iteration;
  4. smooth lightly: 5% weight on a centred 3-year moving average;
  5. negative or invalid values keep the current price;
  6. halve \(\alpha\) when the worst error grows (minimum 0.0625), multiply it by 1.2 when the error more than halves (maximum 1);
  7. stop when the worst yearly \(|cov_t/cap_t - 1|\) over the ETS years is below the maximum error, or after the maximum number of iterations.

    se[t] = sum(bco2[e][t] * etsc[e][t] * semi[e] * (1 - (1 - OBREff) * obr[e][t]) for e) / bce[t] if bce[t] > 0 else 0
    est[t] = log(cap[t] / bce[t]) / (se[t] * vadj) if on[t] and 0 < cap[t] < bce[t] and se[t] < 0 and vadj > 0 else 0
    p[t] = 0 if not on[t] else ((ovr[t] if ovr[t] != blank else cptraj[t]) if ETSPriceOverride == "Yes" else est[t])
    pe[t], rf = p[t] * vadj, 1 / vadj
    for e in ets_sectors:
        etr[e][t]  = pe[t] * etsc[e][t] * (1 - obr[e][t])                       # price wedge
        shpx[e][t] = fb[group(e)][t] * ssc[e] + reg[e][t] + pe[t] * etsc[e][t] * obr[e][t]

# year loop, after CO2
for t in years:
    covered[t] = sum(co2_sec[e][t] * etsc[e][t] for e)
    gap[t] = covered[t] / cap[t] - 1 if cap[t] > 0 else 0
    lr[t] = 0 if cap[t] <= 0 or cap[t] >= bce[t] or covered[t] >= bce[t] else log(cap[t]/bce[t]) / log(covered[t]/bce[t])
    next_p[t] = est[t] if p[t] <= 0 or lr[t] == 0 else p[t] * lr[t] ** ETSNextPriceExponent

6.9.6 Limits

  • Power is covered like any other sector: the power model gives its emissions, and the ETS cost enters its generation cost (Chapter 4).
  • Benchmarks are relative to current emission intensity, and output is proxied by fuel use. Free allocation therefore moves with emissions, and the OBR share equals the benchmark. Benchmarks by sector are not built yet: every group takes the scenario’s auctioned proportion.
  • The volatility adjustment scales behaviour (the tax-equivalent price), not the permit price.
  • No banking, borrowing, price floors or ceilings, and no CBAM process emissions (the legacy CBAM block is placed but not read; industrial process CO2 of the inventory is separate and untaxed).

NoteDesign note: benchmarks instead of one auction share

This note records the design proposed for the model’s ETS. Today the model uses legacy’s one auctioned share per scenario (LD7); the benchmark-based design below is the intended extension. Everything after the split stays as in the legacy split.

  • Inputs per subsector: the covered flag and a benchmark path \(b(t)\), a share of the subsector’s base-year emission intensity (for example 0.9: free allocation at 90% of base-year intensity; a declining path tightens it).
  • Shares. Free allocation \(FA = b \times\) base-year intensity \(\times\) output, with output proxied by baseline fuel use (the model has no output variable). OBR share \(s = \min(FA / \text{covered emissions}, 1)\); auctioned share \(a = 1 - s\). To first order \(s \approx b\) (intensity unchanged). Using actual covered emissions would be circular through fuel use, so the recommendation is scenario-1 (baseline) covered emissions (option A of the cap design note); \(s\) is then a data-driven input.
  • Then as legacy. Effectiveness \(e = a + 0.5\, s\) per subsector; the auctioned part \(p \times a\) is a price wedge (revenue = \(p \times\) (covered emissions − FA)); the OBR part \(p \times s\) joins the sector shadow price (the feebate channel); the fast estimate \(p\) = (reduction left for the ETS) / \((\varepsilon\, e)\), with \(\varepsilon\) and \(e\) weighted by covered baseline emissions.
  • Override. An offline goal seek (a Python script with the same damped log-space step) writes a row of prices to paste. No VBA and no circular reference.

Without benchmarks the ETS is fully effective (permit price = carbon price path \(\times\) coverage, as effective as a carbon tax, per an earlier user decision), and the auction share only affects revenue. With benchmarks, the OBR part moves from the price wedge to the shadow price, so emissions fall less for the same \(p\), and revenue falls. A benchmark of 0 (full auctioning) reproduces the fully effective case exactly.

  1. The fast estimate uses legacy’s sector semi-elasticities. The model could derive them from its own elasticities (a one-off calibration run) to keep them consistent.
  2. Legacy’s linear split (\(p \times a\)) against a log form (legacy’s own TBC note).
  3. Legacy’s relative price 1.1 (auctioned ETS against tax) and the risk-premium inputs (rows 1811-1813) are not used by the legacy fast estimate as cached; the model uses them through \(V\) (LD8).
