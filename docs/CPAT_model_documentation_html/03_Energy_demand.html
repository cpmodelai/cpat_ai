3  Energy demand

This chapter explains how the model projects the use of each fuel in each sector of the economy, year by year. Fuel use is the quantity at the heart of the model: emissions, revenues, the GDP effect and the household effects are all fuel use multiplied by something else.

The core is one behavioural equation, the same for every sector and fuel: last year’s use grows with GDP, shrinks with autonomous efficiency gains, and responds to the after-tax price through two margins, how much the equipment is used and how efficient it is. A handful of special cases sit around it: biofuels blended into road fuels, renewables used outside the power sector, a possible shift of households from modern cooking fuels to biomass, and cooking fuel that is initially exempt from price increases.

The chapter also computes electricity demand by sector, which it hands to the power model (Chapter 4), and it closes with the energy totals by sector group and fuel and their change against the baseline.

The methodology describes this block in methodology, section 3.3. The prices that drive it are built in Chapter 2; road fuel use under the engineer model is in Chapter 5; the GDP growth that feeds the income term comes from Chapter 7.

Indices. Every equation holds for each scenario \(o\) and country \(c\), which are omitted. Sector (subsector) \(s\), fuel \(f\), sector group \(g\) and year \(t\) are shown where they matter; electricity demand uses the demand group \(d\). A value of the baseline (scenario 1) in the same year carries the superscript \(B\).

3.1 Overview

The model distinguishes 16 sectors (Excel-AI calls them subsectors) in four groups, and 9 fuels. Power generation is a fifth group whose fuel input comes from Chapter 4. The tables below give, for each sector and fuel, the keys that the fuel-use equation reads: which row of the elasticity table applies, which additional efficiency input applies, and which electricity demand group the sector belongs to.

Two more “fuels” appear outside this grid: renewables used directly in aviation and navigation (Section 3.3), and electricity, whose demand by sector is computed here but which is supplied by the power model (Section 3.7).

Within one year the block runs in a fixed order. GDP growth is known first, because the GDP effect of a policy enters one year later; electricity demand reads last year’s power price; fuel use reads this year’s after-tax prices, which were computed for all years before the year loop.

3.2 The fuel-use equation

Each year, fuel use in a sector is last year’s use multiplied by five growth factors. This is the constant-elasticity form of the methodology (methodology, section 3.3.3), with three additions: the shadow price of non-pricing policies acts on the efficiency margin only, an additional policy-induced efficiency gain joins the autonomous one, and the income elasticity is adjusted to the country’s income level.

In full (M3.1):

\[ \frac{F^{e}_{s,f,t}}{F^{e}_{s,f,t-1}} = \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha_{s,f} + \alpha^{+}_{g(s)}}\right)^{1 + \epsilon_{U}} \;\Psi_t\; \left(1 + g_t\right)^{\epsilon_{Y}\, inc_t} \left(\frac{p_{s,f,t}}{p_{s,f,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{U}} \left(\frac{p_{s,f,t} + sh_{s,f,t}}{p_{s,f,t-1} + sh_{s,f,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{F}(1 + \epsilon_{U})} \tag{3.1}\]

where

  • \(F^{e}_{s,f,t}\) is fuel use by the elasticity model (ktoe; code enel); in the base year \(t_0\) it is the base-year use of the energy data (Section 3.2.6);
  • \(\epsilon_Y\), \(\epsilon_U\), \(\epsilon_F\) are the income, usage and efficiency elasticities of the fuel in the sector (they depend on the country, the fuel and the sector; the indices are dropped);
  • \(\alpha_{s,f}\) is the autonomous energy efficiency improvement (share per year) and \(\alpha^{+}_{g(s)}\) the additional policy-induced efficiency gain of the sector’s efficiency group (Section 3.2.1);
  • \(\Psi_t\) is the exogenous demand shock factor (Section 3.2.5);
  • \(g_t\) is real GDP growth after the GDP feedback and \(inc_t\) the income-level factor on the GDP elasticities (Section 3.2.2);
  • \(p_{s,f,t}\) is the after-tax price of the fuel in the sector (USD per GJ, real; code atp) and \(sh_{s,f,t}\) the shadow price of non-pricing policies (USD per GJ; code shp), both from Chapter 2 (Section 3.2.4).

The methodology writes the GDP term as \((Y_t / Y_{t-1})^{\epsilon_Y}\), total real GDP (not per capita). The model writes the same ratio as \(1 + g_t\).

The equation is easier to read as a product of named factors. Each is one idea:

\[ F^{e}_{s,f,t} = F^{e}_{s,f,t-1} \cdot A_{s,f} \cdot \Psi_t \cdot G_{s,f,t} \cdot U_{s,f,t} \cdot E_{s,f,t} \tag{3.2}\]

The methodology assumes that all effects take place within the year: the elasticities are long-term elasticities applied in one step. CPAT is therefore better suited to anticipated and progressively phased policies over the medium term than to sudden shocks.

3.2.1 Autonomous and additional efficiency

Equipment becomes more efficient over time even without new policies. The autonomous energy efficiency improvement \(\alpha\) captures this; the methodology sets it between 0.5% and 1% per year depending on the fuel and sector (methodology, section 3.3.5.6).

A scenario can add a policy-induced gain \(\alpha^{+}\) for three groups of sectors:

\[ A_{s,f} = \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha_{s,f} + \alpha^{+}_{g(s)}}\right)^{1 + \epsilon_{U}} \tag{3.3}\]

where

  • \(\alpha_{s,f}\) is the autonomous efficiency gain of the sector’s elasticity sector and the fuel’s autonomous efficiency fuel (Table 3.1, Table 3.2); gasoline and diesel take the oil value;
  • \(\alpha^{+}_{g(s)}\) is the scenario input of the sector’s additional efficiency group (share per year, default 0).

The exponent \(1 + \epsilon_U\) (with \(\epsilon_U < 0\)) is the rebound: more efficient equipment makes each unit of service cheaper, so part of the saving is used up by more use. The methodology puts this rebound from exogenous efficiency between 20% and 60% across fuels and sectors.

3.2.2 Income term, income-level adjustment and GDP feedback

Energy demand grows with the economy. The income term raises GDP growth to the power of the income elasticity, and the income elasticity is scaled by a factor that depends on the country’s GDP per capita:

\[ G_{s,f,t} = \left(1 + g_t\right)^{\epsilon_{Y}\, inc_t} \tag{3.4}\]

where \(\epsilon_Y\) is the income (GDP) elasticity of the fuel in the sector, \(g_t\) real GDP growth after the feedback, and \(inc_t\) the income-level factor.

The income-level factor. The literature finds that income elasticities rise as countries leave low income, peak around lower-middle income (about USD 3,000 per capita) and then decline towards the level of developed countries (methodology, section 3.3.5.2). The model draws this reverse-U as a polynomial in log GDP per capita:

\[ x_t = \min\left(\bar{x},\ \ln y_{t-1}\right), \qquad inc_t = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\max\left(0,\ a x_t^4 + b x_t^3 + c x_t^2 + d x_t + e\right)}{n} & \text{if IncElAdj is Yes} \\[1ex] 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{3.5}\]

where

  • \(y_{t-1}\) is baseline real GDP per capita of the previous year (USD; in the base year, the base year’s own value), from the macro series (real GDP over population);
  • \(\bar{x} = 10\) caps log GDP per capita (about USD 22,000, the methodology’s “developed country” level);
  • \(a, \dots, e\) and \(n\) are fixed constants (table below), \(n = 0.74\) normalising the factor to the global average income elasticity of Burke and Csereklyei (2016);
  • IncElAdj is the scenario switch “Adjust income elasticities for GDP levels?” (default Yes*; a starred Yes counts as Yes).

The factor is a data path: it is computed from baseline GDP per capita before the year loop (code incp, then inc.ela.adj after the switch), so it is the same in every scenario. The same factor multiplies the GDP elasticities of electricity demand (Section 3.7).

The GDP feedback. \(g_t\) is not the baseline growth rate but growth after the effect of the policy’s revenue use on GDP (methodology, section 4.7). The effect is computed in Chapter 7 and enters with a lag of one year, as a change in the GDP level:

\[ \ell_t = 1 + \epsilon^{GDP}_{t-1}, \qquad 1 + g_t = \left(1 + g^{0}_t\right)\frac{\ell_t}{\ell_{t-1}} \tag{3.6}\]

where \(g^{0}_t\) is baseline real GDP growth (IMF WEO), \(\epsilon^{GDP}_{t-1}\) last year’s GDP effect (share of GDP; 0 when endogenous GDP effects are switched off) and \(\ell_t\) the GDP level factor (\(\ell_{t_0} = 1\); code gdp.lvl). The baseline has no fiscal space of its own, so its growth is \(g^{0}_t\). Because the effect is lagged, fuel use in year \(t\) never depends on revenue in year \(t\): there is no circular reference. This is the “rebound” through GDP that Appendix E of the methodology mentions under “Include endogenous GDP effects?”.

Residential biomass. Residential biomass is treated as an inferior good by default: its income elasticity is set to 0 unless the setting BiomassNormal (“Biomass is a normal good”, read from the baseline column; default No) is Yes.

3.2.3 Price terms: usage, efficiency and rebound

A higher price reduces fuel use through two margins (methodology, section 3.3.5.3):

  • the usage (intensive) margin: existing equipment is used less (fewer kilometres driven), elasticity \(\epsilon_U\);
  • the efficiency (extensive) margin: equipment is replaced by more efficient equipment, elasticity \(\epsilon_F\).

\[ U_{s,f,t} = \left(\frac{p_{s,f,t}}{p_{s,f,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{U}}, \qquad E_{s,f,t} = \left(\frac{p_{s,f,t} + sh_{s,f,t}}{p_{s,f,t-1} + sh_{s,f,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{F}(1 + \epsilon_{U})} \tag{3.7}\]

where \(p\) is the after-tax price and \(sh\) the shadow price, both per GJ. The after-tax price (M2.5) includes existing taxes and subsidies, the new carbon tax, the auctioned ETS cost, the new excise, the change in existing carbon pricing, the subsidy and price-control phase-outs, and VAT on all of them; it has a floor of USD 0.01 per GJ. It is built in Chapter 2.

Rebound. When the shadow price is zero the two price terms combine into one:

\[ U_{s,f,t}\,E_{s,f,t} = \left(\frac{p_{s,f,t}}{p_{s,f,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{U} + \epsilon_{F} + \epsilon_{U}\epsilon_{F}} \tag{3.8}\]

The cross term \(\epsilon_U \epsilon_F\) is positive (both elasticities are negative): it is the direct rebound, the extra use of equipment that has become cheaper to run (methodology, section 3.3.5.4). With \(\epsilon_U = \epsilon_F = -0.3\) the combined exponent is \(-0.51\) instead of \(-0.6\), so the rebound offsets about 15% of the price response (the methodology quotes 15.5%, against 26-29% in a meta-analysis of 74 studies).

3.2.4 Shadow prices of non-pricing policies

Some policies do not raise the fuel price but push towards more efficient equipment: feebates, efficiency regulations, vehicle fuel-economy standards, and the free (output-based) allocation of an ETS. The model expresses each as a shadow price per tonne of CO2, which enters the efficiency term only (Equation 3.7) and raises no revenue. A regulation does not make fuel dearer to use, so it has no usage effect; it changes which equipment is bought.

Demand reads the shadow price per GJ (M2.6):

\[ sh_{s,f,t} = \Sigma_{s,t}\; ef_{s,f} \tag{3.9}\]

where \(ef_{s,f}\) is the calibrated CO2 emission factor (tCO2 per GJ; Chapter 6) and \(\Sigma_{s,t}\) the shadow price of the sector (USD per tCO2; code shpx; M1.4), built in Chapter 2 from three parts:

\[ \Sigma_{s,t} = \underbrace{b_{g(s),t}\, a_{s}}_{\text{feebate}} \;+\; \underbrace{reg_{s,t}}_{\text{regulation}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tilde{p}^{E}_t\, \kappa_{s,t}\, \omega_{s,t}}_{\text{ETS free allocation}} \tag{3.10}\]

where

  • \(b_{g,t}\) is the feebate rate of the sector’s feebate group (USD per tCO2) and \(a_s = \mathbb{1}[\text{feebate covers } s] \times\) SPPEffAdjFeebates the share of the feebate on the efficiency margin (code ssc; ScenarioDefinition row 251, default 1);
  • \(reg_{s,t}\) is the regulation shadow price (below);
  • \(\tilde{p}^{E}_t\) is the tax-equivalent ETS price, \(\kappa_{s,t}\) the new ETS coverage and \(\omega_{s,t}\) the share allocated free (output-based) (Chapter 6).

Regulations as shadow prices. The scenario’s main policy (ScenarioSelected, ScenarioDefinition row 13) picks a row of the legacy table of main scenarios. Explicit-pricing scenarios (carbon tax, total carbon price, coal excise, road fuel tax, electricity emissions tax and excise) use the carbon price path as the carbon tax. Shadow-pricing scenarios (feebates, power supply feebates, energy efficiency regulations, power feebate, vehicle fuel economy, residential and industrial efficiency regulations, and “Baseline”) have no explicit carbon tax; the carbon price path becomes a shadow price:

\[ reg_{s,t} = \left(\tau_t - \tau^{x}_t\right) \cdot r \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{the scenario covers } s] \tag{3.11}\]

where \(\tau_t\) is the carbon price path (code cptraj), \(\tau^{x}_t\) the explicit carbon price (equal to \(\tau_t\) in an explicit scenario and 0 otherwise; code cpx), so the bracket is the shadow carbon price (code cp.shd); \(r\) is the efficiency adjustment of the scenario (code regadj) and the coverage comes from the scenario table (code regc).

A text that is not in the scenario table counts as explicit pricing (the shipped scenarios use " - Carbon tax"). Power demand responds to the same shadow prices through the costs of the power model (Chapter 4). The shadow price is not removed for exempt residential LPG and kerosene (Section 3.5), as legacy.

3.2.5 Exogenous demand shock

Legacy CPAT calibrates the first projection years to observed emissions with a one-off shock on energy demand. The model keeps it as a factor:

\[ \Psi_t = 1 + \text{shock}_t \cdot \text{switch} \tag{3.12}\]

where \(\text{shock}_t\) is the country’s shock (share; code shock) and the switch is the setting “Fuel use and power demand: apply the legacy 2023-2024 shock” (Settings C19; 1 = on, the default, as legacy).

The shock values are typed calibration values of the legacy GHGs tab (rows 1202-1388, columns G and H), by country, for the first two projection years (2023 and 2024) and 0 afterwards; a country without a value gets 0. For Egypt they are −20.8% in 2023 and +9.2% in 2024. The same shock multiplies electricity demand, scaled there by EngineerModelProportionOfCovidFactor (Section 3.7).

The methodology describes the shock as a Covid adjustment of 2020 and 2021, calibrated to emissions outturns (methodology, section 3.3.4.4). The model’s base year is 2022, so the shock that remains is legacy’s 2023-2024 calibration.

3.2.6 Elasticities and base-year fuel use

Every fuel and sector pair reads its parameters from one table, the fuel | subsector table (Excel-AI sheet Inputs rows 5-148; A3.2).

Elasticities (A3.1). Legacy CPAT holds income, usage and efficiency elasticities for 8 elasticity fuels (coal, natural gas, gasoline, diesel, oil products, biomass, renewables, electricity) in 4 elasticity sectors (transport, residential, industry, services), and autonomous efficiency gains for 6 fuels (coal, gas, oil, biomass, renewables, electricity) in the same sectors. The country’s value is read from the legacy Elasticities table (column “Elasticity use”; Excel-AI sheet Inputs_ela). A fuel and sector pair takes the values of its elasticity fuel and elasticity sector (Table 3.1, Table 3.2). A blank cell reads as 0.

The methodology explains how these values were chosen (methodology, section 3.3.5): 32 base income elasticities from Burke and Csereklyei (2016), adjusted for income levels after Gertler and others (2016); price elasticities from the meta-study of Labandeira and others (2017), split into the usage and efficiency margins and raised slightly for developing countries; all checked against in-house databases and other models.

Base-year fuel use. \(F^{e}_{s,f,t_0}\) comes from one of two sources, chosen by BalancesOrConsumption (ScenarioDefinition row 234; default Balances):

  • Balances: the IEA energy balance flow of the sector, plus a second signed flow where there is one. Fuel transformation is the negative of own use plus heat (both flows with sign −1), so the residual between primary and final consumption becomes an additional industrial sector (methodology, section 3.3.4.5).
  • EnergyCons: the CPAT energy consumption table (enc.<fuel> of the sector; blank or missing is 0).

Other energy use contains principally military fuel use; it follows the same equation (methodology, section 3.3.4.6). Whether it is taxed is a matter of coverage (Chapter 2).

TipPseudocode: fuel use by the elasticity model 
# before the year loop (prices are known for every year)
for s in sectors:
    for f in fuels:
        # additional efficiency group: road vehicles / residential / industrial
        alpha_add = AdditionalEfficiency[eff_group[s]]
        aef[s,f] = (1 / (1 + alpha[s,f] + alpha_add)) ** (1 + eu[s,f])
        eff_exp[s,f] = ef[s,f] * (1 + eu[s,f])

# year loop
for t in years:
    g = gdp.pos.pct[t]                     # growth after the GDP feedback (lag 1)
    GDPI = (1 + g) ** inc.ela.adj[t] - 1   # income-level factor on the elasticity
    SHOCK = 1 + shock[t] * ApplyDemandShock
    for s in sectors:
        for f in fuels:
            if t == t0:
                enel[s,f,t] = ener0[s,f]   # energy balance or EnergyCons
                continue
            usage = (atp[s,f,t] / atp[s,f,t-1]) ** eu[s,f]
            effic = ((atp[s,f,t] + shp[s,f,t])
                     / (atp[s,f,t-1] + shp[s,f,t-1])) ** eff_exp[s,f]
            # ey = 0 for residential biomass unless BiomassNormal
            enel[s,f,t] = (enel[s,f,t-1] * aef[s,f] * (1 + GDPI) ** ey[s,f]
                           * usage * effic * SHOCK)

3.3 Biofuels, jet fuel and self-generated renewables

A few fuels do not fit the grid of 9 fuels by 16 sectors (methodology, section 3.3.4.1). Jet fuel is a fuel of its own, used in domestic aviation; it follows the fuel-use equation with the oil-products elasticities and is priced at the kerosene price. Road biomass is split into three biofuels, and aviation and navigation can use renewables directly (A3.3).

Blended biofuels. Bioethanol and biodiesel are blended into gasoline and diesel, so they follow the road use of the fossil fuel they are blended into:

\[ B^{bgs}_t = B^{bgs}_{t-1}\,\frac{F^{e}_{rod,gso,t}}{F^{e}_{rod,gso,t-1}}, \qquad B^{bdi}_t = B^{bdi}_{t-1}\,\frac{F^{e}_{rod,die,t}}{F^{e}_{rod,die,t-1}} \tag{3.13}\]

where \(B^{bgs}\) and \(B^{bdi}\) are road bioethanol and biodiesel (ktoe; code trbe). Where last year’s fossil use is 0 the biofuel holds its value.

Other biofuels follow the fuel-use equation with the oil | transport elasticities and autonomous efficiency, the road vehicles additional efficiency, and the road biomass price:

\[ B^{obf}_t = B^{obf}_{t-1}\, \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha^{oil,tra} + \alpha^{+}_{rod}}\right)^{1 + \epsilon_{U}} \Psi_t \left(1 + g_t\right)^{\epsilon_{Y}\,inc_t} \left(\frac{p_{rod,bio,t}}{p_{rod,bio,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{U}} \left(\frac{p_{rod,bio,t} + sh_{rod,bio,t}}{p_{rod,bio,t-1} + sh_{rod,bio,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{F}(1+\epsilon_{U})} \tag{3.14}\]

with \(\epsilon_Y, \epsilon_U, \epsilon_F\) of oil products in transport (code trp.bfe; efficiency factor code trbea). Road biomass is the sum of the three:

\[ F^{e}_{rod,bio,t} = B^{bgs}_t + B^{bdi}_t + B^{obf}_t \tag{3.15}\]

The base-year biofuels are the road flows of the energy balance (bioethanol, biodiesel, other biofuels) or the EnergyCons values, following BalancesOrConsumption.

Self-generated renewables. Renewable energy used directly in domestic aviation and navigation (base year: the EnergyCons value enc.ren of the sector) follows the fuel-use equation with the renewables | transport elasticities, the road vehicles additional efficiency and, as its price, the levelised investment cost of solar power from Chapter 4 (methodology, section 3.3.4.2):

\[ R_{s,t} = R_{s,t-1} \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha^{ren,tra} + \alpha^{+}_{rod}}\right)^{1 + \epsilon_{U}} \Psi_t \left(1 + g_t\right)^{\epsilon_{Y}\,inc_t} \left(\frac{tic^{sol}_t}{tic^{sol}_{t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{U} + \epsilon_{F}(1+\epsilon_{U})}, \qquad s \in \{avi, nav\} \tag{3.16}\]

where \(R_{s,t}\) is renewable use (ktoe; code ener with fuel ren), \(tic^{sol}_t\) the levelised investment cost of solar (USD per kWh; code trpren) and the elasticities those of renewables in transport (code trp.rne; efficiency factor code trrena). There is no shadow price on this price. Renewables count in the sector, group and grand totals, but not in any fuel total (Section 3.8).

TipPseudocode: biofuels and self-generated renewables 
for t in years:
    # blended biofuels follow the fossil fuel they are blended into
    for b, fossil in (("bgs", "gso"), ("bdi", "die")):
        if t == t0:
            trbe[rod,b,t] = road_biofuel_base[b]       # balance or EnergyCons
        elif enel[rod,fossil,t-1] == 0:
            trbe[rod,b,t] = trbe[rod,b,t-1]
        else:
            growth = enel[rod,fossil,t] / enel[rod,fossil,t-1]
            trbe[rod,b,t] = trbe[rod,b,t-1] * growth

    # other biofuels: oil | transport elasticities, road biomass price
    add = AdditionalEfficiencyRoadVehicle
    trbea = (1 / (1 + aei[oil,tra] + add)) ** (1 + usg[oil,tra])
    if t == t0:
        trbe[rod,obf,t] = road_biofuel_base["obf"]
    else:
        usage = (atp[rod,bio,t] / atp[rod,bio,t-1]) ** usg[oil,tra]
        effic = ((atp[rod,bio,t] + shp[rod,bio,t])
                 / (atp[rod,bio,t-1] + shp[rod,bio,t-1])) \
                ** (eff[oil,tra] * (1 + usg[oil,tra]))

                           * (1 + GDPI) ** inc[oil,tra] * usage * effic * SHOCK)
    trbe[rod,bio,t] = trbe[rod,bgs,t] + trbe[rod,bdi,t] + trbe[rod,obf,t]

    for s in ("avi", "nav"):

            continue
        r = trpren[t] / trpren[t-1]

        ener[s,ren,t] = (ener[s,ren,t-1] * trrena

3.4 Residential leakage to biomass

When modern cooking fuels become dearer, some households may switch back to traditional biomass. This “leakage” reduces the emission savings and worsens household air pollution (methodology, section 3.3.4.3; air pollution effects in methodology, section 6.6.5).

The option is off by default. It is switched on by BiomassLeakage (“Include leakage to biomass in residential sector”, ScenarioDefinition row 325, read from the baseline column; on when it starts with “Yes”). It applies only to the country’s most-used cooking fuel, and only if that fuel is natural gas, LPG or kerosene (A3.4).

The calculation runs in five steps for each leaking fuel \(m \in \{nga, lpg, ker\}\) in the residential sector, from the first projection year (all flows are 0 in the base year).

Step 1: the new policy in the price. Only new policies cause leakage, not movements of the international price:

\[ W_{m,t} = N_{res,m,t}\left(1 + v_m\right) \tag{3.17}\]

where \(N_{res,m,t}\) is the total new policy per GJ of the residential row (new carbon tax, ETS cost and new excise; code nce) and \(v_m\) its VAT rate. An exempt fuel (Section 3.5) has no new policy, so it does not leak.

Step 2: the share that leaks. A rise in the new policy relative to last year’s price moves a share of the fuel to biomass:

\[ \pi_{m,t} = \mathbb{1}[\text{on},\ m = m^{*}] \cdot \min\left( \left(1 + \frac{W_{m,t} - W_{m,t-1}}{p_{res,m,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma \eta_{bio} / (\eta_m L)} - 1,\ 1 \right) \tag{3.18}\]

where

  • \(m^{*}\) is the country’s most-used cooking fuel (WHO cooking data, overall sample; LPG if the country has no entry);
  • \(\sigma\) is the cross-price elasticity of biomass with respect to LPG (default 0.25; legacy Elasticities C131, legacy row 360);
  • \(\eta_m\), \(\eta_{bio}\) are the cookstove efficiencies of the fuel and of biomass (table below);
  • \(L\) is the substitution lag (1 year, sheet Constants; it divides the exponent);
  • \(p_{res,m,t-1}\) is last year’s after-tax price of the fuel.

Step 3: the change in fossil use. The share is read as a share of useful energy, so it is weighted by the stove efficiency of the fuel:

\[ d_{m,t} = -\left(F_{res,m,t-1} - X_{m,t-1}\right)\eta_m\,\pi_{m,t} \tag{3.19}\]

where \(F_{res,m,t-1}\) is last year’s actual fuel use (which already includes earlier leakage) and \(X_{m,t-1}\) the initially exempt use (Section 3.5; 0 unless that option is on), which does not leak.

Step 4: the biomass that replaces it. Biomass supplies the same useful energy:

\[ b_{m,t} = -d_{m,t}\,\frac{\eta_m}{\eta_{bio}}, \qquad \text{so that}\quad \eta_m d_{m,t} + \eta_{bio} b_{m,t} = 0 \tag{3.20}\]

Step 5: cumulative changes. The yearly changes add up to level shifts that are added to residential fuel use:

\[ Lk_{m,t} = Lk_{m,t-1} + d_{m,t} + \left(X^{a}_{m,t} - X^{a}_{m,t-1}\right), \qquad Lk_{bio,t} = Lk_{bio,t-1} + \sum_{m} b_{m,t} \tag{3.21}\]

where \(X^{a}\) is the adjustment for initially exempt use (Equation 3.24; 0 unless that option is on), and \(Lk_{t_0} = 0\). The net change \(\sum_m Lk_m + Lk_{bio}\) is reported as a memo row (code lks).

How the leakage behaves:

  • A fall in the new policy moves fuel back from biomass (\(\pi < 0\)). The share is capped at 1.
  • A policy that rises and then stays flat moves fuel only in the years it rises. The shift then stays as a level shift: \(Lk\) is not grown with GDP.
  • With natural gas and a 10% rise in its price from new policies, the exponent is \(0.25 \times 0.20 / 0.58 \approx 0.086\), so \(\pi \approx 0.8\%\); the fossil change is \(\pi \eta_{nga} \approx 0.5\%\) of gas use, replaced by \(0.5\% \times 0.58/0.20 \approx 1.4\%\) of gas use in biomass (in ktoe).
  • Every product of residential fuel use follows the leakage: CO2, CH4, local pollutants, revenues, the weights of the average prices and the distribution module. \(F^{e}\) stays the elasticity model.

3.5 Exempt residential cooking fuel

Two options protect households’ cooking fuel from new policies. Both act through the price or the quantity of the residential rows.

Always exempt residential LPG and kerosene. With AlwaysExemptResLPGKer (ScenarioDefinition row 268, by scenario; default No*; a starred Yes* counts as Yes) the new carbon tax, the new ETS cost and the new excise of the residential LPG and kerosene rows are 0 (exemption factor, code exf; Chapter 2). Their after-tax price then keeps only existing taxes, the change in existing carbon pricing and the subsidy phase-outs, so their demand responds only to those. The shadow price stays, and so does their place in ETS-covered emissions where the residential sector is in an ETS. Exempt fuels do not leak to biomass. Exemptions are introduced in user guide, section 1.2.4.

Initially exempt demand of the most-used cooking fuel. With ExemptMostCook = Yes (row 312), the use of the most-used cooking fuel by the bottom deciles of households is initially shielded from the price response, which is phased in later. The shielded share \(\chi\) is the spending of the bottom ExemptCookDeciles deciles (row 313, default 4) on the fuel, over the economy-wide spending on it: household survey budget share times consumption per capita times the weight of each decile (Settings C83, ExemptCookShare).

The exempt use starts as a share of base-year use and then grows with the elasticity model, but with the usage response to the price removed until the phase-in:

\[ X_{m,t_0} = \chi\,F^{e}_{res,m,t_0}, \qquad X_{m,t} = X_{m,t-1}\;\frac{F^{e}_{res,m,t}}{F^{e}_{res,m,t-1}}\;\left(\frac{p_{res,m,t}}{p_{res,m,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_U(\phi_t - 1)} \tag{3.22}\]

where \(m\) is the most-used cooking fuel (\(\chi = 0\) for the other fuels), \(\epsilon_U\) its residential usage elasticity, and \(\phi_t\) the phase-in of the usage response:

\[ \phi_t = \begin{cases} \min\left(\max\left(\dfrac{t + 1 - T^{pha}}{N^{pha}},\ 0\right),\ 1\right) & \text{if ExemptPhaseout} \\[1ex] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{3.23}\]

with \(T^{pha}\) = YearPha (row 50, default 2027), \(N^{pha}\) = ExemptPhaseoutPeriod (row 51, default 5 years) and ExemptPhaseout (row 49, default FALSE). Where last year’s use or price is 0, the ratio is taken as 1.

\(F^{e}\) already contains the usage term \((p_t/p_{t-1})^{\epsilon_U}\); the last factor multiplies it by \((p_t/p_{t-1})^{-\epsilon_U(1-\phi_t)}\), which takes out the share \(1 - \phi_t\) of it. Before the phase-in the exempt use keeps only the income, efficiency and shock terms; once \(\phi_t = 1\) it responds like the rest.

The difference with the share of the elasticity model is added to residential fuel use through the leakage rows (Equation 3.21):

\[ X^{a}_{m,t} = X_{m,t} - \chi\,F^{e}_{res,m,t}, \qquad X^{a}_{m,t_0} = 0 \tag{3.24}\]

So residential use of the cooking fuel is \((1-\chi)F^{e} + X\) plus the leakage, and the leakage is computed on the use that is not exempt.

3.6 Fuel use used by the rest of the model

The elasticity model \(F^{e}\) is not always the fuel use that the rest of the model reads. Two things can replace or adjust it: the road engineer model, and the residential adjustments above. The result is the fuel use \(F\) (code ener) used everywhere else (M3.2):

\[ F_{s,f,t} = \begin{cases} F^{T}_{f,t} & s = \text{road},\ f \in \{gso, die, lpg, nga, bio\},\ \text{TrModel} = \text{Engineer} \\ F^{e}_{s,f,t} + Lk_{s,f,t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{3.25}\]

where

  • \(F^{T}_{f,t}\) is road fuel use from the engineer road model, calibrated to the energy balance in the base year (Chapter 5); it covers gasoline, diesel, LPG, natural gas and biofuels;
  • \(F^{e}\) is the elasticity model (Equation 3.1), with road biomass from Equation 3.15;
  • \(Lk_{s,f,t}\) is the cumulative residential leakage and exempt-use adjustment (Equation 3.21) for residential natural gas, LPG, kerosene and biomass, and 0 for every other pair.

TrModel (ScenarioDefinition row 338) is Elasticity by default; then road fuel use stays the elasticity model’s and the engineer model’s results go only to the transport co-benefits.

Everything downstream reads \(F\): CO2 and other emissions (Chapter 6), revenues and the GDP effect (Chapter 7), the price averages and indices (Chapter 2) and the distribution module (Chapter 8).

3.7 Electricity demand

Electricity demand by sector follows the same logic as fuel use, with its own elasticities by demand group. It is computed at the start of the year, before the power model, which turns it into the generation required (Chapter 4). The methodology describes it in methodology, section 3.4.2.3 (M4.1).

Sector policies (carbon tax, ETS, feebates, fuel price reform) act on electricity demand only through the electricity price, which carries the policy cost of power generation. The demand price is a blend of last year’s end-user prices, so demand never depends on this year’s power price:

\[ \bar{p}_{d,t} = w_d\,p^{res}_{t-1} + \left(1 - w_d\right) p^{ind}_{t-1} \tag{3.26}\]

where \(p^{res}\) and \(p^{ind}\) are the residential and industrial end-user electricity prices (USD per kWh, real; Chapter 4) and \(w_d\) the weight of the residential price in demand group \(d\). In the base year the demand price is the price of the year before (data).

The demand multiple of each group combines the same terms as fuel use:

\[ M_{d,t} = \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha_d + \alpha^{+}_{pow}}\right)^{1 + \epsilon_{U,d}} \left(1 + g_t\right)^{\epsilon_{Y,d}\,inc_t} \left(\frac{\bar{p}_{d,t}}{\bar{p}_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\epsilon_{U,d} + \epsilon_{F,d} + \epsilon_{U,d}\epsilon_{F,d}} \left(1 + \text{shock}_t \cdot \text{switch} \cdot \kappa^{cov}\right) \tag{3.27}\]

where

  • \(\alpha_d\), \(\epsilon_{Y,d}\), \(\epsilon_{U,d}\), \(\epsilon_{F,d}\) are the electricity elasticities and autonomous efficiency gain of the group (electricity row of the elasticity table, in the group’s elasticity sector);
  • \(\alpha^{+}_{pow}\) is AdditionalEfficiencyPower (ScenarioDefinition row 242, by scenario, default 0);
  • \(\kappa^{cov}\) is EngineerModelProportionOfCovidFactor, the share of the shock passed on to power demand (ScenarioDefinition row 372, default 1);
  • there is no shadow price term: the shadow prices reach power through its own costs (Chapter 4).

Demand of each sector is last year’s demand times its group’s multiple:

\[ E_{s,t} = \begin{cases} E^{T}_{t} & s = \text{road and TrModel} = \text{Engineer} \\ E_{s,t-1}\,M_{d(s),t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad E_{s,t_0} = \text{data} \tag{3.28}\]

where \(E_{s,t}\) is electricity demand (ktoe; code dem), \(E^{T}\) road electricity from the engineer road model (Chapter 5) and \(d(s)\) the demand group of the sector (Table 3.1). Demand covers 15 sectors (fuel transformation has none). The base-year demand comes from the energy balance.

The power model (Chapter 4) adds up demand, \(E_t = \sum_s E_{s,t}\), converts it into required generation and splits consumption into residential and industrial users.

A memo, not a fuel. Electricity demand is shown in the sector blocks and summed by sector group (code eldem), but it is not part of the fuel totals: the fuels burnt to generate it are (Section 3.8). Nothing is counted twice.

3.8 Energy totals and changes against the baseline

The last step adds fuel use up by sector group and by fuel, adds the fuels burnt in power plants, and compares every total with the baseline of the same year (M3.3).

By sector. The total of a sector is the sum over its fuels, plus renewables in aviation and navigation:

\[ F_{s,t} = \sum_{f} F_{s,f,t} + R_{s,t}\,\mathbb{1}[s \in \{avi, nav\}], \qquad F_{g,t} = \sum_{s \in g} F_{s,t} \tag{3.29}\]

for the sector groups \(g\) transport, buildings, industry and other energy use (Table 3.1).

Power. The power group is the fuel input of power generation, coal, gas, oil and biomass (code pet; Chapter 4):

\[ F_{pow,t} = \sum_{k \in \{coa,\, nga,\, oop,\, bio\}} F^{pow}_{k,t} \tag{3.30}\]

Nuclear, wind, solar, hydro and other renewables have no fuel input in this total.

By fuel and in total.

\[ F_{f,t} = \sum_{s} F_{s,f,t} + F^{pow}_{f,t}, \qquad F_t = \sum_{s} F_{s,t} + F_{pow,t} \tag{3.31}\]

where \(F^{pow}_{f,t}\) is the generation fuel use of the type that burns fuel \(f\) (0 for the other fuels). Renewables in aviation and navigation are in \(F_t\) but in no fuel total. A check row confirms that the totals agree:

\[ \left|\sum_{s} F_{s,t} + F_{pow,t} - F_t\right| + \left|\sum_{f} F_{f,t} + R_{avi,t} + R_{nav,t} - F_t\right| = 0 \tag{3.32}\]

Change against the baseline. Each total \(x\) (the 9 fuel totals, the 4 sector groups, power and the grand total) is compared with the baseline value of the same year:

\[ \Delta F_{x,t} = F_{x,t} - F^{B}_{x,t}, \qquad \%\Delta F_{x,t} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\Delta F_{x,t}}{F_{x,t} - \Delta F_{x,t}} & F_{x,t} - \Delta F_{x,t} \ne 0 \\[1ex] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{3.33}\]

where \(F^{B}_{x,t}\) is scenario 1’s value in the same year (codes echg and epct). In the baseline itself the change is 0. The grand total also has its baseline value and percentage change as rows (ener.ref, ener.pct \(= F_t / F^{B}_t - 1\)).

Electricity totals. Electricity demand (memo) is summed by sector group and in total: \(E_{g,t} = \sum_{s \in g} E_{s,t}\), \(E_t = \sum_g E_{g,t}\) (code eldem).
