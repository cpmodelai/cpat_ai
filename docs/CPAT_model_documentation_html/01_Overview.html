1  Overview, notation and policies

This chapter opens the book. It says what the shared model is, how a run is organised, and in which order the model computes. It fixes the notation, the units and the conventions that every later chapter uses. It then explains the first two blocks of the model: the header of macro series (block M0) and the policies (block M1 and its adjustments A1), which turn the scenario inputs into a price signal for every year, fuel and sector.

The model is shared by two implementations. Excel-AI (the Excel prototype, tab CPAT Excel-AI) computes each equation in workbook rows. CPAT Python (Coded CPAT, tab CPAT Python) computes the same equation in one function. Both use the same variable codes and the same order of calculation, and CPAT Python is checked against Excel-AI code by code and year by year.

1.1 What the model is

The model is the mitigation module of the IMF and World Bank Climate Policy Assessment Tool (CPAT), together with its distribution module. It answers one question: what do carbon pricing and related policies do to energy prices, energy use, emissions, government revenue, GDP and households, year by year to 2041?

It follows the method of legacy CPAT, described in the methodology (methodology, section 3.1.2): a reduced-form model in which fuel use responds to income, prices and efficiency, with a techno-economic model of the power sector. It departs from legacy CPAT only where legacy has a bug (always fixed) or where a decision was taken and recorded (the boxes “Differences from legacy CPAT” list them).

Policies in Carbon tax; new emissions trading system (ETS) with a cap; feebates; energy efficiency regulations (as shadow prices); fuel excises (fuel price reform); consumer subsidy, price-control and producer subsidy phase-outs; methane fee; existing carbon taxes and ETS; power-sector policies; road vehicle policies
Results out Retail energy prices; fuel use in 16 subsectors and 9 fuels; electricity generation, capacity and prices; revenue; CO2, other greenhouse gases and local air pollutants; GDP effects; the gap to the country’s NDC; household effects by decile
Country One country at a time in Excel-AI, any number of countries at once in CPAT Python. Equations contain no country numbers: every country value comes from the data, looked up by the country code

The methodology’s own summary of the policies CPAT can assess is in its user guide (methodology, section 1.1).

1.2 How a run is organised

A run takes one scenario set and a choice of countries, and returns every result by output code, country, scenario and year.

1.2.1 Countries, scenarios and scenario sets

A scenario is one full column of inputs: one value for each policy input (the rows of legacy MTInputs and Excel-AI’s additions). A scenario set is a table of such columns, numbered from 1.

  • Scenario 1 is the baseline (\(o = B\)): no new policy, only what the price data already contain (existing taxes, subsidies and carbon pricing).
  • Scenarios 2 to \(n\) are policy scenarios (\(o = P\)). Each runs with the same equations and its own inputs.

In Excel-AI each scenario is a group of year columns on the Mitigation sheet: group 1 is the baseline and group 2 the live policy scenario. In CPAT Python a scenario set is a CSV file (data/inputs/scenarios_<name>.csv, one column per scenario), and any number of scenarios run one after another.

A few inputs are read from the baseline column for every scenario: they are run settings, not policies. They include the results year, the last historical price year, the social cost of carbon settings, the effectiveness of output-based allocation, the price-control (pass-through) choice and the price floor.

1.2.2 Baseline and comparisons

Most results are reported against the baseline. Write \(X^{B}\) for the value of \(X\) in the baseline (scenario 1) in the same year and country. The change against the baseline is

\[ \Delta X_t = X_t - X^{B}_t \tag{1.1}\]

where \(X\) is any result (fuel use, CO2, revenue, GDP) and \(t\) the year. Percent changes are \(\Delta X_t / X^{B}_t\).

Policy scenarios also read some baseline values as inputs: the baseline CO2 by ETS sector (for the ETS cap), the calibration values of the GHG inventory year, and the same-year baseline revenue (for the fiscal space and so for GDP in the next year). This is why the baseline always runs first.

The model runs year by year from the base year \(t_0\) to 2041.

1.2.4 Money: the results year and real USD

Every money value is in real US dollars of the results year \(R\) (input ResultsYear, read from the baseline column; 2026 by default), per unit of the quantity (USD/GJ, USD/tCO2, USD million). A constant real price is a constant number: there is no inflation in the projection, and fuel use responds only to real price changes.

Two indices, both equal to 1 in \(R\), convert nominal values to real ones (block M0, Equation 1.5):

  • the CPI index \(\nu_t\) (US consumer prices) converts nominal domestic values: the price data, existing taxes, the nominal option of the carbon price, producer subsidies;
  • the deflator index \(\delta_t\) (US GDP deflator) converts nominal international prices and GDP.

Policy inputs are real (results-year USD) unless an input says otherwise: only the carbon price has a nominal option. The methodology describes the currency rules in its user guide (methodology, section 1.2.11).

WarningDifferences from legacy CPAT
  • Two indices, as legacy. Legacy uses the CPI for domestic values and the deflator for international prices; the model keeps that split. Up to 2030 the two differ by up to 1.2% (2022: 1.137 against 1.124; 2030: 0.918 against 0.929), so a single index would not reproduce legacy.
  • Horizon. Legacy holds the US CPI flat after the WEO horizon (2031) but not the deflator; so does the model. The model computes to 2041, the horizon of the legacy power block; values after 2030 were not a target of the comparisons with legacy.

1.3 The calculation chain

The model never solves a system of equations. Each year reads only that year and earlier years, so the whole run is one pass in a fixed order. This section gives that order; each later chapter explains one link of it.

1.3.1 Three stages, baseline first

Each scenario runs in three stages.

  1. Prepare (all years at once). Everything that depends on no model quantity: the macro header, the policy paths, the international and retail prices before and after policies, the power cost paths (including the forward-looking levelised costs). Because prices and policies are known for all years before any quantity is computed, a later year’s price can be used in an earlier year’s decision without a circular reference.
  2. Year loop (\(t = t_0, \dots, 2041\)). Every block of the year, in a fixed order. The base year sets the starting values.
  3. Finish (all years). Rows that need the whole path: the NDC targets and the gap.

The baseline runs first, then each policy scenario in turn, because policy scenarios read baseline values. After all scenarios, the distribution module computes household effects in its analysis year, every scenario against the baseline.

1.3.2 One year of the loop

Within a year the blocks run in this order (CPAT Python mitigation/chain.py; Excel-AI computes the same rows, each year reading only that year and earlier years).

because one PJ is a million GJ. Similarly, a cost in USD/kWh times a generation in MWy times \(M\) is in USD million.

1.6 Conventions

A few conventions hold in every chapter.

  • Indicator. \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\) is 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise. For example \(\mathbb{1}[o \ne B]\) is 1 in a policy scenario.
  • Switches. Yes/No inputs are switches of the scenario. A text switch counts as Yes when it starts with “Yes” (so “Yes” and “Yes*” both count; the test is on the first three letters, ignoring case). A tick box is TRUE or FALSE; in arithmetic TRUE is 1 and FALSE or a blank cell is 0.
  • Defaults. An asterisk marks the default option of a dropdown input (“Medium”, ”Real”, “Linear*“). Numbers in parentheses in the parameter tables of this book are defaults of sheet Constants or Settings, not country data.
  • Blank inputs read as 0, as an Excel lookup of an empty cell does.
  • Baseline and previous year. \(X^{B}_t\) is the baseline value in the same year; \(X_{t-1}\) the value of the previous year in the same scenario.
  • Codes. Every variable has a short code (ener, atp, cptraj, …), the same in Excel-AI (row codes) and CPAT Python (variable names). A full output code adds the country, module, sector, fuel and scenario, for example egy.mit.co2.tot.tot.2.
  • Equation ids. Each equation of the model has an id (M1.2, A2.5, …) in the equation lists of the appendices; CPAT Python cites it in the code (EQ-M1.2). This book links them, for example (M1.1).

1.7 Header: the macro series (M0)

The header turns the IMF World Economic Outlook (WEO) data of the country into the series every later block reads: the price indices that make money values real, real GDP in US dollars and local currency, the exchange rate and the baseline growth of GDP. It depends on no model quantity, so it is computed for all years in the prepare stage. The methodology lists the macro data and their sources in methodology, section 3.9.1.

1.7.1 Macro series past the last data year

Each WEO series is data up to its own last data year \(T^{WEO}_x\) and is then extended by a rule of its own (M0.1):

\[ x_t = \begin{cases} x^{WEO}_t & t \le T^{WEO}_x \\ \text{extension rule of } x & t > T^{WEO}_x \end{cases} \tag{1.3}\]

over the macro years 2018 to 2045. The rules are:

Series WEO code Extension past the last data year
US GDP deflator USA.NGDP_D Linear trend: \(x_t = 2x_{t-1} - x_{t-2}\)
US CPI USA.PCPI Held flat: \(x_t = x_{t-1}\)
Real GDP growth NGDP_RPCH Held, or eased off when falling (Equation 1.4)
Real GDP, local currency NGDP_R Grown with real growth: \(x_t = x_{t-1}(1 + g_t)\)
Nominal GDP, local currency NGDP Grown with its last growth rate, held from the last data year
Nominal GDP, USD NGDPD Grown with the nominal growth rate of local-currency GDP
Exchange rate XDC_USD.PA_RT Held flat; a euro country takes the euro area’s rate (WEO G163)
Population LP Grown with its last growth factor: \(x_t = x_{t-1}\,x_{t-1}/x_{t-2}\)

Real GDP growth is held at its last value, unless it was falling. Then it eases off: it keeps at least half of the average of the last three years, and otherwise follows the linear trend of the last two:

\[ g_t = \begin{cases} g_{t-1} & g_{t-1} \ge g_{t-2} \\[4pt] \max\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bar{g}_{t-3..t-1},\; 2g_{t-1} - g_{t-2}\right) & g_{t-1} < g_{t-2} \end{cases} \tag{1.4}\]

where

  • \(g_t\) is real GDP growth in year \(t\) (share per year);
  • \(\bar{g}_{t-3..t-1}\) is the average of the growth rates of the three previous years;
  • \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) is the constant GdpExtHalf (0.5).

Nominal GDP in local currency grows with the growth rate of its last data year; in Excel-AI that rate is the one of the year before the last data year, and CPAT Python does the same.

1.7.2 Price indices, real GDP and the exchange rate

The header rows of the model read these series (M0.2):

\[ \nu_t = \frac{CPI^{US}_{R}}{CPI^{US}_t}, \qquad \delta_t = \frac{D^{US}_{R}}{D^{US}_t} \tag{1.5}\]

where

  • \(\nu_t\) (infl) is the CPI index: it converts nominal domestic prices and nominal policy inputs of year \(t\) to real USD of \(R\);
  • \(\delta_t\) (defl) is the deflator index: it converts nominal international prices;
  • \(CPI^{US}_t\), \(D^{US}_t\) are the US CPI and GDP deflator (WEO, extended as above);
  • \(R\) is the results year.

Both indices equal 1 in \(R\), are above 1 before it and below 1 after it.

Real GDP in US dollars (gdp.usd, USD billion of \(R\)) is converted from nominal GDP in the historical years and grown with real growth from the base year on:

\[ Y^{USD}_t = \begin{cases} \dfrac{Y^{n}_t}{e^{n}_t}\,\delta_t & t < t_0 \\[8pt] Y^{USD}_{t-1}\,(1 + g_t) & t \ge t_0 \end{cases} \tag{1.6}\]

where \(Y^{n}_t\) is nominal GDP in local currency, \(e^{n}_t\) the market exchange rate (local currency per USD) and \(g_t\) real GDP growth (gdp.base, the baseline growth that the GDP block starts from; Chapter 7).

Real GDP in local currency (gdp.lcu) is the WEO real GDP series, \(Y^{LCU}_t = Y^{R}_t\). The exchange rate the model uses is the ratio of the two real GDP series:

\[ e_t = \frac{Y^{LCU}_t}{Y^{USD}_t} \tag{1.7}\]

in local currency per real US dollar (xr). From the base year on both series grow at the same rate, so \(e_t\) is constant in real terms. GDP per capita is \(Y^{USD}_t\) divided by population (times 1000, population being in thousands).

1.8 Policies (M1 and A1)

The policies block turns the inputs of a scenario into price signals: a rate per tonne of CO2 or per unit of fuel, for each year, sector and fuel. Every later block reads these signals. Prices add them to the retail price (Chapter 2); fuel use and power respond to the price and to the shadow price (Chapter 3, Chapter 4); revenues collect them (Chapter 7). The block depends on no model quantity, so it is computed for all years in the prepare stage.

The methodology describes the policy options in its user guide (methodology, section 1.3.2.3) and the price notation in methodology, section 3.2.2.

1.8.2 The policy path

Every policy is a path from a start year to a target year, set per scenario: zero before the start year, linear from a start level to a target level, and then either held or continued (M1.1).

\[ \Lambda(t;\, T_0, T_1, x_0, x_1) = \begin{cases} 0 & t < T_0 \\[4pt] x_0 + (x_1 - x_0)\,\dfrac{t - T_0}{\max(T_1 - T_0,\,1)} & t \ge T_0 \end{cases} \tag{1.8}\]

where

  • \(T_0\) is the start year and \(T_1\) the target year of the policy (inputs);
  • \(x_0\) is the level in the start year and \(x_1\) the level in the target year (inputs);
  • \(\max(T_1 - T_0, 1)\) guards against a target year equal to the start year.

\(\Lambda\) continues the line after \(T_1\). The path held flat after the target year reads the line at \(\min(t, T_1)\):

\[ \bar{\Lambda}(t;\, T_0, T_1, x_0, x_1) = \Lambda\big(\min(t, T_1);\, T_0, T_1, x_0, x_1\big) \quad \text{for } t \ge T_0 \tag{1.9}\]

and 0 before \(T_0\). Which form a policy takes after its target year:

def path(t, t0, t1, x0, x1, hold=True):
    if t < t0:
        return 0
    tt = min(t, t1) if hold else t          # hold: flat after the target year

1.8.3 Carbon tax

The carbon tax is a charge per tonne of CO2 on the fuels and sectors it covers. It is added to the fuel price as the rate times the fuel’s emission factor (Chapter 2). The methodology’s notation is \(NCT\) for the new carbon tax and \(\varphi_{NCT}\) for its coverage (methodology, section 3.9.3.1.1).

The carbon price path. The national carbon price follows the policy path, made real if the inputs are nominal (M1.2):

\[ \tau_t = \Lambda^{*}(t;\, T^{cp}_0, T^{cp}_1, x^{cp}_0, x^{cp}_1)\; n_t, \qquad n_t = \begin{cases} \nu_t & \text{inputs nominal} \\ 1 & \text{inputs real} \end{cases} \tag{1.10}\]

where

  • \(\tau_t\) (cptraj, methodology \(NCT_t\)) is the carbon price in real USD/tCO2;
  • \(\Lambda^{*}\) is \(\Lambda\) if ExtendCarbonPriceBeyondOutro = Linear*, else \(\bar{\Lambda}\);
  • \(T^{cp}_0\), \(T^{cp}_1\) are CPIntro, CPOutro; \(x^{cp}_0\), \(x^{cp}_1\) are CPLevelStart, CPLevelTarget;
  • \(n_t\) (cp.nom) is the CPI index \(\nu_t\) if NomorReal starts with “Nominal”, else 1 (default Real*).

Explicit or shadow pricing. The input ScenarioSelected picks a main scenario from the legacy table of main scenarios. It says whether the carbon price path is an explicit tax or a shadow price (Section 1.8.5). The explicit carbon price is

\[ \tau^{x}_t = \tau_t\; \mathbb{1}[\text{main scenario prices explicitly}] \tag{1.11}\]

(cpx, cp.exp). A text that is not in the table counts as explicit pricing, as the shipped ” - Carbon tax”.

Coverage by fuel and by sector. The tax covers a fuel and a sector through two sets of switches, one per fuel (MCovCoa, …, MCovOop; biomass CTaxCoversBiomass; jet fuel reads the kerosene switch) and one per coverage sector (MCovPow, MCovRod, …, MCovOen). Unticked fuels and sectors are exempt. An exemption can be phased out: in a policy scenario with ExemptPhaseout = TRUE, an uncovered fuel or sector becomes taxed linearly over ExemptPhaseoutPeriod years from YearPha:

\[ \varphi_{t} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{switch ticked} \\[4pt] \mathbb{1}[o \ne B]\;\mathbb{1}[\text{ExemptPhaseout}]\;\mathbb{1}[t \ge T^{cp}_0]\;\min\!\left(\max\!\left(\dfrac{t + 1 - T^{pha}}{N^{pha}},\, 0\right),\, 1\right) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{1.12}\]

where

  • \(\varphi_t\) (ctcov) is the coverage of one fuel (\(\varphi_{NCT,f}\)) or one sector (\(\varphi_{NCT,s}\)), a share;
  • \(T^{pha}\) is YearPha, \(N^{pha}\) is ExemptPhaseoutPeriod;
  • the phase-out share itself is exph.

A fuel used in a sector is taxed by the product of the two coverages: the sector coverage enters the rate by sector below, the fuel coverage the rate per GJ (M2.4, Chapter 2).

The rate by sector. Where the new ETS covers a sector, the ETS replaces the carbon tax. The carbon tax rate of coverage sector \(s\) is (M1.3):

\[ T_{s,t} = \tau^{x}_t\; c_{s,t}\; (1 - \kappa_{s,t}), \qquad c_{s,t} = \begin{cases} 1 - \text{xcov}_{j(s),\min(t, T_x)} & \text{tax complementary to an existing ETS} \\ \varphi_{NCT,s,t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{1.13}\]

where

  • \(T_{s,t}\) (ctr, methodology \(NCT_{s,t}\)) is the carbon tax rate of sector \(s\) in USD/tCO2;
  • \(\kappa_{s,t}\) is the new ETS coverage of the sector (Equation 1.15);
  • \(c_{s,t}\) is the sector coverage of the tax. If CTaxComplimentaryToETS = Yes, the new tax covers what the country’s existing national ETS does not: one minus the existing ETS coverage \(\text{xcov}\) of the sector’s ECP sector \(j(s)\) in the data year of \(t\). Otherwise new instruments add to existing ones.

Residential LPG and kerosene. With AlwaysExemptResLPGKer = Yes (default No*), the new carbon tax, the new ETS cost and the new excise of residential LPG and kerosene are zero. The model applies this through an exemption factor \(x_{s,f}\) (exf) that multiplies each new policy per unit of fuel:

\[ x_{s,f} = \begin{cases} 0 & f \in \{\text{LPG, kerosene}\},\ s = \text{residential},\ \text{switch Yes} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{1.14}\]

Existing taxes, existing carbon pricing and subsidy phase-outs still apply to these fuels, and the shadow price is not exempted (Chapter 2).

1.8.4 New emissions trading system

A new ETS sets a cap on the emissions of the sectors it covers. The model does not clear the permit market inside a run: it estimates the permit price that would meet the cap, applies it like a carbon tax, and reports how far emissions end up from the cap. A goal seek outside the run then refines the price (Chapter 6). The methodology’s user guide describes the option in methodology, section 1.3.2.4.2.

On and coverage. The ETS applies from its start year if NewETS = Yes; it covers the sectors whose switch (ETSCovPow, …, ETSCovOen) is ticked:

\[ \text{on}_t = \mathbb{1}[\text{NewETS}]\;\mathbb{1}[t \ge T^{E}_0], \qquad \kappa_{s,t} = \text{on}_t\;\mathbb{1}[\text{ETS covers } s] \tag{1.15}\]

where \(T^{E}_0\) is ETSIntro and \(\kappa_{s,t}\) (etsc) the effective coverage of sector \(s\).

Free allocation. Part of the permits can be allocated free, in proportion to output (output-based allocation, OBR). The benchmark of a group is the share of its emission intensity allocated free; it runs from one minus the auctioned share at the start year to one minus the auctioned share at the target year, and is held after:

\[ B_{G,t} = \bar{\Lambda}\big(t;\, T^{E}_0, T^{E}_1,\, 1 - a_0,\, 1 - a_1\big), \qquad \omega_{s,t} = \min\big(1, \max(0, B_{G(s),t})\big) \tag{1.16}\]

where

  • \(B_{G,t}\) (etsb) is the benchmark of ETS group \(G\) (power, transport, residential, industry);
  • \(a_0\), \(a_1\) are ETSAuctStart, ETSAuctTarget, the same for every group (default: nothing auctioned, so \(B = 1\));
  • \(T^{E}_1\) is ETSOutro;
  • \(\omega_{s,t}\) (obr) is the OBR share of sector \(s\), the benchmark of its group \(G(s)\) bounded to \([0, 1]\).

The cap. The cap is a change against the baseline emissions of the covered sectors:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathit{bce}_t &= \sum_s \mathit{CO2}^{B}_{s,t}\;\kappa_{s,t} \\ \mathit{chg}_t &= \bar{\Lambda}\big(t;\, T^{E}_0, T^{E}_1,\, \mathit{chg}_0,\, \mathit{chg}_1\big) \\ \mathit{cap}_t &= \begin{cases} \mathit{cap}_{t-1} & t > T^{E}_1 \text{ and the cap is held Constant} \\ \text{on}_t\; \mathit{bce}_t\,(1 + \mathit{chg}_t) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \tag{1.17}\]

where

  • \(\mathit{bce}_t\) (ets.bce, MtCO2) is the baseline covered emissions;
  • \(\mathit{CO2}^{B}_{s,t}\) (bco2) is the baseline CO2 of ETS sector \(s\), stored from scenario 1 as data;
  • \(\mathit{chg}_0\), \(\mathit{chg}_1\) are ETSChangeRelStart, ETSChangeRelTarget (for example \(-0.05\) and \(-0.20\));
  • ETSCapCont = Constant holds the cap at its last level after the target year (and only while the ETS is on); otherwise the relative change is held.

Volatility. A volatile permit price raises the cost of abatement: firms act as if the price were lower. The volatility adjustment converts the permit price into a tax-equivalent price:

\[ V = \frac{1 + r\, i}{1 + v\, i} \tag{1.18}\]

where

  • \(V\) (ets.vadj) is the tax-equivalent price per USD of permit price;
  • \(v\) (ets.vol) is the assumed annual price volatility of the ETS, and \(i\) (ets.imp) the abatement-cost increase per unit of volatility, both read by label from the table below;
  • \(r\) is the carbon tax policy risk (ETSCTRisk, 0.2).
Label (ETSVolatility, ETSVolImpact) Medium* High Low Zero
Volatility \(v\) 0.42 0.63 0.28 0
Impact \(i\) 0.5 0.75 0.333 0

At Medium, \(V = (1 + 0.2 \times 0.5)/(1 + 0.42 \times 0.5) = 1/1.1\).

The fast price estimate. With one semi-elasticity of covered emissions to the price, the price that brings covered emissions from \(bce\) to the cap solves \(cap = bce\; e^{\bar{\sigma} V p}\) (A1.1):

\[ \bar{\sigma}_t = \frac{\sum_s \mathit{CO2}^{B}_{s,t}\,\kappa_{s,t}\,\sigma_s\,\big(1 - (1 - \vartheta)\,\omega_{s,t}\big)}{\mathit{bce}_t} \tag{1.19}\]

\[ \hat{p}_t = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\ln(\mathit{cap}_t / \mathit{bce}_t)}{\bar{\sigma}_t\, V} & \text{on}_t = 1,\ 0 < \mathit{cap}_t < \mathit{bce}_t,\ \bar{\sigma}_t < 0 \\[8pt] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{1.20}\]

where

  • \(\bar{\sigma}_t\) (ets.se, per USD/tCO2) is the effective semi-elasticity of covered emissions, 0 when nothing is covered;
  • \(\sigma_s\) is the semi-elasticity of the sector’s semi-elasticity group (power, transport, residential, industry; legacy table ETS+LTS, by country);
  • \(\vartheta\) is the effectiveness of output-based allocation (setting ETSOBREffectiveness, 0.5): a permit allocated free has half the effect of an auctioned one;
  • \(\hat{p}_t\) (ets.est) is the fast estimate of the permit price in USD/tCO2.

Permit price, tax-equivalent price, revenue factor.

\[ p^{E}_t = \text{on}_t \begin{cases} \text{override}_t \ (\text{blank: } \tau_t) & \text{ETSPriceOverride = Yes} \\ \hat{p}_t & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad \tilde{p}^{E}_t = p^{E}_t\, V, \qquad \rho = \frac{1}{V} \tag{1.21}\]

where \(p^{E}_t\) (ets.p, methodology \(NETSP\)) is the permit price, \(\tilde{p}^{E}_t\) (ets.pe) the tax-equivalent price that enters behaviour, and \(\rho\) (ets.rf) the revenue factor: permit revenue per USD of tax-equivalent cost. The override row holds prices pasted from the goal seek (Chapter 6).

The ETS cost by sector. The tax-equivalent price splits by the OBR share. The auctioned part enters the fuel price like a tax; the free part acts only on efficiency, through the shadow price (Equation 1.25):

\[ A_{s,t} = \tilde{p}^{E}_t\;\kappa_{s,t}\;(1 - \omega_{s,t}) \tag{1.22}\]

where \(A_{s,t}\) (etr) is the ETS cost in the fuel price of sector \(s\), USD/tCO2 (M1.3). Revenue counts the auctioned part times \(\rho\), the permit price; the free part raises none (Chapter 7).

TipPseudocode: new ETS 
for t in years:
    on[t] = NewETS starts with 'Yes' and t >= ETSIntro
    for s in coverage_sectors:                                   # power + 16 subsectors
        etsc[s][t] = on[t] * ETSCov[s]
    for G in ['pow', 'tra', 'res', 'ind']:
        etsb[G][t] = path(t, ETSIntro, ETSOutro, 1 - ETSAuctStart, 1 - ETSAuctTarget, hold=True)
    for s in coverage_sectors:
        obr[s][t] = clip(etsb[ets_group(s)][t], 0, 1)
    vadj = (1 + ETSCTRisk * imp[ETSVolImpact]) / (1 + vol[ETSVolatility] * imp[ETSVolImpact])
    bce[t] = sum(bco2[s][t] * etsc[s][t] for s in coverage_sectors)     # stored baseline CO2
    chg[t] = path(t, ETSIntro, ETSOutro, ETSChangeRelStart, ETSChangeRelTarget, hold=True)
    cap[t] = on[t] * bce[t] * (1 + chg[t])
    if ETSCapCont starts with 'Constant' and t > ETSOutro:
        cap[t] = cap[t-1] if on[t] else 0
    se[t] = sum(bco2[s][t] * etsc[s][t] * semi[semi_group(s)] * (1 - (1 - obr_eff) * obr[s][t])
                for s in coverage_sectors) / bce[t]   if bce[t] > 0 else 0
    est[t] = log(cap[t] / bce[t]) / (se[t] * vadj)   if on[t] and 0 < cap[t] < bce[t] and se[t] < 0 else 0
    p[t] = (override[t] or cptraj[t]) if ETSPriceOverride starts with 'Yes' else est[t]
    p[t] = p[t] if on[t] else 0
    pe[t] = p[t] * vadj                                         # tax-equivalent price
    rf = 1 / vadj                                               # revenue factor
    for s in coverage_sectors:
        etr[s][t] = pe[t] * etsc[s][t] * (1 - obr[s][t])        # auctioned part, in the fuel price

1.8.5 Feebates and regulations: the shadow price

Feebates and regulations make energy use more efficient without raising the price of energy. A feebate charges above-average emitters and rebates below-average ones, so it is revenue neutral; a regulation sets a standard. The model represents both as a shadow price per tonne of CO2 that acts only on the efficiency margin of fuel use (Chapter 3) and on power (Chapter 4), and raises no revenue. The user guide describes feebates in methodology, section 1.3.2.4.3 and regulations in section 1.3.2.6.

Feebate rates. One rate per feebate group \(G\) (power, transport, residential, industry), linear from FeebateIntro to FeebateOutro and held after:

\[ b_{G,t} = \bar{\Lambda}\big(t;\, T^{b}_0, T^{b}_1,\, b_{G,0},\, b_{G,1}\big) \tag{1.23}\]

where \(b_{G,t}\) (fb) is in USD/tCO2 and \(b_{G,0}\), \(b_{G,1}\) are Feb_Level_Start_<group>, Feb_Level_Target_<group>. Food and forestry and services take the residential rate; other energy use the industry rate.

Regulations. The main scenario (ScenarioSelected) decides whether the carbon price path is an explicit tax or a shadow price, which efficiency adjustment applies and which sectors the shadow price covers:

Under shadow pricing there is no explicit carbon tax (\(\tau^x = 0\)), and the carbon price path becomes the shadow carbon price:

\[ \mathit{reg}_{s,t} = (\tau_t - \tau^{x}_t)\; a^{reg}\; \mathbb{1}[\text{main scenario covers } s] \tag{1.24}\]

where \(\mathit{reg}_{s,t}\) (reg) is the regulation shadow price of sector \(s\), \(\tau_t - \tau^x_t\) the shadow carbon price (cp.shd) and \(a^{reg}\) (regadj) the efficiency adjustment of the main scenario.

The shadow price by sector. Feebates, regulations and the free part of the ETS add up (M1.4):

\[ \Sigma_{s,t} = b_{G(s),t}\; a^{fb}\; \mathbb{1}[\text{feebate covers } s] \;+\; \mathit{reg}_{s,t} \;+\; \tilde{p}^{E}_t\;\kappa_{s,t}\;\omega_{s,t} \tag{1.25}\]

where

  • \(\Sigma_{s,t}\) (shpx) is the shadow price of sector \(s\) in USD/tCO2;
  • \(a^{fb}\) is SPPEffAdjFeebates, the share of the feebate on the efficiency margin; the product \(a^{fb}\,\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\) is ssc;
  • the feebate coverage switches are FbCovPow, …, FbCovOen.

Other regulation inputs act directly on efficiency rather than through a price: the additional policy-induced efficiency gains AdditionalEfficiencyRoadVehicle, AdditionalEfficiencyResidential, AdditionalEfficiencyIndustrial (Chapter 3) and AdditionalEfficiencyPower (Chapter 4). The road engineer model’s vehicle feebate uses the road regulation shadow price (Chapter 5).

1.8.6 Fuel excises: the fuel price reform

A new excise is a tax per unit of fuel, set in the fuel’s own price unit (USD/liter for gasoline, diesel, LPG and kerosene, USD/bbl for other oil products, USD/GJ for coal, gas and biomass, USD/kWh for electricity). The model calls it the fuel price reform: one increase path per price block, all with the same start and target year (FprIntro, FprOutro). The methodology describes new excises in methodology, section 3.9.3.1.4.

Up to the target year the path is linear; after it, the input ExtendCarbonPriceBeyondOutro (the same as for the carbon price) chooses how it continues (M1.5):

\[ F_{g,f,t} = \begin{cases} 0 & t < T^{F}_0 \\[2pt] x_0 + j\,(t - T^{F}_0) & t < T^{F}_1 \text{ or Linear*} \\[2pt] \hat{x}\,(1 + k)^{\,t - T^{F}_1 + 1} & \text{Percentage} \\[2pt] x_1 & \text{otherwise (held)} \end{cases} \tag{1.26}\]

with

\[ j = \frac{x_1 - x_0}{T^{F}_1 - T^{F}_0}, \qquad k = \left(\frac{x_1}{x_0}\right)^{1/(T^{F}_1 - T^{F}_0)} - 1, \qquad \hat{x} = x_0 + j\,\max(T^{F}_1 - T^{F}_0 - 1,\, 0) \tag{1.27}\]

where

  • \(F_{g,f,t}\) (fpr) is the excise increase of price block \((g,f)\) in its price unit;
  • \(x_0\), \(x_1\) are the start and final increases (FprStart<Fuel><Sector>, FprFinal<Fuel><Sector>);
  • \(j\) (fprj) is the annual step, 0 if the target year is not after the start year;
  • \(k\) (fprk) is the average annual growth rate of the path; where the start is zero it divides by \(x_0 + j\) instead of \(x_0\); it is 0 if both levels are zero or the years do not increase;
  • \(\hat{x}\) (fprp) is the value of the year before the target year; the value after the target year is fpra;
  • if the target year is not after the start year, the held value is \(x_0\).

The new excise of a price block. The gross new excise (ntxg) is the reform path, plus the Pigouvian tax when AddEfficientTaxes = Yes (Section 1.8.12). It applies in policy scenarios only:

\[ N^{x}_{g,f,t} = \mathbb{1}[o \ne B]\; F_{g,f,t} \tag{1.28}\]

(ntxb). Prices divide it by the GJ per price unit and multiply it by the exemption factor \(x_{s,f}\) to get the new excise per GJ of each subsector (Chapter 2). The electricity blocks reach the electricity price through the power block, together with the manual electricity excise of AdditionalExcise (A4.7, Chapter 4).

1.8.7 Subsidy and price-control phase-outs

Many countries subsidise fuels, either explicitly (a consumer or producer subsidy) or by controlling the retail price so that it does not follow the supply cost. The model can phase out three kinds of support, each with its own switch, start year, period and share: producer subsidies, consumer subsidies and price controls. The methodology describes these reforms in methodology, section 3.9.3.1.6 and section 3.9.3.1.7.

The phase-out factor. For each phase-out \(k\), the share of the original support still in place (M1.7):

\[ \phi_{k,t} = \max\!\left(1 - \bar{s}_k,\;\; \begin{cases} 1 - \bar{s}_k\,\dfrac{t - T_k}{\max(N_k, 1)} & \text{on}_k \text{ and } t > T_k \\[6pt] 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\right) \tag{1.29}\]

where

  • \(\phi_{k,t}\) (pof) is the share of the original subsidy (or floating tax) kept, from 1 down to \(1 - \bar{s}_k\);
  • \(T_k\) is the start year (YearProdSubPha, YearConsSubPha, YearPrcContPha);
  • \(N_k\) (po.n) is the phase-out period in years, \(\bar{s}_k\) (po.sh) the share phased out, and \(\text{on}_k\) (po.on) whether the phase-out applies.

The baseline can phase out subsidies too (a reform already decided). The baseline and policy scenarios read different inputs:

Producer subsidies Consumer subsidies Price controls
On, baseline ProdSubPhaBaseline ConsSubPhaBaseline PrcContPha and PrcContPhaBaseline
On, policy scenario ProdSubPh ConsSubPha PrcContPha
Period, baseline FFSProdPhaseoutPeriodBA FFSConsPhaseoutPeriodBA PrcControlPhaseout
Period, policy scenario FFSProdPhaseoutPeriod FFPhaseOut PrcControlPhaseout
Share, baseline ShareOfProdSubPhaBA ShareofConsSubPhaBA as consumer subsidies
Share, policy scenario larger of ShareOfProdSubPhaBA, ShareofProdSubPha larger of ShareofConsSubPhaBA, ShareOfConsSubPha as consumer subsidies

A switch counts as on when it is TRUE or starts with “Yes”. Price controls are phased out only where the policy switch is on (and, in the baseline, the baseline switch too), and they use the consumer-subsidy shares (issue I48).

Scope by fuel and sector. Scope switches let a reform cover some price blocks or fuels only:

\[ \psi^{cs}_{g,f,t} = 1 - i^{cs}_{g,f}\,(1 - \phi_{cs,t}), \qquad \psi^{pc}_{g,f,t} = 1 - i^{pc}_{g,f}\,(1 - \phi_{pc,t}), \qquad \psi^{ps}_{f,t} = 1 - i^{ps}_{f}\,(1 - \phi_{ps,t}) \tag{1.30}\]

where

  • \(\psi^{cs}\) (csf), \(\psi^{pc}\) (pcf) are the consumer-subsidy and price-control factors of each of the 12 fuel price blocks, and \(\psi^{ps}\) (psf) the producer-subsidy factor of each producer fuel (coal, gas, oil, biomass, electricity);
  • \(i^{cs}\), \(i^{pc}\), \(i^{ps}\) (csi, pci, psi) are the scope switches (ConsSubPhaseScope_<fuel>_<sector>, PriceCtrlPhaseScope_<fuel>_<sector>, ProdSubPhaseScope_<fuel>), 1 where the reform applies.

The factors become wedges in the after-tax price: the consumer-subsidy reform removes part of the fixed subsidy, the price-control phase-out part of the floating tax or subsidy that absorbs supply-cost changes (Chapter 2). The saved subsidy is revenue (Chapter 7). Subsidy reforms of electricity prices are part of the power block (Chapter 4).

1.8.8 Producer subsidy reform

A producer subsidy lowers the cost of supplying a fuel. The model reads the subsidy of each fuel from the price data, spreads it over the demand it supports to get a subsidy per unit, and lowers it with the producer phase-out factor after the last historical price year (A2.5):

\[ \mathit{ps}_{f,t} = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{\mathit{PROS}_{f,t}}{D_{f,t}} & t \le L \\[8pt] \mathit{ps}_{f,L}\;\psi^{ps}_{f,t} & t > L \end{cases} \tag{1.31}\]

where

  • \(\mathit{ps}_{f,t}\) (psu) is the producer subsidy per unit of fuel \(f\) (negative: USD/liter for oil, USD/GJ for coal, gas and biomass);
  • \(\mathit{PROS}_{f,t}\) (pros) is the producer subsidy (price data, USD billion nominal, times the CPI index and 1000: USD million real; held after \(L\)), times \(10^6\) to get USD;
  • \(D_{f,t}\) (psd) is the demand that carries the subsidy: world demand times liters per ktoe for oil; the average of world demand and domestic consumption (excluding electricity and heat) times GJ per ktoe for coal and gas; domestic consumption times GJ per ktoe for biomass;
  • \(\psi^{ps}_{f,t}\) is the producer-subsidy factor psf of Equation 1.30.

The per-unit subsidy enters the supply cost of each price block of the fuel, so the floating taxes and price controls absorb part of a phase-out, as in legacy (Chapter 2). The subsidy itself is a fiscal line (rpsu, Chapter 7). The electricity producer subsidy is allocated between user groups in the power block (Chapter 4).

1.8.9 Methane fee

The methane fee is a charge per tonne of CO2-equivalent of methane from the sources it covers (fuel supply, gas distribution, agriculture, waste). It drives methane abatement and raises revenue (Chapter 6, Chapter 7). The methodology describes methane in methodology, section 3.5.4.2.

\[ m_t = \Lambda\big(t;\, T^{m}_0, T^{m}_1, m_0, m_1\big)\;\mathbb{1}[o \ne B] \;+\; \tau_t\;\mathbb{1}[\text{carbon tax applies to methane}] \tag{1.32}\]

where

  • \(m_t\) (mfee) is the methane fee in USD/tCO2e; the path alone is mfee.p;
  • \(T^{m}_0\), \(T^{m}_1\), \(m_0\), \(m_1\) are MPIntro, MPOutro, MPLevelStart, MPLevelTarget; the path continues linearly after the target year;
  • the fee path applies only in policy scenarios; the carbon price \(\tau_t\) is added in any scenario where CTforMethane = Yes (M1.8).

Which methane sources the fee covers is set by switches per source (Excel-AI sheet Manual inputs; Chapter 6).

1.8.10 Existing carbon pricing

Some countries already price carbon. The IMF retail prices contain whatever existing carbon pricing the country had in the price data, so the model adds only the change against that level: a scheme switched off lowers the price by its embedded level, growth or new data raise it. The data are the World Bank Carbon Pricing Dashboard and ICAP (legacy sheet ECP, as of 1 April 2025; 52 countries). The methodology describes the options in methodology, section 3.9.3.1.3.

Data by ECP sector and fuel. For each ECP sector \(j\) (power, transport, residential, industry), fuel \(f\) and data year \(y\) (2021 to \(T_x = 2025\)), in real USD/tCO2:

\[ \mathit{XT}_{j,f,y} = r_{f,y}\; k^{T}_{j,y}\; l^{T}_{f,y}, \qquad \mathit{XE}_{j,f,y} = p^{nat}_y\; k^{N}_{j,y}\; l^{N}_{f,y} \;+\; p^{EU}_y\; k^{EU}_{j,y}\; l^{EU}_{f,y} \tag{1.33}\]

where

  • \(\mathit{XT}\) is the existing carbon tax and \(\mathit{XE}\) the existing ETS price of the sector and fuel;
  • \(r_{f,y}\) is the carbon tax rate of the fuel where the country has fuel rates and no “single price” flag, otherwise the single rate; \(k^{T}\), \(l^{T}\) its sector and fuel coverage;
  • \(p^{nat}\) is the national ETS price with sector and fuel coverage \(k^{N}\), \(l^{N}\); \(p^{EU}\) the EU ETS price with the country’s regional coverage \(k^{EU}\), \(l^{EU}\), counted only if the country is in the EU ETS (flag Yes or Both);
  • prices are made real with the CPI index of the data year; blank coverage is 0.

Scenario factors. Each scheme can be switched off or grown after the last data year:

\[ \phi^{ct}_t = \mathbb{1}[\text{ExistingCTApply}]\,(1 + \gamma^{ct})^{\max(0,\, t - T_x)}, \qquad \phi^{ets}_t = \mathbb{1}[\text{ExistingETSApply}]\,(1 + \gamma^{ets})^{\max(0,\, t - T_x)} \tag{1.34}\]

where \(\phi^{ct}\) (xct.p), \(\phi^{ets}\) (xets.p) multiply the data of year \(t^* = \min(t, T_x)\) (xct.c), and \(\gamma^{ct}\), \(\gamma^{ets}\) are ExistingCTGrowth, ExistingETSGrowth. The level per GJ is then the emission factor times \(\mathit{XT}\,\phi^{ct} + \mathit{XE}\,\phi^{ets}\); the price block takes the change against the level embedded in the price data, of year \(\min(L, T_x)\) (A2.2, Chapter 2). The change also enters the variable cost of power plants (Chapter 4).

1.8.12 Pigouvian (efficient) taxes

With AddEfficientTaxes = Yes, the new excise of each price block adds a Pigouvian tax: the external cost of a unit of fuel that its price does not already cover (A1.2). It has three parts:

  • air pollution: the value of averted PM2.5 (from the air pollution module) times the module’s concentration coefficients of the source times the combustion factors of the fuel, per GJ, weighted by base-year fuel use (residential coal only where coal is above ApCoalResMin, 0.1% of buildings fuel use);
  • climate (if ClimateEffTax): the national SCC times the generic CO2 factor of the fuel;
  • road transport: accident, congestion and road-damage costs per km (from the transport co-benefits module), converted per unit of fuel with base-year fuel economy, the elasticity share of usage and the road share of the fuel.

The sum is phased in over AddEfficientTaxesPhaseInYears from CPIntro. Where an existing pre-tax wedge already covers part of it, the new excise is \(\min\big(n,\ \max(0,\ n - w\,\gamma)\big)\), with \(n\) the gross excise, \(w\) the existing wedge per GJ and \(\gamma\) the GJ per price unit (issue I113). The module values enter in the prepare stage, so the Pigouvian tax is exogenous to the year loop (issue I151).
