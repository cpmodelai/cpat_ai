7  Revenues, GDP and welfare

This chapter follows the money. Once the model knows how much of each fuel is used and how much electricity is generated in a year, it can say what the government collects and what it pays out, how spending that revenue moves GDP, how the energy import bill changes, how heavily carbon is priced in effect, and what the policy is worth to society once climate damages, air pollution, road externalities and efficiency costs are counted.

The blocks of the chapter, in the order the model computes them within a year:

The GDP effect of a year acts on GDP only in the following year. That one-year lag is the only link from year \(t\) to year \(t+1\) in this chapter, and it is what keeps the model free of circular references (Section 7.3.7).

7.1 Header and summary rows

A handful of macro rows sit at the top of every scenario: they give the GDP against which revenues, trade and welfare are scaled. Every section of the model also ends with a summary row, so the results can be read at a glance.

7.1.1 GDP rows of the header

Baseline real GDP comes from the IMF World Economic Outlook, extended past its last data year as described in (M0.1). The exchange rate is the one implied by real GDP in the two currencies, so it is constant in real terms:

\[ e = \frac{Y^{LCU}}{Y^{0}} \tag{7.1}\]

where

  • \(Y^{LCU}\) is baseline real GDP in local currency (gdp.lcu), bn;
  • \(Y^{0}\) is baseline real GDP in USD (gdp.usd), bn;
  • \(e\) is the exchange rate (xr), local currency per USD (Egypt: 14.34 EGP per USD).

7.2 Revenues

Revenue is computed fuel by fuel and subsector by subsector: fuel use times a tax, subsidy or policy rate per GJ. The model keeps three separate lines (existing taxes, existing subsidies, new policies), adds the power sector, subsidy reforms, producer subsidies and the methane fee, and then rearranges the same money by fuel and by policy instrument. The methodology describes this in section 3.6, Fiscal revenues.

All revenue rows are in USD million, real. A negative number is a fiscal cost (a subsidy).

7.2.1 Revenue of each fuel and subsector

For each subsector \(s\) and fuel \(f\), the price block of the fuel (Chapter 2) gives the components of the retail price per GJ. Revenue multiplies them by fuel use.

Existing taxes include excise and other taxes, the VAT payment, and the change in existing carbon pricing against the year embedded in the price data, with VAT on that change (M6.2):

\[ revExi_{s,f} = \theta\,F_{s,f}\left(txo^{+}_{c} + vat_{c} + \Delta xcp_{s,f}\,(1 + v_{s,f})\right) \tag{7.2}\]

Existing consumer subsidies are the negative part of the existing taxes:

\[ cs_{s,f} = \theta\,F_{s,f}\,txo^{-}_{c} \tag{7.3}\]

New policies are the new carbon tax, the new excise (fuel price reform, and Pigouvian taxes where they are switched on), the ETS cost on the auctioned part, and VAT on all of them (M6.1):

\[ rev^{new}_{s,f} = \theta\,F_{s,f}\left(nct_{s,f} + nexc_{s,f} + \rho\,nets_{s,f} + v_{s,f}\,nce_{s,f}\right) \tag{7.4}\]

Subsidy reform removes consumer subsidies and price controls as separate policy lines; its revenue includes VAT:

\[ rev^{sr}_{s,f} = \theta\,F_{s,f}\,(csw_{s,f} + pcw_{s,f})\,(1 + v_{s,f}) \tag{7.5}\]

where

  • \(c = c(s,f)\) is the price fuel of the pair (its price position in the price blocks);
  • \(txo^{+}_{c}\), \(txo^{-}_{c}\) are the positive part (taxes, etx) and negative part (subsidies, esub) of excise and other taxes, USD/GJ;
  • \(vat_{c}\) is the VAT payment on the price before new policies (vat), USD/GJ;
  • \(\Delta xcp_{s,f}\) is the change in existing carbon pricing against the price data (xcpd), USD/GJ;
  • \(v_{s,f}\) is the VAT rate of the pair (Inputs), share;
  • \(nct\), \(nexc\), \(nets\) are the new carbon tax (ctxnew), new excise (ntx) and ETS cost (ets), USD/GJ, and \(nce\) their total (nce);
  • \(\rho\) is the ETS revenue factor, the auctioned share (ets.rf); feebates are revenue-neutral and raise nothing;
  • \(csw\), \(pcw\) are the consumer subsidy reform wedge and the price-control phase-out wedge of the subsector (csw, pcw; Chapter 2), USD/GJ.

Two splits of the same money are kept per pair for the report by instrument (Section 7.2.5): the new excise and the VAT.

\[ rev^{exc}_{s,f} = \theta\,F_{s,f}\,nexc_{s,f}, \qquad rev^{vat}_{s,f} = \theta\,F_{s,f}\left(vat_{c} + (\Delta xcp_{s,f} + nce_{s,f} + csw_{s,f} + pcw_{s,f})\,v_{s,f}\right) \tag{7.6}\]

A memo row gives the level of existing carbon pricing, \(rev^{xcp}_{s,f} = \theta\,F_{s,f}\,xcp_{s,f}\) (rxcp). Only its change against the price data is revenue (inside Equation 7.2); the part embedded in the price data is already in the excise.

7.2.2 Power-sector revenues

The power sector raises revenue in two places: on the fuels burnt in power plants, and on the electricity sold to users. Both are computed in the power block (Chapter 4) and summed here (A6.1).

By generation type \(k\) (coal, gas, oil, nuclear, wind, solar, hydro, other renewables, biomass):

\[ \begin{aligned} rev^{tx}_{k} &= \delta_k\,\theta\,PE_k\,(txo^{+} + vat)_{c(k)} && \text{existing fuel taxes }(\texttt{prtx})\\ rev^{sub}_{k} &= \delta_k\,\theta\,PE_k\,txo^{-}_{c(k)} && \text{existing fuel subsidies }(\texttt{prsub})\\ rev^{xc}_{k} &= xc_k\,H\,G_k && \text{change in existing carbon pricing }(\texttt{prxc})\\ rev^{new}_{k} &= pc_k\,H\,G_k - CO2_k\,A_{pow}\,(1 - \rho) && \text{new policies }(\texttt{prnew})\\ rev^{rns}_{k} &= rns_k\,H\,G_k && \text{renewable subsidies }(\texttt{prrns}\text{, negative}) \end{aligned} \tag{7.7}\]

where

  • \(\delta_k\) is 1 for a type that burns a fuel (coal, gas, oil, biomass), else 0;
  • \(PE_k\) is the fuel use of type \(k\) (pe), ktoe, and \(c(k)\) its price fuel;
  • \(G_k\) is generation (gen_mwy), MW-years, and \(H\) converts MW-years to million kWh (MWyMkWh, 8.76);
  • \(xc_k\), \(pc_k\) are the existing carbon cost and the new policy cost per kWh of the type (xck, xac), USD/kWh;
  • \(CO2_k\) is the CO2 of the type, MtCO2, and \(A_{pow}\) the ETS cost rate of power (etr), USD/tCO2: the free part of the ETS, \(1-\rho\), is not revenue, so the ETS counts at the permit price on the auctioned part only;
  • \(rns_k\) is the renewable subsidy per kWh (rns), USD/kWh.

A memo row gives the level of existing carbon pricing of each type, prxl = \(CO2_k\) times the existing carbon tax and ETS prices of the price data year.

By electricity user group \(u\) (residential, industry):

\[ \begin{aligned} rev^{vat}_{u} &= pp_u\,\frac{v_u}{1+v_u}\,cons_u && \text{VAT on electricity }(\texttt{rvat})\\ rev^{nvat}_{u} &= \left(\overline{pc}\,\pi\,(1 - rb) + x_u\right) v_u\,cons_u && \text{VAT on the policy cost passed through }(\texttt{rnvat})\\ rev^{reb}_{u} &= -\,rb\,\overline{pc}\,\pi\,cons_u && \text{rebate of the carbon cost }(\texttt{rreb}\text{, negative})\\ rev^{x}_{u} &= x_u\,cons_u && \text{electricity excise }(\texttt{rpex})\\ rev^{gap}_{u} &= gap_u\,cons_u && \text{electricity subsidy gap }(\texttt{rgap}\text{, negative}) \end{aligned} \tag{7.8}\]

where \(pp_u\) is the electricity price before policies (rppre), \(\overline{pc}\) the average policy cost of generation (ccpav), \(\pi\) the pass-through of the power-sector price change (PowerSectorPriceChangePassOn), \(rb\) the rebate switch (rbs), \(x_u\) the electricity excise (pex), \(gap_u\) the subsidy gap (sgap), all per kWh, \(v_u\) the VAT rate and \(cons_u\) consumption in million kWh. The excise splits into the excise of the scenario “Electricity excise” (rexs = exs times total consumption) and the manual excise (rexm, the rest).

The power sector’s lines of the three revenue totals are then:

\[ \begin{aligned} rev^{tx}_{pow} &= \textstyle\sum_k (rev^{tx}_{k} + rev^{xc}_{k}) + \sum_u rev^{vat}_{u}\\ rev^{sub}_{pow} &= \textstyle\sum_k (rev^{sub}_{k} + rev^{rns}_{k}) + \sum_u rev^{gap}_{u}\\ rev^{new}_{pow} &= \textstyle\sum_k rev^{new}_{k} + \sum_u (rev^{nvat}_{u} + rev^{reb}_{u} + rev^{x}_{u}) \end{aligned} \tag{7.9}\]

Subsidy reform raises nothing in power (rsr.pow = 0).

7.2.3 Producer subsidies and the methane fee

Producer subsidies are a fiscal cost outside the fuel and subsector pairs. For each fuel group \(f\) (coal, gas, oil, biomass, electricity), the subsidy before any phase-out is scaled by the phase-out factor:

\[ rev^{psu} = -\sum_{f} pros_f\,psf_f, \qquad psf_f = 1 - \psi_f\,(1 - pof) \tag{7.10}\]

where

  • \(pros_f\) is the producer subsidy before the phase-out (pros), USD million: price data (USD bn nominal) times the CPI factor up to the last price year, then held;
  • \(\psi_f\) is the inclusion switch of the fuel and \(pof\) the phase-out factor of producer subsidies (Chapter 2);
  • \(psf_f\) is the share that remains (psf).

The phase-out also lowers the supply cost and so the prices (Chapter 2); this row is its fiscal line (rpsu).

The methane fee raises \(rev^{mf}\) (rmf = ch4.rev): methane emissions of the sources the fee covers, times their global warming potential, times the fee. It is computed with the methane block (Chapter 6). It is not a fuel revenue, so it appears in the totals and in the report by instrument, not in the report by fuel.

7.2.4 Totals

The totals add the pairs over subsectors and fuels and add the power line. Net revenue from existing taxes and subsidies, and total revenue (M6.3):

\[ rev^{net} = \sum_{s,f} (revExi_{s,f} + cs_{s,f}) + rev^{tx}_{pow} + rev^{sub}_{pow} \tag{7.11}\]

\[ rev = rev^{net} + rev^{new} + rev^{sr} + rev^{psu} + rev^{mf} \tag{7.12}\]

\[ \Delta rev = rev - rev^{B} \tag{7.13}\]

where \(rev^{new} = \sum_{s,f} rev^{new}_{s,f} + rev^{new}_{pow}\) and \(rev^{sr} = \sum_{s,f} rev^{sr}_{s,f}\). The lines rtx, rsub, rnew, rxcp and rsr are also summed by sector group (transport, buildings, industry, other energy use) and given for power.

7.2.5 Fiscal reports

The fiscal reports (block 12d) rearrange the same revenue three ways: by fuel, by policy instrument, and against GDP. Each report has a check row that must be 0, because it splits the totals exactly.

Revenue by fuel, net of subsidies. For each final-use fuel, the existing taxes, subsidies, new policies and subsidy reform over all subsectors. Electricity collects its VAT, subsidy gap, VAT on the policy cost, rebates, excise and renewable subsidies. Fuels burnt in power plants have their own rows:

\[ rfu_{f} = \sum_{s} \left(revExi_{s,f} + cs_{s,f} + rev^{new}_{s,f} + rev^{sr}_{s,f}\right), \qquad rfu_{ecy} = \sum_u \left(rev^{vat}_u + rev^{nvat}_u + rev^{reb}_u + rev^{x}_u + rev^{gap}_u\right) + \sum_k rev^{rns}_k \tag{7.14}\]

\[ rfg_{k} = rev^{tx}_{k} + rev^{xc}_{k} + rev^{sub}_{k} + rev^{new}_{k}, \qquad k \in \{\text{coal, gas, oil, biomass}\} \tag{7.15}\]

The check rfu.chk = \(\left|\sum_f rfu_f + rfu_{ecy} + \sum_k rfg_k - rev^{net} - rev^{new} - rev^{sr}\right|\) is 0. The methane fee and producer subsidies are not fuel revenues and are not in these rows.

7.3 GDP effects

The policies change what the government collects. The model assumes that the extra revenue is spent, in shares the user chooses, on tax cuts, investment, current spending or transfers. Fiscal multipliers translate each use into a change in GDP over the following years. The higher energy taxes themselves weigh on GDP too, at the excise multiplier. The resulting change in the GDP level then feeds back into GDP growth, which drives fuel use, power demand and the non-energy GHG drivers.

The methodology describes the multipliers in chapter 4 and the feedback in section 4.7, GDP feedback in CPAT. The model follows legacy CPAT section 17, with legacy bug L24 fixed.

7.3.2 Fiscal multipliers

A multiplier says by how many percent GDP responds, in the year of a fiscal change and in each later year, to a change of a fiscal instrument by 1% of GDP. The model holds one multiplier per instrument \(i\) and lag \(k = 0, \dots, 19\) (A8.1):

\[ m_{i}(k) = \bar{m}_{G,i}\big(\min(k, k^{last})\big) + \sigma\,s_{G,i}\big(\min(k, k^{last})\big) \tag{7.18}\]

where

  • \(G\) is the multiplier group (fis.grp): global, the country’s income group (HIC, UMIC, LMIC, LIC), the country’s estimated table, or the manual table;
  • \(\bar{m}_{G,i}(k)\) is the multiplier of the group at lag \(k\): for global and income groups, the median of the World Bank’s MFMod (methodology 4.3, 4.4);
  • \(s_{G,i}(k)\) is the standard deviation of the income group, and \(\sigma\) is 0 for Base, −1 for Low, +1 for High;
  • \(k^{last}\) is the last lag with data (11): lags 12-19 hold the lag-11 value, as in legacy.

7.3.3 Fiscal space

The fiscal space is the revenue the scenario gains over the baseline, as a share of baseline GDP. It is total revenue: existing taxes net of subsidies (power included), new policies, subsidy reform, producer subsidies and the methane fee (M8.1):

\[ rev^{fis} = rev^{net} + rev^{new} + rev^{sr} + rev^{psu} + rev^{mf} \tag{7.19}\]

\[ fs_t = \frac{rev^{fis}_t - rev^{fis,B}_t}{Y^{0}_t\,M} \tag{7.20}\]

where \(rev^{fis}\) is in USD million (fis.rev; equal to \(rev\) of Equation 7.12), \(rev^{fis,B}\) is that of scenario 1 in the same year (fis.ref), and \(fs\) is the fiscal space (fis.sp), share of GDP.

Lower fuel use also lowers the cost of existing subsidies (base erosion, Equation 7.16), and that counts. The methane fee counts on the scenario’s own methane activity. In the baseline, \(fs = 0\), so scenario 1 has no GDP effect.

7.3.4 Fiscal impulse

The impulse of a year is the new revenue gain of that year: the change in the gain in USD, over this year’s GDP. It is 0 before the start year of the GDP calculations:

\[ imp_t = \begin{cases} 0 & t < t^{GDP}\\[2pt] fs_{t} & t = t_0 \ge t^{GDP}\\[2pt] fs_t - fs_{t-1}\,\dfrac{Y^{0}_{t-1}}{Y^{0}_t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{7.21}\]

where \(t^{GDP}\) is GDPStartYear, \(t_0\) the base year and \(imp_t\) the impulse (fis.imp), share of GDP.

The revenue uses take the impulse only from the year the carbon price starts, as legacy does (it sets the revenue-use shares to 0 before then):

\[ imp^{p}_t = \begin{cases} 0 & t < T^{ct}_0\\ imp_t & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \tag{7.22}\]

where \(T^{ct}_0\) is the carbon pricing start year (CPIntro) and \(imp^{p}\) is fis.impp. Earlier impulses still act through the excise drag.

Each impulse starts a cohort that acts for 20 years. The rows fis.lag.l1 … fis.lag.l19 (and fis.lagp.l1 … fis.lagp.l19 for \(imp^{p}\)) hold the impulses of the 19 earlier years: each is the previous year’s value of the row above it, and 0 in the base year.

7.3.5 GDP effect of each use

The effect of each use is its share times the sum, over the 20 lags, of the impulse of that cohort times the multiplier at that lag. The impulse of year \(t\) counts at lag 0 in year \(t\) (M8.2):

\[ eff_{exc,t} = -\sum_{k=0}^{19} imp_{t-k}\; m_{exc}(k) \tag{7.23}\]

\[ eff_{i,t} = \frac{sh_i}{100}\sum_{k=0}^{19} imp^{p}_{t-k}\; m_{i}(k), \qquad i \in \{pit, cit, capt, gnfs, trns\} \tag{7.24}\]

\[ \varepsilon_t = \mathbb{1}[\text{GDPEndogenous} = \text{Yes}]\;\Big(eff_{exc,t} + \sum_{i} eff_{i,t}\Big) \tag{7.25}\]

where

  • \(sh_i\) is the revenue share of use \(i\), percent;
  • \(eff_{i,t}\) is the GDP effect of use \(i\) (fis.eff.<i>), share of GDP;
  • \(\varepsilon_t\) is the GDP effect (gdp.eff): the change of the GDP level against the baseline, as a share of GDP; 0 if endogenous GDP effects are off.

The revenue itself counts as an energy tax increase: share −1 at the excise multipliers.

7.3.6 GDP level and growth: the feedback

The GDP effect of year \(t\) raises the level of real GDP from year \(t+1\) (M8.3):

\[ lvl_t = \begin{cases} 1 & t = t_0 \\ 1 + \varepsilon_{t-1} & t > t_0 \end{cases} \tag{7.26}\]

\[ Y_t = Y^{0}_t\; lvl_t \tag{7.27}\]

\[ 1 + g_t = (1 + g^{0}_t)\,\frac{lvl_t}{lvl_{t-1}}, \qquad g_{t_0} = g^{0}_{t_0} \tag{7.28}\]

where

  • \(lvl_t\) is the GDP level factor used this year (gdp.lvl);
  • \(Y_t\) is real GDP after the feedback (gdp.pos.usd), USD bn;
  • \(g^{0}_t\) is baseline real GDP growth (gdp.base) and \(g_t\) real GDP growth after the feedback (gdp.pos.pct).

A steady effect therefore raises the level once and leaves growth unchanged afterwards; it does not compound.

Growth after the feedback is what drives demand in the year: it is computed at the very start of year \(t\), before the power and fuel-use blocks. The fuel-use block reads it raised to the income factor of the elasticities (Python GDPI = \((1+g_t)^{\,inc}-1\), with \(inc\) = inc.ela.adj), together with the demand shock factor SHOCK; see Chapter 3. The road engineer model reads GDP after the feedback too (Chapter 5), and the non-energy GHG drivers read GDP growth (Chapter 6).

7.3.7 The timing rule

Revenue of year \(t\) changes GDP only from year \(t+1\). GDP growth after the feedback reads the level of the current year, which holds the effect of the previous year. The rule has three consequences, kept as rules of the model:

  1. The fiscal space of year \(t\) reads revenue of year \(t\) only: no later year, no lookup over a whole row.
  2. A same-year GDP effect never reaches same-year demand. The impulse of year \(t\) counts at lag 0 in \(\varepsilon_t\), which acts on GDP from \(t+1\).
  3. No other block reads a later year. With this lag, no revenue line can close a circular reference, whatever it reads (fuel use, power, methane).

This lag is what makes the whole model computable year by year: GDP growth depends only on earlier years.

7.4 External balance

Lower fossil fuel use means lower imports, or more exports, of oil, gas and coal. Block 16b values the change in fuel use at the supply price and scales it by the share of each fuel group that is traded, then adds it to the energy trade balance and to the current account of the baseline. All rows are in USD bn, real (A8.2).

7.4.1 Trade data

For each trade group \(g\) (oil products, gas, coal) the base-year energy balance gives imports \(M_g\), production \(P_g\) and exports \(X_g\) (exports are negative in the balance). From them:

\[ ts_g = \frac{M_g - X_g}{M_g + P_g - X_g} \quad (1 \text{ where the denominator is } 0) \tag{7.30}\]

\[ TB_{g,t} = -(M_g + X_g)\,\theta\,\frac{p^{int}_{g,t}}{u_g}\,\frac{1}{M} \tag{7.31}\]

where

  • \(ts_g\) is the share traded of the group (ts_<group>); Egypt: gas 0.19, oil products 0.47, coal 1.0;
  • \(TB_{g,t}\) is the baseline energy trade balance, exports minus imports (xtb.<group>);
  • \(p^{int}_{g,t}\) is the real international price of scenario 1’s price source (oil per barrel, gas per MMBtu on the country’s gas market, coal per tonne), and \(u_g\) the GJ per unit (6.12 GJ per barrel, 1.055056 GJ per MMBtu, 25.14 GJ per tonne).

The oil group holds gasoline, diesel, LPG, kerosene, jet fuel and other oil products.

7.4.2 Change in net imports

For each fossil fuel, the change in use against the baseline is valued at the supply price and scaled by the share traded of its group:

\[ \Delta NIM_{f,t} = \Delta F_{f,t}\;\theta\;sp_{f,t}\;ts_{g(f)}\;\frac{1}{M} \tag{7.32}\]

where

  • \(\Delta F_{f,t}\) is the change in total use of the fuel against scenario 1 (echg, block 11b, Chapter 3), ktoe;
  • \(sp_{f,t}\) is the supply price (sp, Chapter 2), USD/GJ;
  • \(\Delta NIM_{f,t}\) is the change in net energy imports (xnim.<fuel>), positive for more imports.

7.4.3 Trade balance and current account

The scenario’s energy trade balance is the baseline balance less the change in net imports:

\[ TB^{P}_t = \sum_g TB_{g,t} - \sum_f \Delta NIM_{f,t} \tag{7.33}\]

Both, and the change in net imports, are also given as shares of baseline real GDP (xtb.pct = \(TB^P/Y^0\), xnim.pct = \(\Delta NIM/Y^0\); 0 where GDP is 0).

The current account starts from the WEO series and adds the change in net imports, in USD and in percent of GDP:

\[ \begin{aligned} CA^{r}_t &= CA_t\,\nu_t && \text{real current account, pre-policy }(\texttt{bca.r})\\ \Delta CA_{g,t} &= -\Delta NIM_{g,t} && \text{change by fuel group }(\texttt{bca.d})\\ CA^{P}_t &= CA^{r}_t + \textstyle\sum_g \Delta CA_{g,t} && \text{after the policy }(\texttt{bca.p})\\ \hat{Y}_t &= \left|CA^{r}_t / ca_t\right| && \text{implied GDP / 100 }(\texttt{bca.y}\text{; 0 if }ca_t = 0)\\ ca^{P}_t &= ca_t + \textstyle\sum_g \Delta CA_{g,t} / \hat{Y}_t && \text{after the policy, percent of GDP }(\texttt{bca.pp}) \end{aligned} \tag{7.34}\]

where

  • \(CA_t\) is the current account balance (WEO BCA, bca), USD bn nominal, held after its last data year;
  • \(ca_t\) is the current account in percent of GDP (WEO BCA_NGDPD, bca.gdp), held likewise;
  • \(\nu_t\) is the US CPI index (infl, 1 in the results year);
  • bca.dp.<group> = \(\Delta CA_{g,t}/\hat{Y}_t\); the memo bca.n is the current account in current prices, 0 after the last data year.

7.5 Carbon pricing indicators

How heavily is carbon priced, once every tax and subsidy on energy is counted? Section 17 answers with revenue per tonne of CO2, by fuel and for the economy, and with the share of emissions the new carbon tax and ETS cover (A7.5). The methodology discusses coverage in section 3.9.3.1.1.

7.5.1 Implicit carbon price and effective carbon rate

Implicit carbon price by fuel. The revenue net of subsidies of the fuel, final use and power generation, divided by its CO2:

\[ tcp_f = \frac{rfu_f + rfg_f}{CO2_f}, \qquad tcp_{all} = \frac{\sum_{f \in \text{fossil}} rfu_f + \sum_{k \in \{coal,gas,oil\}} rfg_k}{CO2} \tag{7.35}\]

where \(CO2_f\) is the CO2 of the fuel (power included; Chapter 6), MtCO2, \(rfg_f\) applies to coal, gas and other oil products only, and \(tcp\) is in USD/tCO2 (tcp; 0 where the CO2 is 0). Biomass is left out: it has no CO2.

This is the tax-and-subsidy wedge per GJ divided by the emission factor. A carbon tax of \(\tau\) USD/t raises every fuel’s implicit price by about \(\tau\).

Effective carbon rate. All revenue net of subsidies, per tonne of energy CO2:

\[ ecr = \frac{rev^{net} + rev^{new} + rev^{sr}}{CO2} \tag{7.36}\]

so VAT, the electricity subsidy gap and electricity VAT count. ecr is the summary row of section 17.

7.5.2 Coverage

CO2 covered by the new carbon tax in each of the 17 coverage sectors \(e\) (power and the 16 subsectors): the CO2 of the fuels whose coverage switch is on, counted only where the sector’s carbon tax rate is above 0:

\[ CO2^{cov}_e = \mathbb{1}[ct_e > 0]\sum_{f} CO2_{e,f}\;\kappa^{ct}_f \tag{7.37}\]

where \(\kappa^{ct}_f\) is the fuel’s carbon tax coverage switch (ctcov). Power uses the CO2 of coal, gas and oil generation. A sector in a non-complementary ETS has a carbon tax rate of 0 and so no covered CO2.

Coverage shares and effective carbon tax:

\[ cov^{ct}_G = \frac{\sum_{e \in G} CO2^{cov}_e}{CO2}, \qquad cov^{ets} = \frac{CO2^{ets}}{CO2}, \qquad cov = cov^{ct}_{all} + cov^{ets}, \qquad p^{eff} = \tau\;cov^{ct}_{all} \tag{7.38}\]

where \(G\) is a sector group (transport, buildings, industry, other energy use, power) or all sectors, \(CO2^{ets}\) the CO2 covered by the new ETS (co2.ets, Chapter 6), \(\tau\) the carbon price (cptraj) and \(p^{eff}\) the effective carbon tax (cpeff), USD/tCO2.

7.6 Welfare and externalities

Section 18 puts a money value on what the policy does to society. It values climate damages at the social cost of carbon, air pollution and road transport externalities with the inputs of the two co-benefit modules, and efficiency (deadweight) costs with the tax wedges. From the same building blocks it computes fossil fuel subsidies, explicit and implicit, and efficient prices. The methodology describes the framework in section 3.7, Monetized social costs and benefits.

Results are in USD bn, real, unless stated.

7.6.1 Social cost of carbon

The social cost of carbon (SCC) values a tonne of CO2. The model offers five paths and picks one for the national value and one for the global value (M1.6):

\[ \begin{aligned} SCC^{tgt}_t &= SCC^{*}\,(1 + g^{scc})^{\,t - T^{*}}\\ SCC^{Rn}_t &= SCC^{R}_{c}\,(1 + g^{scc})^{\,t - t_0}, \qquad SCC^{Rg}_t = SCC^{R}_{world}\,(1 + g^{scc})^{\,t - t_0}\\ SCC^{EPA}_t &= SCC^{EPA}\,(1 + g^{scc})^{\,t - t_0}\\ SCC^{man}_t &= \text{manual path} \end{aligned} \tag{7.39}\]

where \(SCC^{R}_c\) is the national value of Ricke et al. (2018) for the chosen pure rate of time preference and elasticity of marginal utility (sheet SCC, key <country>.<rate>.<elasticity>), \(SCC^{R}_{world}\) the global value (key wld.<rate>.<elasticity>), and \(t_0\) the base year. The national SCC (\(SCC^{nat}\), scc.nat) and the global SCC (\(SCC^{glo}\), scc.glo) take the path chosen by NSCCSrc and GSCCSrc (first letter: T, R, M, U or E).

7.6.2 Climate damages

Climate damages are the SCC times GHG emissions excluding LULUCF (Chapter 6); the welfare gain is the damage avoided against the baseline:

\[ D^{x} = \frac{SCC^{x}\;GHG}{M}, \qquad W^{cli,x} = D^{x,B} - D^{x}, \qquad x \in \{nat, glo\} \tag{7.41}\]

where \(GHG\) is in Mt CO2e, \(D\) the climate damage (dmg.nat, dmg.glo) and \(W^{cli}\) the damage avoided (wcli.nat, wcli.glo), USD bn.

7.6.3 Efficiency costs

A tax or subsidy drives a wedge between what users pay and what supply costs. When a policy changes fuel use, the welfare cost is the wedge times the change in use: a Harberger triangle plus, with a prior tax, a rectangle (methodology 3.7.1.3). The model takes the average of the wedges of the scenario and the baseline (a trapezoid).

The wedge is revenue net of subsidies per unit of use:

\[ w_f = \frac{rfu_f + rfg_f}{F_f}, \qquad w_{ecy} = \frac{rfu_{ecy}}{F_{ecy}} \tag{7.42}\]

where \(F_f\) is total use of the fuel (ener, section 11), \(F_{ecy}\) electricity demand (eldem), both ktoe, \(rfg\) applies to coal, gas, other oil products and biomass, and \(w\) is in USD million per ktoe (wdg; 0 where use is 0).

The efficiency cost of each fuel (biomass and the power fuels included) and of electricity:

\[ EC_f = -\,\frac{w_f + w^{B}_f}{2}\;\frac{\Delta F_f}{M}, \qquad EC = \sum_f EC_f + EC_{ecy} \tag{7.43}\]

where \(\Delta F_f\) is the change in use against the baseline (echg; electricity: eldem − eldem\(^B\)) and \(EC\) is the efficiency cost (eff), USD bn: positive is a cost. With subsidies (negative wedges), a fall in use is a gain.

Efficiency cost of the methane fee. For each methane source \(j\) (oil and gas upstream, downstream gas, coal mines, biomass, livestock, rice, waste), the abatement curve gives the abated share \(a_j\,p^{b_j}\) at a fee \(p\) per tonne of CH4. The fee used on the curve is capped where abatement reaches 1:

\[ p^{mf}_j = \begin{cases} 0 & a_j = 0\\ \min\!\left(m\,GWP_j\,\delta^{mf}_j,\ a_j^{-1/b_j}\right) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \tag{7.44}\]

\[ MC_j = E^{0}_j\,\frac{Act_j}{Act^{0}_j}\;abt_j\;p^{mf}_j\;\frac{b_j}{1+b_j}\;\frac{1}{10^{6}}, \qquad MC = \sum_j MC_j \tag{7.45}\]

where

  • \(m\) is the methane fee (mfee), USD/tCO2e, \(GWP_j\) the global warming potential and \(\delta^{mf}_j\) 1 if the fee applies to the source;
  • \(a_j\), \(b_j\) are the parameters of the abatement curve (country, else average); where \(b_j = 0\) or the cap overflows, there is no cap;
  • \(E^{0}_j\) is the source’s emissions in the inventory year (kt CH4), \(Act_j / Act^{0}_j\) its activity against the base year, and \(abt_j\) its abated share (Chapter 6);
  • \(p^{mf}_j\) is mfp (USD/tCH4) and \(MC\) the methane fee cost (mfc), USD bn: the area under the abatement curve.

7.6.4 Air pollution costs

The air pollution module provides two things (Chapter 6 gives the pollutant emissions it reads): the marginal damage of each fuel in each subsector, and the value of a change in modelled PM2.5 concentration (A8.3). The methodology describes the economic valuation in chapter 6, section 6.7.

Cost per unit of fuel, by price code. The marginal damage per GJ of each subsector (airpol.mc, computed before the year loop with the Pigouvian taxes, A1.2) is averaged over the subsectors of the price code, weighted by the scenario’s own fuel use of the year:

\[ ap_{c} = u_c\;\frac{\sum_{s \in c} mc^{ap}_{s,f}\,F_{s,f}}{\sum_{s \in c} F_{s,f}} \quad \Big(\text{the plain average where } \textstyle\sum_{s \in c} F_{s,f} = 0\Big) \tag{7.46}\]

where \(u_c\) is the GJ per price unit (litres for gasoline, diesel, LPG and kerosene; 1 for coal, gas and other oil products, which are in USD/GJ) and \(ap_c\) is airpol.cost. The price codes are coal and gas for industry, residential (buildings subsectors) and power (the plant’s own value), the four road and household oil products over all oil-using subsectors, and other oil products for power and industry. Per GJ, \(ap^{gj}_c = ap_c / u_c\) (airpol.cost.gj). Residential coal counts only where buildings’ coal use exceeds ApCoalResMin (0.1%) of buildings’ total fuel use.

Averages for other uses (block 18), weighted by the use of the year (apq): coal and gas “other” average industry, residential (buildings use) and power (power fuel use); “other oil and diesel” (odo) averages diesel in transport and other oil products in power and industry (0 where all three are 0); oop.avg averages other oil products in power and industry.

Air pollution cost of electricity (memo), USD/kWh:

\[ ap_{ecy} = \frac{\sum_{k \in \{coal, gas, oil\}} ap^{gj}_{pow,k}\;PE_k\;\theta}{G^{tot}\,H} \tag{7.47}\]

Modelled PM2.5 concentration by module source \(m\) (coal, gas and other power plants, road, ground level, other, food), from the pollutant emissions of section 15:

\[ PM_m = 1000 \sum_p AP_{m,p}\;\kappa_{m,p}, \qquad PM = \sum_m PM_m \tag{7.48}\]

where \(AP_{m,p}\) is the emission of pollutant \(p\) by source \(m\) (aps), kt, \(\kappa_{m,p}\) the module’s concentration coefficient (µg/m³ per t; NOx, SO2, BC, POM, other PM, VOC, CH4, CO, PM2.5, OC) and \(PM\) the modelled ambient PM2.5 (air.con.pm2.mod), µg/m³.

7.6.5 Road transport externalities

The transport co-benefits module gives the cost of accidents, congestion and road damage per vehicle-km. The model turns them into a cost per litre (or kWh) of road fuel, using the road engineer model (Chapter 5) for fuel economy and the split between passenger and freight use (A8.4). The methodology describes these costs in chapter 7, section 7.7.5.

A change in fuel use comes partly from driving less and partly from more efficient vehicles. Only the driving part changes the externalities. Its share, for road fuel \(f\) (gasoline, diesel, LPG, gas, electricity), is:

\[ w_f = 1 - \frac{Q^{fre,B}_f}{Q^{cls,B}_f} \;(w_{gso} = 1), \qquad \mu_f = \frac{\epsilon^{U}_{die} + w_f\,(\epsilon^{U}_{gso} - \epsilon^{U}_{die})} {\epsilon^{U}_{die} + \epsilon^{F}_{die} + w_f\,(\epsilon^{U}_{gso} + \epsilon^{F}_{gso} - \epsilon^{U}_{die} - \epsilon^{F}_{die})} \tag{7.49}\]

where

  • \(Q^{cls}_f\) is the road fuel use of the vehicle classes of the fuel (tran.cls: passenger and freight; gasoline: passenger and two-three wheelers; electricity: all three), bn litres or kWh, and \(Q^{fre}_f\) its freight part;
  • \(w_f\) is the passenger weight of the fuel in the baseline (tran.wcl);
  • \(\epsilon^{U}\), \(\epsilon^{F}\) are the road usage and efficiency elasticities of gasoline and diesel;
  • \(\mu_f\) is the driving share of the response (tran.mrg), 0 where the baseline class use is 0.

The cost per unit of fuel, for each kind of externality:

\[ \begin{aligned} acc_f &= a^{km}\;\overline{fe}_f\;\mu_f\;\rho_f && \text{accidents }(\texttt{tran.acc.cost})\\ con_f &= k^{km}\;\overline{fe}_f\;\mu_f\;\rho_f && \text{congestion }(\texttt{tran.con.cost})\\ rdm_f &= r^{km}\;\overline{fe}_f\;\mu_f\;\phi_f\;\rho_f && \text{road damage, not gasoline }(\texttt{tran.rdm.cost}) \end{aligned} \tag{7.50}\]

where

  • \(a^{km}\), \(k^{km}\), \(r^{km}\) are the accident, congestion and road-damage costs per vehicle-km of the module (tran.acc.km, tran.con.km, tran.rdm.km), USD/km; road damage is per freight vehicle-km;
  • \(\overline{fe}_f\) is the average fuel economy of the stock (trafg, trafo, trafe), km per litre or kWh;
  • \(\phi_f = Q^{fre}_f / Q^{cls}_f\) is the freight share of the scenario (tran.fsh);
  • \(\rho_f\) is the road share of the fuel’s total use (tran.rsh; electricity: road demand over total demand);
  • the costs are in USD per litre (USD per kWh for electricity).

Per GJ, for gasoline, diesel and LPG, divide by the GJ per litre (tran.acc.gj, tran.con.gj, tran.rdm.gj). The total road externality excluding climate, USD/GJ (tot.ext):

\[ ext_{gso} = ap^{gj}_{gso} + acc^{gj}_{gso} + con^{gj}_{gso}, \qquad ext_{die} = ap^{gj}_{die} + acc^{gj}_{die} + con^{gj}_{die} + rdm^{gj}_{die} \tag{7.51}\]

7.6.6 Welfare gains

The national welfare gain adds the climate damages avoided at the national SCC, the air pollution and road transport co-benefits, and subtracts the efficiency costs (A8.5, methodology 3.7.3).

Air pollution. The value of a change in modelled PM2.5:

\[ W^{air} = v^{PM}\,\left(PM^{B} - PM\right) \tag{7.52}\]

where \(v^{PM}\) is the value of averted modelled ambient PM2.5 (air.vpm), USD bn per µg/m³, from the air pollution module.

Road transport. The per-km costs times the change in vehicle-km of the road engineer model:

\[ W^{acc} = a^{km}\,(VKT^{B} - VKT), \qquad W^{con} = k^{km}\,(VKT^{B} - VKT), \qquad W^{rdm} = r^{km}\,(VKT^{fre,B} - VKT^{fre}) \tag{7.53}\]

where \(VKT\) is total road vehicle-km (trkmt) and \(VKT^{fre}\) freight vehicle-km (trkmf), bn km, from the road engineer model whatever road model is selected (Chapter 5).

\[ W = W^{cli,nat} + W^{air} + W^{acc} + W^{con} + W^{rdm} - EC - MC \tag{7.54}\]

7.6.8 Fossil fuel subsidies

Explicit subsidies are the gap between the supply cost and the retail price, where users pay less than supply costs. Implicit subsidies are the further gap up to the efficient price: the supply cost plus external costs plus the VAT consumers would pay on both (A8.6). They are computed for 16 lines \(j\): gasoline, diesel, LPG, kerosene and other oil products; gas and coal for industry, residential, power and “other”; electricity for industry, residential and “other”.

\[ p^{*}_j = sc_j + ext_j + vat_j, \qquad vat_j = (sc_j + ext_j)\;\eta_j\;\bar{v}_t \tag{7.56}\]

\[ S^{exp}_j = \max(0,\ sc_j - p_j)\;\frac{Q_j}{M}, \qquad S^{imp}_j = \mathbb{1}[p_j \le p^{*}_j]\;\left(p^{*}_j - \max(p_j, sc_j)\right)\frac{Q_j}{M} \tag{7.57}\]

where

  • \(sc_j\) is the supply cost of the scenario (supcost: sp; electricity spe), USD/GJ or USD/kWh;
  • \(ext_j\) is the external cost (extcost): global climate cost plus tot.ext for gasoline and diesel; plus the air pollution cost for LPG, kerosene, other oil products (the oop.avg average) and coal and gas lines; 0 for electricity;
  • \(\eta_j\) is the VAT base flag: the household share of the oil product for gasoline, diesel, LPG and kerosene, 1 for residential lines, 0 for other oil products, industry and power;
  • \(\bar{v}_t\) is the general VAT rate of the year (vatgen): the WEO VAT rate up to the last historical price year, then held;
  • \(p_j\) is the retail price of the scenario (rpsub: rpn; electricity rp);
  • \(Q_j\) is consumption (con), PJ (GWh for electricity): total use for oil products (kerosene with jet fuel), sector use for coal and gas (residential = buildings; power = power fuel use; other = transport and other energy use), electricity demand of industry, buildings, and transport with other energy use;
  • the “other” lines of coal, gas and electricity take the consumption-weighted averages of \(sc\), \(ext\), \(vat\) and \(p\) of the lines above them (the plain average where consumption is 0);
  • \(S^{exp}\), \(S^{imp}\) are the explicit and implicit subsidies (expsub.con, impsub.con), USD bn; their totals are also given as shares of GDP (expsubgdp.con, impsubgdp.con).

7.6.9 Efficient prices

The efficient price of a fuel is what it would cost with every externality priced: the supply cost, the Pigouvian taxes (air pollution, climate, road transport) and VAT. The tax gap compares it with the retail price (methodology 3.7.2). It is computed per price unit (USD per litre for oil products, USD/GJ for coal and gas, USD/kWh for electricity) for 12 price codes \(c\): gasoline, diesel, LPG, kerosene; coal and gas for industry, residential and power; electricity for industry and residential.

\[ pig_c = ap_c + cd_c + acc_c + con_c + rdm_c \tag{7.58}\]

\[ p^{eff}_c = sc^{B}_c\,u_c + pig_c + \left(sc^{B}_c\,u_c + \chi\,pig_c\right)\eta_c\,\bar{v}_t, \qquad tg_c = p_c\,u_c - p^{eff}_c \tag{7.59}\]

where

  • \(ap_c\) is the air pollution cost (airpol.cost.s): airpol.cost for oil products, airpol.cost.gj for coal and gas, and for electricity the baseline value of the generation-weighted cost \(\sum_k ap^{gj}_{pow,k} G_k / G^{tot}\) converted to USD/kWh (airpol.ecy.g);
  • \(cd_c\) is the climate damage per unit at the national SCC (scc.cost = cc.code = \(SCC^{nat}\,EF_c\,u_c\)), 0 for electricity;
  • \(acc_c\), \(con_c\) (gasoline and diesel) and \(rdm_c\) (diesel) are the road externalities per litre of the scenario (Equation 7.50); 0 for other codes;
  • \(sc^{B}_c\) is the baseline supply cost per GJ (electricity per kWh), \(u_c\) the GJ per price unit;
  • \(\chi\) is 1 if ExternalityAddVAT = Yes (externalities are part of the VAT base), else 0; \(\eta_c\) is 1 for consumer codes (oil products and residential), else 0;
  • \(p_c\) is the retail price of the scenario (rpn; electricity rp), so \(p_c u_c\) is rp.eff;
  • \(pig_c\) is the Pigouvian tax (pig), \(p^{eff}_c\) the efficient price (eff.price; its VAT part is vat.cost) and \(tg_c\) the tax gap (tgap).

Per tonne of CO2. The Pigouvian tax and its air pollution part are also expressed per tonne of CO2, and the baseline excise likewise:

\[ pig^{CO2}_c = pig_c\,\frac{SCC^{glo}}{cd_c}, \qquad app_c = pig^{CO2}_c\,\frac{ap_c}{pig_c}, \qquad txo^{CO2}_c = txo^{B}_c\,u_c\,\frac{SCC^{glo}}{cd_c} \tag{7.60}\]

each 0 where its denominator is 0 (and \(txo^{CO2} = 0\) for electricity). The averages pig.co2e.avg and app.avg weight the ten fossil codes by fuel use (effp.q: total use for oil products, kerosene with jet fuel; industry, buildings and power use for coal and gas).
